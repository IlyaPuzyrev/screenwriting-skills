# Premise, Theme, Thematic Conception, and the Core of the Play (前提·主题·立意·戏核)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

This skill answers four questions: **what to write** (subject selection), **why write it** (premise / controlling idea / third rail), **where to cut in** (angle / 戏核, xìhé — the core premise without which the play would not stand), and **how to say it in one sentence** (logline / one-line story). Sources: Lajos Egri's 《编剧的艺术》 (The Art of Dramatic Writing), Robert McKee's 《故事》 (Story), Neill Hicks's 《编剧的核心技巧》 (Screenwriting 101: The Essential Craft of Feature Film Writing), Lisa Cron's 《怎样写故事》 (Story Genius), Julian Hoxter's 《编剧的十二条法则》 (The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules), Blake Snyder's 《救猫咪》 (Save the Cat!), and 陆军 (Lu Jun)'s 《编剧理论与技法》 (Playwriting Theory and Technique), chapters 1–6. How they complement one another: Egri supplies the dialectic and the tyranny of the premise; McKee supplies "controlling idea = value + cause" and the method of reasoning backward from the climax; Cron supplies the concrete steps for building the protagonist's inner conflict (desire vs. misbelief); Lu Jun supplies the tools of Chinese drama for choosing material, deepening the thematic conception, finding an angle, and testing the core; Hicks, Hoxter, and Snyder supply the commercial tests of a one-liner.

For the example library and worksheets, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. The Premise Is a Tyrant (Egri)

- There is a premise in every second. An inventor who does not know what his device is for, a runner who does not know where he is going — these people are insane; a script packed with frenzied behavior in which no one knows the motive is just as insane.
- **The three parts of a premise**: "Frugality leads to waste" — frugality = character, "leads to" = conflict, waste = ending. "A good premise is the shortest possible synopsis of the play." The premises of famous plays: 《罗密欧与朱丽叶》 (Romeo and Juliet) = great love can overcome everything, even death; 《李尔王》 (King Lear) = blind trust leads to destruction; 《麦克白》 (Macbeth) = cold ambition leads to self-destruction; 《奥赛罗》 (Othello) = jealousy destroys itself and the one it loves; 《群鬼》 (Ghosts) = the sins of the fathers are visited on the children; 《玩偶之家》 (A Doll's House) = inequality between the sexes in marriage breeds unhappiness.
- What the premise checklist lacks is **the author's belief**: "Which side are you on?" "Any person with a strong conviction is a storehouse of premises"; a borrowed premise does not belong to you; "never write about anything you do not believe." The premise is not written into the dialogue, but the whole play proves it.
- **One play can have only one premise**; two premises = walking in two directions (the first 3¾ acts of 《影子与实体》 (Shadow and Substance) say "wisdom defeats superstition," and then the ending suddenly changes). A sub-premise supports the main premise.
- A premise need not be a universal truth; within your work, it is the truth. Rodin cut the hands off the Balzac statue: "No part can be more important than the whole."
- You can start from a character, an accident, or an idea, but before you write you must find the premise; if you change your mind, change the premise and start over. The derivation from idea to premise: write about love → great love → "love conquers all" is too vague → "Can love transcend death? Yes" → from this derive the traits the characters must have (the girl cannot be stupid and cold, the boy cannot be shallow) and the ladder of struggle.
- **The premise drives the only choice** (the workroom scene in 《玩偶之家》 (A Doll's House)): we need a married couple → Helmer cares about respectability → Nora's action must threaten his social standing and must arise from Helmer's own reasons → it has to involve money → theft will not do (it would change the premise into a condemnation of theft) → emergency funds → Helmer would rather die than borrow → only her father can guarantee the loan → **"Nora's father must die"** → forged signature. "The premise is a tyrant: it permits you to walk only one road."
- The proof of the premise is the "obligatory scene": only when the premise is being proved can you arouse the highest expectation; the obligatory scenes of many plays are dull because the premise is vague.
- Dialectic creates character: thesis (external demand) / antithesis (the character's desire) / synthesis (the ending); the Irene case — you must prove "why it has to be she who becomes a prostitute," ruling out every other possibility.
- The ending must be caused by the characters' actions; it cannot be an accident or a natural death (the Aunt Clara case).

## II. Controlling Idea = Value + Cause (McKee)

- **Premise** is a "what if… what would happen?" question (《大白鲨》 (Jaws), 《克莱默》 (Kramer vs. Kramer)); it need not be kept in the finished work. **Controlling idea** is one sentence: "Justice is served because the protagonist is more violent than the criminals" (《肮脏的哈里》 (Dirty Harry)) / "smarter than the criminals" (《可伦坡》 (Columbo)); "Tyranny flourishes because it is backed by a corrupt CIA" (《失踪》 (Missing)); "Learn to love unconditionally" (《土拨鼠日》 (Groundhog Day)). It is a creative commandment: Sherlock-style deduction from a ski cap is wrong for Harry; a .44 Magnum is wrong for Columbo.
- Storytelling is a creative argument about truth, the transformation of thought into action, and it **never uses explanatory discourse**; "crime does not pay" has to be acted out.
- **Write the climax first, then look back**: what value does the climax bring? What is the main cause? "The story tells you its meaning"; "If the plot unfolds exactly as you first planned it, your approach to writing is too cautious."
- **Idea vs. counter-idea**: sequences alternate the positive and negative dimensions, giving both sides equal firepower; if the antagonist is a fool, victory is worthless; preaching = using art as a scalpel; Kubrick's three anti-war films all admit that human beings are warlike. The classics do not give answers; they give "a medicine that keeps us clear-headed."
- Three kinds of controlling idea: **idealism** rises; **pessimism** falls (《唐人街》 (Chinatown): "Evil flourishes because it is part of human nature"); **irony** — positive irony = the redemption plot, "the overpursuit of success, wealth, and fame will destroy you, but if you see it in time and let go of your obsession you can be redeemed" (《克莱默》 (Kramer vs. Kramer), 《走出非洲》 (Out of Africa), and 《达拉斯猛龙》 (North Dallas Forty), where the hero opens his arms and lets the ball drop); negative irony = the punishment plot, "obsessively and ruthlessly pursue the satisfaction of desire and then destroy yourself" (《华尔街》 (Wall Street), 《尼克松传》 (Nixon)). Irony is the hardest to write: a single action is positive and negative at once; it must be clear rather than ambiguous, and it must be motivated; the positive and negative must not cancel each other out.
- The more you build around one clear idea, the more meanings the audience discovers; the more you stuff in, the more the story collapses. Persistence: great writers have a single theme that ignites their passion (death for Hemingway, the search for a father for Dickens, the evil of human nature for Molière).
- Ask yourself, "Do I believe the meaning of this story?" The artist's only responsibility = to tell the truth.
- **For the thematic sentence, first find a folk saying, then rewrite it into the character's own words** (魏明伦 (Wei Minglun), p0076 / p0074): for 《巴山秀才》 (The Bashan Scholar), a folk song collected on location — "In the old society, famine was heartless and the officials more so; in the new society, floods are heartless but the Party has a heart" — finally becomes the protagonist's dying words: "When will the day come when famine is heartless but the state has a heart!" The thematic sentence first exists as a colloquial saying, then is rewritten into the character's words. **Failure mode**: when the folk saying and the character's linguistic register are too far apart (a scholar character speaking a street proverb), rewrite rather than copy. **Self-check**: put the rewritten line back in the character's mouth and say it aloud; if it is only the author's voice and not the character's, go back.

## III. The Third Rail: Desire vs. Misbelief (Cron)

- **Story ≠ plot**. Story is "how what happens affects someone in pursuit of a difficult goal, and how it changes him inside"; writing a great many events without a story is the root cause of 96% of rejected manuscripts.
- **The third rail** = the protagonist's inner struggle: the thing she has long desired vs. the **misbelief** that blocks her. Every action, plot event, and sensory detail must sit on this rail.
- Five fallacies: style above all; free writing; "all first drafts are shit"; outlining external events first; applying an external structural template ("first comes a good story, then comes structure").
- **From a flash to a what-if question, four steps**: ① capture the moment that pierces you ② why you care ③ the theme (cliché is fine — "almost every story begins as a cliché") ④ the what-if question: it contains context, surprise, conflict, and implication, and may run several lines.
- **Why, three steps**: ① what the protagonist wants before the novel begins (from the vague to the specific you can see with your eyes closed) ② why she wants it (what it means to her) ③ **misbelief** — not called a fatal flaw or a trauma: the protagonist is convinced it is true because at a crucial moment in her life it was once "right" ("sincere involvement will hurt you").
- **The origin scene**: the moment the misbelief takes root, written in first person, in chronological order, in detail, with four questions: the old belief before entering / why / the goal of this scene / how the expectation is fulfilled — even if the wish is fulfilled, it can collapse because the cause is the opposite. Add **three turning-point scenes**: each time she comes one step closer to the desire but retreats because of the belief, and desire and belief strengthen together.
- Theme grows out of inner contradiction; the external what-if question triggers the inner contradiction that forms the theme (Romeo: the parents' feud → the desire to be together vs. loyalty to the family).
- The ending ≠ the external event of the last act; it is **the moment the misbelief is defeated and the world is seen with new eyes**. Write the moment of epiphany first; the opening can already hint at the direction.
- Two tests for the primary external problem: the external three columns (can it escalate to the last page, does it have concrete immediate consequences, is there a ticking clock) + the internal two questions (does it force a rebellion against the misbelief, is the emotional damage concrete); surface events (stealing a dog, fixing a pipe) are only means of forcing her to face her inner self.

## IV. The Five Premise Questions and Ten Elements (Hicks)

- **Premise** = a brief statement of the main conflict, with five questions: Who is the protagonist? Who is the antagonist? Why do they fight? What change does the outcome of the conflict bring? Why must the protagonist act? Only when these are answered do you have a gestalt. Write it in three sentences: a protagonist against another; why they must fight each other to reach their respective goals; what change will come to the protagonist's life.
- Drama = conflict that brings **obvious change** to the protagonist's life and changes the surrounding environment at the same time; choose events with direct cause and effect around "the obvious transformation X."
- **Inner need**: the quality the protagonist ought to have but precisely lacks, believes she already has, or considers unimportant (compassion, forgiveness, courage) — the main reason the audience keeps watching is that the protagonist must achieve it in order to resolve the conflict. **External goal**, two tests: the audience must know where the goal is (they need not approve of it); the audience must feel the protagonist is threatened. Check the goal's plausibility against Maslow's hierarchy (fighting for glory when food and shelter are in doubt is unreasonable).
- Theme is not a textbook: "the greatest torture is a film that feels like a textbook"; the audience expects to make the world more understandable through shared experience.

## V. The Theme Stays Fixed; the Attitude Changes (Hoxter)

- Almost every film is guided by a theme; three questions: What does the protagonist want? What is she fighting? Is the theme love / betrayal / loyalty / growth / friendship / revenge / survival? A film about falling in love does not necessarily have love as its theme.
- **The sweet spot: once the theme is determined it never changes; what changes is the attitude toward it**. What is the protagonist's attitude toward the theme at the beginning, and at the end? A naive friend vs. a cynical friend, plus each one's lover = a four-way interaction that "contains the value of the entire script." A theme that is too general or passive is useless; ask, "How does this theme drive the story?"
- Theme test bench: take the same material (a widowed father "carried off" by a space captain) and run it through growth / love / sacrifice / obsession / family / friendship / revenge / justice, and see which theme makes the characters most alive.
- Conflict: both protagonist and antagonist should have goals related to the theme (in 《驯龙高手》 (How to Train Your Dragon), father and son both want to hunt dragons but in different ways); **obstacle ≠ antagonist** (the dragon mother is an obstacle; the father is the antagonist, because only the latter embodies the theme).
- Three questions of the climax: Does the villain's failure express the theme? Has the protagonist done something she could not do in Act One? Does it simultaneously complete the plot line and account for the whole story?
- Character: the protagonist has distinct opinions about six things (from atheism down to how to cook wide noodles) → four related characters each take a position → this generates thematic conflict and plot.

## VI. The One-Line Story (Snyder) and the Logline (Hoxter)

- **Snyder's four elements**: an ironic hook ("A cop comes to Los Angeles to visit his estranged wife, and the building where she works is taken over by terrorists"); a wonderful mental picture (including a time range); target audience and cost; a killer title (《律政俏佳人》 (Legally Blonde) is good; 《为爱还是为钱》 (For Love or Money) is the worst). The enhanced version + an adjective for the protagonist + an adjective for the villain + the primal goal. If you cannot write a passable one-liner, the story itself has a problem; pitch it to a stranger in the Starbucks line and see whether his eyes drift away.
- **The ten story genres** (Monster in the House / Golden Fleece / Out of the Bottle / Dude with a Problem / Rites of Passage / Buddy Love / Whydunit / Fool Triumphant / Institutionalized / Superhero): classified by "what the story is about"; learn the genre's rules first, then break them; when pitching, do not say "X meets Y."
- **Primal enough**: survival, hunger, sex, protecting loved ones, fear of death; for relationships use husband, wife, father, daughter, mother, son, ex.
- **Hoxter's logline**: one or two sentences that make the concept understandable; checklist — protagonist, antagonist, genre, conflict, challenge, stakes, theme, twist, **hook**, inciting incident; formula: "When his partner is killed by an evil alien (inciting incident), tenacious Brooklyn beat cop Lauren Brown (protagonist) must find the aliens' home planet (challenge) to avenge his partner (theme)." **A synopsis ≠ a tagline** (a tagline is post-release marketing); "X meets Y" is not a synopsis. Plot vs. story: plot = the events on screen; story = the subtext, the why, the emotional journey; a synopsis leans toward plot.
- McKee: a title = a name that designates character / setting / theme / genre (《大白鲨》 (Jaws) does all four); a literary title that names nothing is undesirable.
- **Style is determined by the mismatch between "the nature of the character × the nature of the event," not chosen in advance** (魏明伦 (Wei Minglun), p0081): 孟登科 (Meng Dengke) in 《巴山秀才》 (The Bashan Scholar) is a pedantic, absurd comic figure, yet he is caught up in a great tragedy — "**this determines the basic style of our play: a comedy with tears, a tragedy with a smile**." The mismatch is also the source of the ironic hook. **Failure mode**: when the nature of the character matches the nature of the event (a tragic character meets a tragic event), there is no mismatch to use, and the style must be grounded elsewhere. **Self-check**: write down the protagonist's attribute and the event's attribute in one word each; if the two words are the same, do not force the label "tragicomedy."

## VII. Choose the Material Strictly (选材要严, xuǎn cái yào yán) (Lu Jun)

- 鲁迅 (Lu Xun): "Choose material strictly, dig deep; do not fill a piece with any trivial, meaningless incident."
- **Four taboos**: greed for the large and heavy (a big subject ≠ big meaning; a protagonist with high political consciousness ≠ a deep theme; most surviving gems of the short play are drawn from ordinary life); chasing fashion (for a bandwagon subject, "one is worth the same as a thousand"); lack of theatricality (subject and genre out of alignment); mismatched capacity (pressing material for a full-length play into a one-act leaves only a dry outline).
- **Four ways to choose**: **choose with your heart** — write from feeling; choose what makes your heart pound and stays with you for decades (李准 (Li Zhun)'s works all contain "those two sesame cakes"; 丁西林 (Ding Xilin)'s 《压迫》 (Oppression) came from a friend who died from the oppression of renting a house, yet he wrote about a united struggle that wins); **choose with your body** — write what you know (老舍 (Lao She)'s "box": only when you have many clothes do you have something to change into); ancient material must connect with lived experience; if you do not know it, go and soak in a cheap flophouse; "fresh on the outside, familiar on the inside"; **choose with your eye** — artistic discovery; Rodin: "a mediocre artist always wears someone else's glasses"; Flaubert sat before a campfire until he saw how it differed from other fires; **choose with drama** — choose only what can enter drama: the three elements of conflict, action, and emotion; for small-scale opera add "song."
- **Six-character formula: small, simple, with a strong core**. The event must be small (borrowing a pair of boots, delivering a basket of eggs, mediating a quarrel; 《打金枝》 (Striking the Golden Branch) writes about an emperor who does no more than mediate a quarrel; the very word "pointed" (尖, jiān) is itself the truth); the plot must be simple (**anything whose thread cannot be told clearly in five minutes is not a good work**; 李渔 (Li Yu): "cut the side threads and establish the main idea"; simple ≠ thin; **you must complicate the simple thing**: asking for a watermelon → the vine → counting the melons → the seeds); you must have a unique move / a killer technique.
- A foreign writer's three sentences: I have a good story; I have the passion to tell it to the end; **this story can only be told by me, and no one else can tell it — the third sentence is the most important.**
- **Hometown layer vs. native-place layer** (罗怀臻 (Luo Huaizhen), p0029–0030): what stands on dialect jokes, local customs, and "the memory and associations of a hometown once perceived" is the **hometown** layer (家乡层, jiāxiāng céng), and it fails the moment it leaves its popular region; what stands on one's place of origin and lineage, on "a set of pictures or sounds about hometown life told from generation to generation," is the **native-place** layer (原乡层, yuánxiāng céng), and it can move people who never had that experience (descendants of Mongols who migrated to Northern Europe centuries ago are still drawn by the Mongolian long song (长调, chángdiào)). **Self-check**: delete all the dialect jokes and folk-custom displays from the play — what is left? If nothing is left, it is pure hometown-layer material. **Interface**: for localization adaptation, see `sw-format-adaptation`.

## VIII. Dig Deep = Thematic Conception (开掘要深, kāi jué yào shēn; 立意, lìyì) (Lu Jun)

- Goethe: "One of the best signs of originality is the ability, after choosing a subject, to develop it fully." Digging = thematic conception, like chess: one player thinks three moves ahead, another five. Three revisions of 《妇女代表》 (The Women's Representative): strengthen the sanitation campaign (step one) → write the old-vs-new thinking of mother-in-law and daughter-in-law (step two, stale) → connect reforming the family with reforming society, write the struggle for political and economic rights, and 张桂蓉 (Zhang Guirong) takes out the land deed (step three). Adaptation as digging: 《春草闯堂》 (Chuncao Storms the Court) digs out of "a righteous man gets two wives" the idea that "the lowly can shake the feudal order"; 《团圆之后》 (After the Reunion) completely reverses the theme of 《施天文》 (Shi Tianwen), which preached the virtue of the filial widow.
- **Four methods**: ① **big environment, small story** — put the small story into the context of the times (in 《绿鹦鹉》 (The Green Parrot), the actor moves from killing on stage to killing for real, entirely because of the background of the Great Revolution; in 《定心丸》 (The Reassurance Pill), 常根 (Chang Gen) goes from a "soft-shelled crab" to a true man because the new policy restores his dignity); counterexample: 《打工棚》 (The Migrant Workers' Shed) downgrades a social problem into a personal grudge. ② **study the characters** — when inspiration runs dry, study a distinctive real person (六宝 (Liu Bao): poverty strips away her pride, prosperity restores her dignity; 小丁 (Xiao Ding): a social comic strip); 《十五贯》 (Fifteen Strings of Cash) added 《疑鼠》 (The Suspicious Rat) starting from a study of the judge 况钟 (Kuang Zhong). ③ **study the ending** — "the process of digging the idea is the process of constantly studying the ending" (three endings for 《雨夜访贫》 (Visiting the Poor on a Rainy Night): a political fairy tale → no explanation of who repaired the roof → the brick-and-tile magnate: "a Communist county magistrate cannot know only how to visit the poor and ask about their hardships"). ④ **the power of symbol** — in 《动物园的故事》 (The Zoo Story) the park bench = middle-class status.
- **Three nevers**: do not inflate (finding class struggle in a black bench; scoring the bird-fighting ending with the 《黄河颂》 ("Ode to the Yellow River")); do not preach or diagram (Engels: "the tendency should emerge naturally from the situations and the action"; if the finished work equals a billboard slogan, it is a diagram); do not pretend to be deep (if the play still stands after you delete the "good guy / bad guy" labels, do not paste them on).
- **Surface truth vs. essential truth** (罗怀臻 (Luo Huaizhen), p0156–0157): the digging of historical material does not lie in the surface restoration of official systems, architecture, costume, and language — "**the true reproduction of the historical surface is futile and impossible**" — but in "the truth of historical emotion, the truth of historical human nature, the truth of the overall spiritual state of historical people" (to write the Tang dynasty, you must express the overall self-confidence of the people of that era). **Criterion**: "**a field of energy that runs through past and present, one that makes you feel this piece of history could happen at any moment in our own time**." **Failure mode**: documentary works whose selling point is textual research and reconstruction itself are a separate case.

## IX. Take a Fresh Angle (视角要新, shì jiǎo yào xīn) (Lu Jun)

- Subject collisions are not frightening; the five hundred arhats each have a different posture because each is seen from a different angle. The same faithless-lover frame: 《西西里酸橙》 (Sicilian Limes) "squeezes" the faithless man out of the abandoned woman's gaze; 《归来》 (The Return) reflects the abandoned woman from the faithless man's point of view, using the scene of relatives waiting with full enthusiasm to contrast with the cold 王彪 (Wang Biao).
- The charm of angle: to paint "a hidden ancient temple in the deep mountains," paint only a monk carrying water; for "hooves are fragrant on the road where flowers were trampled," paint only butterflies chasing the horse's hooves — find the cut that does not show the subject head-on yet captures all its spirit.
- Three techniques: **turn the ordinary into the marvelous** (in 《我的爸爸》 (My Dad), parents claim an essay; 《小宝贝与小祖宗》 (Little Treasure and Little Ancestor) writes about only-child education with no adult on stage and not one word of preaching); **compress time and space** (in 《利他主义》 (Altruism), a group of people around a single river jump reveal their inner selves); **condense emotion** (in 《骑马下海的人》 (Riders to the Sea), instead of writing the sea voyage, write the family's worry; in 《煤的代价》 (The Price of Coal), the son is only injured yet no one can smile — "today's Ellen is tomorrow's Mary"; in 《永远在一起》 (Together Forever), that chair).
- Three angle requirements for a small play: **peephole** (use "the girl looking at the qipao" rather than "a panorama of the trade fair"); **focus** (the same event, written differently according to character: 崔莺莺 (Cui Yingying) vs. 祝英台 (Zhu Yingtai), 刘翠屏 (Liu Cuiping) vs. 王宝钏 (Wang Baochuan)); **distortion** (a saint arrested, an obituary for a living person, a barber and a nurse in a joint venture — put the main energy on the social-theme level, keep the level of charm implied but unspoken, and avoid "dig wherever there is drama").

## X. Make the Core Strong (戏核要好, xìhé yào hǎo) (Lu Jun)

- Tolstoy: "The most important thing in a work of art is that it should have a focus… and this focus cannot be fully expressed in words."
- **戏核 (xìhé)**: it has a life that extends and generates in the plot, an explosive force that counteracts and intensifies in the conflict, and a deep, unique penetrating power in its meaning — **without it there is no play; without it there is no "this one" play** (in 《三块钱国币》 (Three Dollars in National Currency), the college student deliberately smashes another vase and pays the rich lady three dollars according to her own logic).
- 戏核 is the root; **戏胆 (xìdǎn — the "gall" of the play)** is the branch (a prop or key plot event that does special work: the peach-blossom fan, Chuncao changing the letter); **戏眼 (xìyǎn — the "eye" of the play)** is the leaf (a key character or vivid scene: the magistrate gives up his sedan chair). Test: remove it — does the play still stand? If it does, it was only a branch or leaf.
- Four ways of presenting it: a strange action (building an ancestral hall for a "dead son"; a husband and wife each planning an affair; a love letter written by three schoolboys); a carefully chosen prop (a magic chair that tells the truth; a gong mallet; one cent); a situation in a character's fate (stealing money for a five-yuan children's bicycle); a song or a line or two (the ballad in 《月亮上升的时候》 (The Rising of the Moon); "those typewriters").
- Once the 戏核 is fixed, the theme (in 《婴儿杀戮》 (The Slaughter of the Innocents) it generates a contrast: two children, one dies in care and one is strangled by a relative, for the same reason), the structure (mistaken death → fight over inheritance → resurrection), and the characters (the quack doctor who writes obituaries for the living) all take shape with it.
- Three sources: everyday details (a husband fined; paying back a peddler); the mysteries of a character's psychology (the master steals the servant's cigarettes); the imagined world (bringing a badger to court; a pound of flesh).

## XI. Make the Conflict True and the Plot Surprising (冲突要真、情节要奇, chōng tū yào zhēn, qíng jié yào qí) (Lu Jun)

- **Three levels of true conflict**: true (not false), meaningful (not pointless), dramatic (not argumentative). Eight diseases self-check: no social meaning; false (a soldier who breaks off an engagement after being wounded; a mother-in-law who refuses to acknowledge her daughter-in-law); inaccurate (a contribution bonus; a foreign businessman "testing" the factory director); no dramatic character (a debate of opinions rather than a clash of characters — "people who see the problem the same way can still conflict": 周瑜 (Zhou Yu) vs. 诸葛亮 (Zhuge Liang)); casual misunderstanding; dodging the heavy for the light (a novel conflict that halfway turns into a cliché); a feint; lack of tension. Belinsky: real drama is when both sides want to get the upper hand, each stabs at a particular side of the other's character, and the result produces a new relationship.
- **The plot should be surprising but first true**: "in a one-act play you should write absurd things" (Chekhov); 胡可 (Hu Ke): "the audience likes to see a hundred-year-old woman take command, not a hundred-year-old woman being cared for; they like 武松 (Wu Song) beating a tiger, not beating a dog"; discard what is surprising but has no social meaning (a hand reaching out of a coffin to test the rain). When the plot is not believable, first stand the characters up (four revisions of 《名誉妻子》 (The Honored Wife): make the reason for the marriage sufficient, give the characters a plausible backstory and desire).
- **Finding the extraordinary in the ordinary is the basic skill**: 《借靴》 (Borrowing Boots) distills "borrow, boast, take, sacrifice, try" into one wave with three turns; better to keep the dramatic flavor of a drug name hiding a coded message than to become more and more "principled" and mediocre — "meaning is the rice, writing is the cooking that turns it into a meal, poetry is the brewing that turns it into wine."
- Transforming material: learn Shakespeare's five methods: raise the secondary characters' minds; reposition the antagonist; strengthen the protagonist's intelligence and cut his endurance; patch the holes in plausibility and characterize along the way; reset the ending so that all the characters stand on one line and the play ends quickly.

## XII. From Idea to Premise: A Workflow

1. **Capture the flash** and write down "why I care" (Cron); confirm that it is a story "only I can tell" (Lu Jun).
2. Run the material through **the four uses and four taboos**: Does it come from feeling? Is it familiar, or can you connect it to your own experience? Have you discovered a trait no one else has seen? Can it enter drama? Is the event small enough, the plot simple enough?
3. **Write the theme in one sentence** (a cliché is fine) → derive the **what-if question** (context, surprise, conflict, implication).
4. **Set the premise** (Egri's three-part formula: character trait + leads to + ending) and confirm that you believe it; one play, one premise.
5. **Build the third rail**: what the protagonist desires (concrete and visible), why, what misbelief blocks her; write the origin scene and three turning-point scenes.
6. **Answer Hicks's five questions** and Hoxter's three questions (what she wants / what she fights / the theme word); what attitude do protagonist and antagonist each hold toward the theme; list the attitudes of four related characters.
7. **Dig two moves ahead**: list three thematic conceptions for the same material and choose the highest; use big environment / small story, study the characters, study the ending, symbol.
8. **Find the 戏核**: remove it — does the play still stand? Is it an action, a prop, a situation, or a line?
9. **Fix the angle**: write it head-on or "squeeze" it from the side? Peephole, focus, or distortion?
10. **Write the climax / moment of epiphany**, reason backward from the climax to the controlling idea (value + cause), mark it idealism / pessimism / irony; design the alternating sequence of idea vs. counter-idea.
11. **One-line story** (Snyder's four elements + adjectives + primal goal) and **logline** (protagonist + inciting incident + challenge + stakes + theme, with the hook foregrounded); say it once to a stranger.
12. **Self-check**: the eight diseases of conflict; the three nevers of digging; are two premises walking in two directions? Is the ending caused by the characters' actions rather than an accident? Does the theme word stay fixed while the attitude changes?
