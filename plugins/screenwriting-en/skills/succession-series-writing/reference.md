> This file contains the full comparison tables, script quotations, and evidence lists moved out of SKILL.md. ## II. Script Evidence for the Structure Layer (S1–S12)

**The sample for S1 criterion ②**: switches of location and mode of transport — S3E1 unfolds entirely in the departure lounge, in flight, and in traffic; every `Intercut with:` is a transaction built on information asymmetry. An act must end with a transfer of information or power; it need not have a cliffhanger hook (act placement is an inference).

**S2: container inventory across four seasons (21 containers)**: the gala S1E4, family dinner S1E5, bachelor-party warehouse S1E8, wedding castle S1E9–E10, hunting lodge S2E3, the rival family's ancestral home S2E5, summit S2E6, birthday banquet S2E8, congressional hearing S2E9, yacht S2E10, shareholders' meeting S3E5, donor summit S3E6, birthday party S3E7, wedding S3E9, rehearsal + karaoke S4E02, retreat S4E05, Investor Day S4E06, party S4E07, election night S4E08, funeral S4E09, distribution of effects + board meeting S4E10.

**S2: four ways to use a container**:

- **Stamp on-screen clock captions** — S1E4 gala 4 p.m./6:15/6:30/7:20/7:51/8:25/9:26, a small beat every 20–30 minutes: the glass crisis → the butter crisis → the speech.
- **Use the ritual order as a cue sheet** — S4E09 funeral follows Introductory Rite → Liturgy of the Word → eulogy → Recessional → committal → wake (treating these six stages as act placements is an inference).
- **Use external events as act-break captions** — S4E08's "On screen –"; every call is an external act out.
- **Leave the head seat empty first** — at the S3E9 wedding Logan does not come, everyone is looking for him, and **the ceremony becomes a search**.

**S2: seating is the relationship map**: S3E9 explicitly lists a row of seats, "Kendall, Tom, Shiv, Roman, Contessa, Greg, Comfrey"; three hours later this row splits into two camps.

**S3: seven instances of the pressure chamber + communications cutoff**: hospital corridor S1E2; Thanksgiving's "Skeleton staff" S1E5; the warehouse party's **phone pouch** S1E8 ("Okay, boys, we're pouching."); the castle's phone-check counter S1E9–E10; "Phones on the table" S2E3; the three safe rooms that rhyme with one another S2E4; the white-noise machine and armed guards S3E6. Advanced version S3E7: the birthday party demands that phones be handed in, and **everyone refuses** — the refusal is itself a statement.

**S4: the core-question list for the ten episodes of S2**: to sell or not / who goes to Vaulter / who tells the truth / who is in the room / can they fake 48 hours of happy family / can they sign before the story runs / who gets into the house / who becomes CEO / can they survive with "nobody knew" / who gets handed over.

**S5 sample scene counts for finales**: S3E9 has only 24 scenes (E5 in the same season has 60), four long pillars: a negotiation, a ceremony, a secret conversation, a confrontation. S4E03 and S4E04 have 35 and 34 scenes (the two lowest of the season). S1E2 has 84 scenes, S1E6 has 80. Of S4E03's 35 scenes, the middle section is roughly 30 minutes of real-time action in a single location.

**S6 three measures of season-shape symmetry**: the price version — in S1E1 Kendall acquires Vaulter at $140 a share, while his father collapses and the throne evaporates; in S1E10 he uses **the same 140** to buy his father's company, while he collapses and his father is restored. The line version — S4E03's "What we do today will always be what we did the day our father died" → paid off in S4E10. The music version — Handel's "Sarabande" appears once each in S1's E4, E7, and E10, each time scoring a juxtaposition of "pageantry + wretchedness."

**S7 the climax with the protagonist absent, four versions**: S1E6 no-confidence vote — the initiator is stuck in traffic (a terror threat closes the sky, the tunnel is jammed), speaks remotely by Polycom, and the suspense shifts from "the vote count" to "can he get there"; S1E10 return after the car crash — everyone is dancing, "It's like a dream. **No one missed him.**"; S3E9 — for the first eighty percent of the episode Logan is present only by phone, by report, and by absence, and **absence is the strongest device for sustaining suspense**; S4E03 — the protagonist **dies off-screen**.

**S8 three samples of external clockwork**: S4E08 hands the acts (inference) to the clock of a public event; characters can only decide between two calls; S3E5 takes place entirely on the day of the shareholders' meeting, everyone is packed into the green room, and there are only three reasons to go in or out: to go on stage, to find a bathroom, to take a call; S2E6 is a countdown to "sign before the scandal hits the papers," and the story finally pops up on a phone under the negotiating table.

**S9 two contrasting examples of foreshadow dimming**: S3E3 cut Tom's blunt line "Divorce" to the receptionist because it "cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events," leaving only the lawyer's name **Rex Hendon** for attentive viewers, paid off only in S3E9. Reverse example: S2E8 Shiv stops the champagne with "She doesn't drink"; later in the same episode Logan himself amplifies this piece of gossip into "Pricktease."

**S10 three cases of relocated cut scenes**: S2E10's Roman–Gerri restaurant scene was cut for an overload of material, and its substance was held for the start of the next season (landing in S3E1, where a footnote says it "find a spot to nestle more comfortably"); the "We knew" argument at the S2E10 card table moved to the S3E2 bedroom, where a footnote says it is "more germane and sprung-in" — **the value of a scene depends on whether there is enough pressure in front of it**; S1E1's cut scene of Shiv giving the old-house photo album was later stitched into S2E8 "Dundee."

**S11 mutual contamination within a single occasion, the cleanest example**: S4E07–E09 all use this method. In S4E09 Shiv, in her only act of unconditional kindness, sends exhausted Tom home to sleep (stage direction: "The greatest gift in the world."), which makes Tom absent from the funeral, and Matsson in his S4E10 job interview reads the funeral absence as a plus ("Wasn't like he was gonna un-die if I made it?") — **kindness is rewritten by structure into leverage, and the script never says a word about it**.

**S12 inventory of absurd obstacles**: S1E10's "deliver the acquisition letter" is a one-line action, stretched by three small obstacles into an entire suspense sequence: the printer is connected to the castle's Wi-Fi, a child wanders into the war room, a colleague dodges responsibility. Similar: in S3E3, getting into the building is stalled by a dead access card, the freight elevator, and the air conditioning turned down; S4E08's "I am down to my last fucking touchscreen!" and mustard in the eye.

## III. Script Evidence for the Character Layer (C1–C8)

**C1 the constant layer of seven wants in S1**: Logan wants never to hand over power; Kendall wants to be recognized as the heir; Roman wants to be treated as "a serious person" (S1E2 "you're not a serious person"); Shiv wants to win outside the family on her own merits while keeping the leverage of the Roy name; Tom wants to enter the family and be truly loved; Greg wants a job and not to be humiliated; Connor wants to be treated as the eldest son.

**C2 the humiliation chain in full**: S1E8 is one chain from start to finish — Logan curses Roman for closing a deal where "the other side was smiling" ("It's no good if he's smiling.") → Roman dumps Tom's old friend in the wilderness → Tom uses Greg as a punching bag → Kendall forces Greg to snort two lines of cocaine. S2E3's Boar on the Floor is the ritualized version of this chain.

**C3 three versions of the Dog Pound** (S1E8): Roman trots out his childhood trauma in a business setting (the cage, the dog bowl, the leash) to drive the buyer away; Kendall denies it wholesale ("It was a game. You enjoyed it."); Connor offers a third version (you asked to go into the cage; the bowl contained chocolate cake). Then Connor inadvertently reveals his father's dog-training theory — "You got two fighting dogs. You send the weak one away… Then everyone knows the hierarchy and everyone's happy." — and Kendall, hearing it, "thinks, makes a calculation. And exits." He switches from selling his shares to launching a leveraged buyout.

**C4 each season sends him back to a place that makes him small**: S2E7 "Return" is an entire episode built on this rule — Logan returns to England, Kendall returns to the bridge where he killed someone (and is given a glass of cloudy water in the dead man's kitchen), Roman and Shiv return to their mother's kitchen, Tom sleeps on Greg's floor. S2E8, the intensified version: Logan returns to his childhood home in Dundee and **does not get out of the car** — "Nah. I can see it. I can see it."

**C5 the successor = the most replaceable person, three pieces of evidence**: the S4 introduction states that the children lack application, and more crucially **they arrive at the wrong moment** — nowadays a non-media founder can only be "an amenable man like Tom to rise gently like a bubble of air in the tank. A corporate fusion of Kenneth Widmerpool and Joseph Stalin."; in S4E10 Shiv assesses Tom to Matsson — "a highly modular interchangeable part" (**her assessment is his reason for being appointed**); Matsson offers the job — "I don't need a partner, I need a **frontman**… So I'll need a **pain sponge** while I get under the hood." **Neither of the two thematic lines is spoken by Tom himself.**

**C6 the three old retainers' refusal trio** (S3E9, each more personal than the last): **Gerri (fiduciary duty)** "how does it serve my interests?"; **Karl (money)** "Golden handshake. Big fucking golden handshake."; **Frank (accumulated grievance)** "you are careless and you have been cruel and vain and wanton." — the final line is written as a stage direction, "leaning in so only Roman can hear": **"I've eaten a bellyful for you, son. Go fuck yourself."** Similarly: S2E10's show trial makes each person **write someone else's indictment** instead of defending himself ("argue the brief").

**C7 four sample types: mirror characters and external judges**:

- **Mirror family**: S2E5's Pierces are the mirror of the Roys — the same inheritance anxiety, a different costume of values (grace, the Latin lintel "In veritate triumpho," house wine, reciting Richard II); the more orderly the order on the other side of the table, the louder the Roys' silence.
- **Mirrored incapacity**: S3E5 juxtaposes two incapacitated old men — Sandy, post-stroke, is "translated" by his daughter; Logan, delirious from a UTI, is spoken for by his children.
- **External judge**: Josh Aaronson (S3E4 "So can you work together?"), Mencken, Matsson; their function is to **force the protagonist to say what he would not normally say**, then leave the truth of the line suspended — in S3E4 Logan says publicly, "He's a good kid and I love him… maybe the best one of all of them," and the script then spends a whole stage direction on the ambiguity of the look ("Did he mean it… Or was it the opposite – disdain.").
- **The weakest person names the game's logic**: Greg "I thought you got spared if you told the truth? That seemed to be the rule?"; Willa "Sell seems cool?"; the victim Kira "Oh, okay, here it comes!" **The joke is the theme.**

**C8 the graduations on the physical-decline timer**: S3's motif — in E1 Logan throws his phone and stoops painfully to retrieve it ("All is not well. He's alone."); in E4, on the island death march, he vomits from heatstroke and twists his ankle; E5 UTI delirium; E9 he reads his grandson a children's book about death (*Goodbye Mog*) and, halfway through, finds it bad luck and swaps books. The S1 version: Logan practices walking inside a rectangle of tape on the floor, "Walking's not good. It's fucking **normal**."

## IV. Script Examples for Dialogue and Stage Directions (D1–D13)

**D1 stage directions write subtext and "maybe"**:

- "True? Or passive-aggressive? Kendall sort of wants his dad to tell him what to do, but doesn't want to ask for direction." (S1E1)
- "There is subtext."; "Yes. But maybe no." (S2)
- "But Logan is not happy, not at all." (S2E6)
- "Both a real moment and a highly manipulated and manipulative one." (S4E06, **not either/or**)
- Writing the interior as a question: "When does Kendall think, 'I don't need to have been here'?" (S1E10)
- The "maybe" culture of S3 (Prebble): "Now I always keep the maybes in."

**D2 a reversal turns on a single word**: S2E10 season finale — Kendall at the press conference first **recites the compliant version in full** — "it has been suggested I would be a suitable figure to absorb the anger and concern" — then says only one word: **"But."**

**D3 the trigger must be a word established earlier**: the trigger in S2E10 is **NRPI** ("No Real Person Involved"). In S2E9 it is an internal company document term; in S2E10, in his father's cabin, it becomes a word of comfort for his son — "Nah NRPI, kiddo. You're a good kid." — the father thinks he is granting absolution, but in fact files his son under "not a real person"; **absolution and insult are the same sentence**.

**D4 a comic plant cashed in as evidence**: "Mo Lester" is an inside joke in the first few episodes of S2; at the S2E9 hearing a senator reads it out word for word and it becomes an accusation — "What was it a joke about? 'Mo Lester'?" — the more the defendant tries to escape behind "a joke," the more it proves systemic complicity.

**D5 samples of power aphorisms given to supporting characters**: Frank "You are what you do." / "I am just an attendant lord"; Gerri's sin-cake eater; "Young cardinals vote for old popes"; Stewy translating an epic into "a few extra cents on their dollar. **And that's all this is.**"

**D6 four denials, four character biographies** (the cut card-table scene in S2E10, not written as a confession): Kendall "We knew." / Roman "Well I didn't know." / Shiv "What are you saying we knew?" / Connor "We kind of knew."; Shiv's final "I did not know what I was laughing about" is the most precise character definition of the season.

**D7 let the protagonist lie successfully**: in S1E9 Tom asks "Are you fucking around on me?", and the bet is written into the stage direction — "If she looks away, he'll know. But she doesn't." She lies successfully, and Tom therefore volunteers every detail of the cruise scandal to her, including "Greg's expendable."

**D8 a polite question as the turn**: S3E9's biggest turn of the season (Matsson proposing the acquisition) is written as — "…if you want to tell me to fuck off, tell me to fuck off?" — Logan: "You're not fucking serious."

**D9 let the antagonist speak the protagonist's interior monologue**: the next line in the same scene, Matsson "You're thinking, 'Every bit of me wants to tell him to fuck off and eat shit except for the bit that knows every word he says is true.'" — saving an entire scene of introspection while establishing the new antagonist's intellectual rank.

**D10 three ways comedy carries tragedy**:

- (a) **The darkest confession is caught by an inside-family joke**: S3E9 Roman chops the charge of murder down level by level to "at worst you're a **manslaughter-er**," and the script notes, "It is so dark that it is kind of funny."
- (b) **Downgrade an undebatable big issue into a debatable small one**: S4E08 "So because we had so much chicken when we were kids, I have to elect a fascist?" / "Yeah. Basically."
- (c) **Use comedy to complete mourning**: S4E04's obituary codebook game — "'A complicated man'" → "Threw phones at underlings."
- The principle (Frank Rich): "drama or comedy" is a false question; the laugh grows out of the character's own psychology, not the spectator's sarcasm; "The show is a human comedy, not a satire" — **you laugh with them first, then you cry with them**.

**D11 an argument escalates to the most vulnerable point**: every line of the S4E07 balcony marital battle gets closer to the other's most vulnerable spot, landing on "you are maybe not a good person to have children." — and the audience knows Shiv is pregnant; Tom does not; the stage direction says only "A shift at this partly successful attempt to actually hurt her."

**D12 three samples of omission as theme**: S4E09, after the two old retainers finish talking about Logan, they look at each other — **"Right?" "Right."**; S4E08 Darwin's caveats are never broadcast; S1E10's title comes from Berryman's "Dream Song 29," but a footnote says letting the poem flow out of the radio was "far too direct" — **keep the source of the poetry in the title, delete it from the image**.

**D13 when a relationship has no new dialogue, rewrite it as physical action**: S4E10, when Kendall hugs Roman he grinds the stitches open ("the stitches tear a little, blood seeps"), and the dialogue says only "I love you, man." / "I fucking hate you." The final passage of the whole series: Tom extends his palm, "Not eager but available… **no squeeze. Just contact.** … A queen and a prince. **Two bombs being transported with great care.**"

## V. The Writers' Room and Production: Evidence from Footnotes and First-Hand Accounts

**Writing long (corresponds to Section 5, item 1)**: "I like to write long and I encourage my fellow writers to write long. I rewrite long." The reason: shooting a few more **dialogue pages** (rather than car crashes) "returns exponential rewards," and only then does the edit have room to refocus; writing long also permits **not choosing**, preserving those "small or weird or nothingy" moments.

**The wall and the mega-chart (Section 5, item 4, Prebble)**: the writers' room had 8–12 people, a British-American mix, a mix of comedy and tragedy backgrounds, meeting 5–6 hours a day. Large sheets of white paper were headed with **character names**, ideas written beneath — under Roman "Afraid of sharks?", under Kendall in S2 "Beaten Dog" and in S3 "**Goes Beyond**"; surviving items entered the **mega-chart** — characters on the horizontal axis, episodes on the vertical; "The basic beats of the season were plotted within."

**Entering the room with the season ending fixed (Section 5, item 5)**: before the S3 room opened, only three things were locked — the siblings would join forces, Tom must inform, and "how we got there was totally up for grabs."

**Pitching starts with the trashy version (Section 5, item 6, Prebble)**: openings use "This is the trashy version" / "Not this, but maybe – Something like this." rather than "This is shit but –" ("Pitching bestows terrible privilege on the articulate."). **The S3E7 generation chain is the template**: public suicide → performance art that fakes a suicide → deciding not to go on stage and sing. "**Pitching is the way in which you work out what the show isn't, as well as what it is.**"

**Two ranking rules (Section 5, item 7)**: "An appeal to reality over the mythic was always the most powerful pitch in the room, only occasionally losing out to whatever was the most funny." Sample argument: whether Logan would sell the company, "Murdoch literally did it, it's more real!"

**The "does two things" cut test (Section 5, item 8)**: Tom not going to prison was kept because it does three things at once — shows how the system works + advances the marriage crisis + creates the funniest scene. **Anything that does fewer than two gets cut.**

**Footnote evidence table: page → set → edit; the latter two have the power to veto the first (Section 5, item 9)**

| Episode | Change | Reason given in the footnote | Stage overruled |
|---|---|---|---|
| S4E02 | The "fake cancer" was cut | "a stratagem too far" | Page |
| S4E09 | Gerri's entire consolation scene was deleted | "in the cut he seemed too far gone to be reached" — **the character's state no longer accepts comfort** | Edit over page |
| S4E10 | The signing scene was rewritten | "Kieran really didn't feel Roman would go into the big room to sign" | Set over page |
| S4E09 | Matsson's car | Forced by scheduling, yet better | Production constraint improves page |
| S3E8 | The entire nanny line was cut | The reason was simply "the episode was just too long" | Edit |
| S3E6 | Roman doesn't use drugs | Kieran Culkin believed Roman doesn't use drugs and steered around any script hint | Actor over page |
| S3E3 | Tom's "Divorce" was cut | "cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events" | Edit (see S9) |
| S2E10 | The Roman–Gerri restaurant scene was cut | Overload of material; in S3E1 it "find a spot to nestle more comfortably" | Edit + relocation (see S10) |

The universal principle of the cutting room: "the edit generates its own powerful centrifugal force – throwing off material which is further from the show's core questions and concerns."

**The distribution of footnotes is itself information**: in the S1 volume, 15 footnotes cluster in E1 (6), E3 (5), E2 (2), and E10 (2), with almost none in E7–E9 — **the episodes that shot smoothest have no footnotes**. A footnote is "the reason for a change," not "the intention of the writing."

**The final image is left to the set (Section 5, item 10)**: S3E9 footnote, "At script, I didn't know what the final image would be."; S4 introduction, "You won't find the end of the show, as it went to air, written in these scripts. **That we found on the day.**"

**Support system and firefighting (Section 5, item 11)**: consultants were divided by field — Merissa Marr on business operations, Jon Klein on media, Jesse Eisinger and Matt Friedrich on legal, Tracey Pruzan and Derek Blasberg on New York society, Eric Schultz and Ben Ginsberg on politics; firefighting relied on a fixed group of writers — "to help me write – and to rewrite – when we had an episode that wasn't firing."

**What a showrunner is (Section 5, item 12)**: "essentially a writer. But a writer empowered to protect the scripts through the rooms where they are conceived and written and rewritten, performed, captured and edited." Running the writers' room = **throwing a party**; the room was in London rather than New York for the sake of "a sense of having been forgotten about"; at the same time, **2–3 writers were kept on set every day**; the soundproofing was so bad that key plot points had to be given code names (one in S4 was "Larry David").

**Characters rewritten because of actors (Section 5, item 14, Frank Rich)**: Gerri was originally a man, "Jerry"; Hugo, Matsson, and Kerry were given more material because of the performances; in S4, Sarah Snook's pregnancy generated a mid-season storyline — on the condition that "the series' architecture could support it without buckling the overall design."

## VI. The Evidence Chain for Engineering the Ending

**1 The engine-losing-pressure thesis**: it was not creative exhaustion ("the motor in the room was still running hot") but rather **"the big plot engine, the brutal materialist plumbing which ran under the show, was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure."** The external world supplied a deadline at the same time: legacy media's war on tech "was not a fair fight, it was a rout"; Redstone died, Murdoch faded.

**2 Choosing one of three season shapes, by writers' committee vote**: December 2021, Brixton; on the wall was written "one final season of ten episodes, or two of six or eight episodes." Will Tracy: one season; Jon Brown: two seasons (he had always thought five was the right shape); Tony Roche wanted to continue but thought creatively it should end; Lucy Prebble settled it — "**the ten-episode season was the muscular way to go out.**"

**3 The four anchors of S4**: ① Logan dies early, suddenly, "a blast from the captive bolt pistol"; ② the children experience it by phone at a wedding; ③ the GoJo deal **must go through** — "That was the case in the world, so it had to be the case for us."; ④ Tom wins (from S2 on it "started to glitter in the distance").

**4 The eight steps of the off-screen death** (S4E03; Armstrong says this section "came in a tumble, the long middle section barely revised after its first draft"):

1. Information enters through a single unreliable door — Tom "I wasn't there"; the medical authority is the anonymous "MD Live," and he even gets it wrong as "MD Line."
2. Someone else turns "goodbye" into an action — "speak to him… in case it's a last chance."
3. Three "by his ear" monologues, each ≤8 lines, with no response, each a compression of that character's season arc, the emotion carried by technical repetition — "Am I by his ear?"
4. A ladder of denial — "Medically, right." → "five, three percent possible" → "What if it's a drill?"
5. Even "telling" is a power move — "do we definitely tell him?"
6. They are physically separated from the body — the boat leaves shore.
7. The officialization of death is a string of technical nodes — CPR for 25/30 minutes → "They've stopped the CPR." → "Is he covered?" → the share price → the pronouncement.
8. The irony comes from making each person do what their job requires — Karolina lists the timeline, Frank writes "my version," Tom has Greg delete the J drive.

**5 Omission as theme**: the method is to set up the setup, hold the tone, build the world right, and then "**step back from the mechanism, let it run**." His definition of satire is a single sentence: "Taking a hard look at the world as it is." — and "afflicting the comforted… will go better if you avoid announcing your intention too clearly at the door."

**6 The regression game** (S4E10: in the kitchen the three siblings mix the new king a "meal fit for a king": mayo, cocoa powder, sugar, Branston Pickle, milk, a lot of Tabasco, and Shiv spits in it too). Four points:

- (a) For the first and last time, the three are in the same intention, and every laugh is **collaborative**;
- (b) their mother's stinginess ("don't eat Peter's cheese, darling") supplies the material to imitate;
- (c) the only time someone tries to say the tender thing out loud ("We're gonna be alright, right?") it is immediately cut off ("Drink it and shut the fuck up!") — **this unfinished pause is the ending's preview**;
- (d) the spit is the smallest rehearsal of betrayal.

**7 ghost phone**: S4E10 on the terrace Kendall picks up a pretend phone — "Ghost phone. Spooky. … He's saying, yeah it is Roman. Okay, Dad. What— Kill … Shiv …" — Shiv plays along; then the same prop carries sincerity — "He fucking promised it to me. Promised. When I was seven." / "No. He shouldn't have said that."

**8 Letting the show "not know it was ending"**: throughout writing and shooting, multi-season versions were kept alive ("Almost so that the show itself didn't know it was ending"), which avoided both an ending that was "freeze-dried and pre-packaged" and "the metal gears of LA business" crushing every possibility in advance.

**9 *Logan's Dinner*: writing the most moving passage as a "cuttable background layer."** The S4 appendix is a full scene written last, initially only "a bit of additional flavour… which might or might not make the cut" for the TV in the background of E10. Each guest gets a "party piece": Logan recites in rhyme **the list of losing presidents** — his mnemonic stores only the losers; Gerri "My party piece has been keeping you out of jail for twenty years."; Karl sings Burns's "Green Grow the Rashes," and Logan covers the emotion with "You're fucking murdering it, man." His first line is "**Film it!**"; his last is "I'd like to have it with me always— in case I go fucking rat-catching."

**10 Open writing is method, not vagueness**: quoting Darcey Bussell — what draws you is not perfect execution but "the fear that she might be on the verge of falling"; the showrunner's power must be used to protect the gaps, otherwise the writing becomes "closed-off, invulnerable… every scene sleek and sealed" — "**the cracks are where the light gets in**."

### 10.2 Comparison of the Four Volumes' Notes on the Text

| Volume | What the Note on the Text records | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | Position: the script is "just a recipe" (a recipe – a set of instructions for action and suggested dialogue), but it must itself have coherence and integrity. Reasons for divergence are **ranked by likelihood in seven categories**: ① The edit (the countermeasure is to write long, 70+ pages per episode); ② Alt (one or two days before shooting, pick 0–15 lines and send them to 3–5 writers); ③ Late change (ballpoint pen on paper); ④ Fog of war (small word-order changes; an actor's paraphrase counts as an "implied note"); ⑤ Improvisation (one freebie take after the scripted version is shot); ⑥ ADR (clarifying plot, adjusting details, adding laughs); ⑦ The remembrance of things past (reordering after the table read; actors stitching deleted fragments back in) | The ranking is itself the three-stage power table: **the edit comes first; the page only reaches as far as "write long"** |
| S2 / S3 / S4 (all three volumes use the same formulation) | All three volumes print **the scripts as they stood when shooting began** ("as they stood when we started to film"); differences from the broadcast version are attributed to only **three causes**: material cut for length in the edit / new lines given by the writer and colleagues on set / actor improvisation. Footnotes are added only where "the reason for a change is not self-evident," along with research sources. The S2 volume reports 12 footnotes in the whole book | The three causes correspond to the last two stages of the three-stage model; **a footnote is "the reason for a change," not "the intention of the writing"**; the identical formulation across three volumes shows this is a system, not an accident. Cross-confirmed by the S4 introduction: "the final draft of the show isn't the script. It's the version we air." |
