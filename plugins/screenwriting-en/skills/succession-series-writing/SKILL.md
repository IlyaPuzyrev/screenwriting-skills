# Succession-Style Series Writing (A Breakdown of Armstrong's Four Seasons and 39 Episodes)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

This skill is distilled from an episode-by-episode breakdown of Jesse Armstrong's *Succession: The Complete Scripts*, four volumes (Faber, 2023): ten episodes in S1, ten in S2, nine in S3, and ten in S4 — **39 shooting scripts** in total — together with Armstrong's introductions to S1 and S4, the Note on the Text in all four volumes, all of the author's footnotes, and first-hand accounts of the London writers' room by Frank Rich (S2 introduction) and Lucy Prebble (S3 introduction, "One Last Pitch").

**The episode-by-episode structural parameter table for all 39 episodes across four seasons (Section VIII) and the season-arc grid for all four seasons (Section IX)**, the ten-set-piece comparison table, the original quotations and evidence lists for each pattern (S/C/D numbering), the footnote evidence table (an eight-row summary in Section V; the complete set in Section X), the ending-engineering evidence chain, and the list of thematic statements and maxims are all in [reference.md](reference.md) in this directory.

Core creed (Armstrong's own account): **"the script is just a recipe"** (a recipe – a set of instructions for action and suggested dialogue); **"I like to write long, and I encourage my colleagues to write long, and when I rewrite I write long too"**; **"I have nothing to say, only something to show you"** (quoting Benjamin). The paradox of satire: with nothing to say it does not live; once you say it, it dies — "like a leaflet scattered in the street."

General methods are not repeated here: for single-episode act structure and the typology of act outs, see `sw-series-structure`; for the engine, bible, pilot, and character network, see `sw-series-engine-bible`; for writers'-room process and taking notes, see `sw-writers-room`; for general dialogue method, see `sw-dialogue`; for scene value turns, see `sw-scene-craft`.

## Sources and a Note on the Text

**The four volumes print the scripts as they stood when filming began** ("as they stood when we started to film"), not the broadcast versions. The four Notes on the Text give the same list of differences, ordered by likelihood: **editing** (the main one) → **alts** (lines replaced a day or two before shooting) → **late change** (not caught in time for typing, changed in ballpoint on paper) → **fog of war** (small changes in word order) → **improvisation** (the freebie take) → **ADR** → **the remembrance of things past** (reordering after the read-through; actors remember the old draft and stitch deleted fragments back in on set). Armstrong says publishing the shooting scripts made him feel vulnerable, because "the final draft of the show isn't the script. It's the version we air."

**Footnotes are added only where "the reason for a change is not self-evident,"** and they record research sources along the way. **The distribution of footnotes is itself information** — the episodes that shot most smoothly have no footnotes (for the distribution statistics for S1 and all the footnote evidence, see Section V of reference.md). A footnote is "the reason for a change," not "the intention behind the writing."

**Formatting features**: **no ACT marks, no page numbers**. Scene headings read `Int./Ext. LOCATION – Day/Night`. From S1E2 onward the scripts use **DAY ONE / day two** story-day markers (S2E2 runs from day one to day seven); S4E05 and S4E10 print `day one / day two` subheads directly on the page; S4E08 prints **timestamps** (4:54 P.M. → 3:40 A.M.) and writes "On screen –" in the margin beside each state's vote-count status. Extensive use of `Intercut with:` for phone cross-cutting. Stage directions often say "Maybe …?"; `(then)` marks a pause before a change of subject; `[square brackets]` hold the second half of an interrupted line that is never spoken, written out so the actor can understand it.

**The "invisible act" is an inference, not text**: every "invisible act" division in this skill and in reference.md comes from a **scene-by-scene inference** about the scripts in `_notes/`; the scripts themselves carry no act marks, and the basis is stated each time. **Treat it as a testable analysis, not as a declaration by Armstrong** — across the four volumes he never once talks about his own structure in terms of "acts."

## I. The Four Seasons: Comparison and Evolution

| Season | Episodes | Engine question | Season shape | Written by Armstrong himself | How the season finale redraws the alliances |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| S1 (2018) | 10 | Will Logan hand over power today? | **A symmetrical U-turn**: E1 takes Vaulter at $140 a share but loses the throne; E10 pays the same 140 for his father's company but loses everything | E1, E9, E10 | Logan wins, and his way of winning is **owning the murder suspicion hanging over his son**; the story shifts from "four children competing" to "a father holding each of their debts" |
| S2 (2019) | 10 | To sell or not to sell? And if it comes to a fight, who takes over? | **Choose an heir → choose a sacrifice**: the season finale answers not "who inherits" but "who gets handed over" | E1, E9, E10 | The father hands over his most obedient son as a blood sacrifice; the son hands over his father on live television; the father looks pleased — **the engine resets to war** |
| S3 (2021) | **9** (the only nine-episode season) | The father wants to sell the kingdom; do the children have any cards to play? | **Father against children → sibling alliance**: they had one card, and the parents tore it up before the children could walk in the door | E1, E2, E8, E9 (the first two and the last two of the season) | The alignment becomes "the siblings' alliance vs. the father + Tom + Matsson"; Tom gets a wink on the balcony |
| S4 (2023) | 10 (final season) | Can inheritance be decided by the siblings' own vote? | **The protagonist dies at the 30% mark**, and the remaining seven episodes are aftershocks; E10 puts the season's material goal into a single procedural vote and places the characters' fates in the 20 minutes after the vote | E1, E3, E4 (with Prebble), E8, E9, E10 | Not a redrawing of alliances but **switching off the engine**: the company name does not change, but the surname does — Matsson — and the CEO is the most replaceable person |

**The evolution line** (as practiced in the later seasons): **scene counts fall and scenes lengthen** (S1E2 has 84 scenes; the season finale S3E9 has only **24 scene headings**, four long pillars holding up an entire episode); **act marks move from hidden to visible** (S1E1 has no DAY markers at all; S4 prints subheads and timestamps directly on the page — **streaming writers are still writing acts, they are just disguising acts as a schedule**); **containers become more fixed** (eight of S4's ten episodes are compressed into a public occasion with a fixed protocol); **answers arrive earlier and earlier** (in S1 a question often spans episodes — E5 counts the votes / E6 calls the result; in S4 each episode's question is answered by the end of that episode); **the stakes degrade from "who inherits" to "who gets disposed of"** (S1 asks about the throne, S2 about the sacrifice, S3 about whether there is a vote at all, S4 about "whether a dead man's handwriting counts as a voice"). For the item-by-item scene counts and symmetry measures, see Section II, S5 and S6, of reference.md.

## II. Reusable Patterns at the Structural Layer

**S1 The three tests for an invisible act.** With no ACT marks, mark acts with three things: ① **story-day markers** (a change of day is a change of act); ② **a change of location and mode of transport**; ③ **the moment the core question turns**. The end of an act must contain **a transfer of information or power**; it does not need a cliffhanger. The closing syntax is an event plus a new question, or **a lone button-style stage direction** (S3E9, Roman on the lake, thumbing his phone: "Gerri? Shiv?? Kendall??? **No one to call.**"). All act positions are inferences.
> First write out each episode's 2–7 story days, find one transfer of power within each day — that is your act. No episode should exceed seven days (S2E2's day one–seven is the ceiling).

**S2 Container episodes and the ritual beat sheet.** Compress the whole episode into **a public occasion with a fixed protocol**, and let the protocol's nodes replace act breaks. Four uses: **stamp clock times on screen**, **use the rite as a schedule**, **use an external event as an inter-act title card**, **leave the head seat empty first** (S3E9, Logan does not come to the wedding, and **the ceremony becomes a search**). Also: **seating is the relationship map**. For the full spectrum of 21 containers across the four seasons, samples of the four uses, and the seating evidence, see Section II, S2, of reference.md.
> List the fixed steps first (arrival, gifts, toasts, speeches, the first dance, fireworks), and put one conflict into each step. The stronger the ritual's order, the stronger the sense of transgression in the hidden line, so conspiracies can advance only through corridor conversations, things passed palm to palm, the bathroom, the parking lot.

**S3 Pressure chamber + communications cutoff.** Lock everyone in one building, **then add a physical restriction on information** (S1E8, the phone-pouching bags at the warehouse party: "Okay, boys, we're pouching."). Restrict the flow of information first, then make information travel by body. The advanced version is S3E7: the phones are demanded, and **everyone refuses** — the refusal is itself a statement. For seven examples, see Section II, S3, of reference.md.
> Constraint does not create difficulty; it creates a demonstration of "who can change the rules."

**S4 One core question per episode, answered within the episode.** The syntax is fixed: "Can X do Y today?" The answer comes at the end of the episode (for the list of questions across all ten episodes of S2, see Section II, S4, of reference.md).
> Cross-episode suspense is carried by the new configuration of power, not by unanswered questions.

**S5 Season finales: fewer scenes, longer.** S3E9 has only 24 scenes (E5 in the same season has 60), four long pillars: a negotiation, a ceremony, a private talk, a confrontation.
> Compress the season finale into 3–5 long pillars. If the finale's scene count is above the season average, you are probably writing an "event list," not a "reckoning."

**S6 Symmetry of season shape.** In S1E1 Kendall takes Vaulter at $140 a share while his father collapses and the throne evaporates; in S1E10 he uses **the same 140** to buy his father's company while he himself collapses and his father is restored. For the line version and the music version, see Section II, S6, of reference.md.
> Give a season one repeatable quantity (a price, a line, a tune), use it once at the start and once at the end, and explain it nowhere in between.

**S7 A climax with the protagonist absent.** S1E6, the vote of no confidence: the initiator is stuck in traffic and speaks remotely on a Polycom, and the suspense shifts from "the vote count" to "whether he can get there." **Absence is the strongest device for sustaining suspense**, and its extreme form is S4E03 — the protagonist **dies off-screen**. For four versions, see Section II, S7, of reference.md.

> ⚠️ **Two preconditions; remove one and the device becomes a flaw**, and item 5 of the diagnostic checklist in `sw-chinese-series-practice` ("the protagonist is present and has enough screen time") will rule it a failure outright — both are right; the difference lies in the preconditions:
> 1. **The absent person must still be exerting will** (speaking remotely on a Polycom, a phone call, a report, a rule he laid down), and **the episode must be about his absence**. Someone who merely "does not speak" during the absence is not a device but a hole.
> 2. **The sense of presence must have a reserve.** Succession's absences all occur after the audience has followed dozens of episodes; in a show that has aired only three episodes, the protagonist's presence is not yet strong enough to be sustained by an empty seat. **Use with caution early on.**
> The real diagnostic question is not "Is he present?" but "**Is action being generated during his absence?**"
> Move the initiator of the climax off the scene. Suspense then shifts from "How will it turn out?" to "Can he get there / will he be found out / will they believe him?"

**S8 Decisions in the gap between external clock times.** S4E08 hands the acts (inferred) over to the clock of a public event, and the characters can decide only between two calls. For three samples, see Section II, S8, of reference.md.
> Fix the external clock times first (the vote-count call, the ceremony's protocol, the board roll call, the time a story goes live), and fill the gaps with the characters' choices — the decision then looks as if time forced it.

**S9 Dimming the foreshadowing.** S3E3 cut Tom's blunt line "Divorce" because it "cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events," leaving only the lawyer's name, **Rex Hendon**, for attentive viewers, and cashing it in only in S3E9. For the counter-example, see Section II, S9, of reference.md.
> Cut the blunt line and leave only a name, a habit, an inconsistency. The attack need not point at the heart of the matter — let the opponent show an inconsistency in something trivial, then hand it to a third party to detonate.

**S10 Relocating cut scenes.** **Do not throw away a cut scene; move it to a position with a lower "resting heart rate."** The "We knew" argument at the card table in S2E10 moved to the bedroom in S3E2, and the footnote says it was "more germane and sprung-in" there — **the value of the same scene depends on whether there is enough pressure before it**. For three cases, see Section II, S10, of reference.md.
> When a scene is cut, note whether it is "in the wrong position" or "wrong"; wait for a structural gap for the former, and throw away only the latter.

**S11 Mutual contamination on the same occasion.** S4E07–E09 all use this method: stuff the deal line and the marriage line into the same party, corridor, or funeral, so that a victory on one line automatically becomes the cause of defeat on the other — **goodwill is rewritten by structure into leverage, and the script never says a word about it**. For the cleanest example (Shiv tells Tom to go home to sleep → Tom misses the funeral → Matsson reads it as a plus), see Section II, S11, of reference.md.

**S12 Technical failure delays action.** In S1E10, "delivering the acquisition letter" is a one-line action, stretched by three small obstacles into an entire passage of suspense; for a similar case, see Section II, S12, of reference.md ("I am down to my last fucking touchscreen!").
> An absurd prop makes a grand decision look like an office accident — this is the show's basic tone for handling power.

## III. Reusable Patterns at the Character Layer

**C1 Seven wants in one room.** An ensemble drama needs no extra conflict design; just put seven people's wants for the episode into the same room and let them block one another (for the constant layer of seven wants in S1, see Section III, C1, of reference.md).
> After writing, check: which two people's wants do not collide in this episode? That line is filler.

**C2 Humiliation passed downward.** Anyone who receives a humiliation must pass it on to someone weaker in the next scene. S1E8 is a single chain for the whole episode (Logan → Roman → Tom → Greg; full text in Section III, C2, of reference.md); the Boar on the Floor in S2E3 is the ritualized version of this chain.
> This rule generates scenes automatically. When you cannot write the next scene, ask, "Who was just humiliated, and who is the weakest person around him?"

**C3 Three versions of a family memory.** The Dog Pound in S1E8: three versions of the same childhood incident (the person involved's accusation / the brothers' flat denial / a third party's third version). The third party then inadvertently leaks the father's theory of dog training, and Kendall, hearing it, "thinks, makes a calculation. And exits." — changing from selling his shares to launching a leveraged buyout. For the full text of all three versions, see Section III, C3, of reference.md.
> Let the version the third party lets slip by accident be the trigger of the turn, not a thematic annotation. The unreliability of memory is the driver of the turn.

**C4 Send each character back once a season to a place that makes him smaller.** S2E7, "Return," is an entire episode built on this rule; S2E8 is the intensified version: Logan returns to the old family house in Dundee and **does not get out of the car** — "Nah. I can see it. I can see it." (For the full list across the cast, see Section III, C4, of reference.md.)
> At least once per person per season. The harshest version is to have him reach the door and not go in.

**C5 The heir = the most replaceable person.** S4's winner is Tom: the children lack application, and more importantly, **they arrive at the wrong moment**. The theme is carried by two lines of dialogue, **neither of them spoken by Tom himself** — in S4E10, when Matsson offers the job, he says, "I don't need a partner, I need a **frontman**… a **pain sponge**." For the original introduction and Shiv's comment, see Section III, C5, of reference.md.
> If the theme is "the empire no longer belongs to the family," the person who ends up on top must be someone with no agenda, a high pain threshold, and the ability to be swapped out at any time; the reason for the appointment must be spoken by an enemy or by the person who despises him most.

**C6 The opponents are more logical than the protagonist, and each for a different reason.** The core of the S3E9 showdown is not the father but the three old retainers' threefold refusal of help, each more personal than the last (fiduciary duty / money / accumulated grievance); the final blow is written as a stage direction, "leaning in so only Roman can hear." For the three speeches and similar samples, see Section III, C6, of reference.md.
> Not one reason is "the villain is simply bad." Shrink the final blow to a whisper that only one person can hear.

**C7 Mirror characters and external judges.** Four devices: **mirror family** (the Pierces in S2E5 — the more orderly the other side is, the louder the Roys' silence); **mirror incapacity** (S3E5 juxtaposes two elderly people who are spoken for); **external judge** (Josh, Mencken, Matsson, whose function is **to force the protagonist to say things he would not normally say** and then to leave the truth of the line in suspension); **the weakest person names the logic of the game** (Greg, Willa, Kira) — **the joke is the theme**. For samples of the four types and quotations, see Section III, C7, of reference.md.

**C8 Physical decline as a hidden timer.** S3's motif: from bending down to pick up a phone to UTI delirium to reading his grandson a children's book about death. The S1 version: Logan practices walking inside a rectangle of tape on the floor, "Walking's not good. It's fucking **normal**." (For the gradations, see Section III, C8, of reference.md.)
> Write "there is not enough time" as a physical detail, not as a line. Three or four times a season, each more specific than the last.

## IV. The Dialogue and Stage-Direction Layer

For the full quotations and examples of the thirteen patterns, see Section IV of reference.md.

**D1 Stage directions carry the subtext and the "maybe."** Write the real intention as a note, then let the dialogue say the opposite: "True? Or passive-aggressive? Kendall sort of wants his dad to tell him what to do, but doesn't want to ask for direction." (S1E1). You also need to be able to characterize without settling on one of two options, write the inner life as a question, and keep the maybe (Prebble: "Now I always keep the maybes in.").
> Do not fix the expression; write the character's psychological question; keep the maybe; use square brackets for the unspoken second half of an interrupted line. The gaps you leave on the page are what the set can give you back.

**D2 A reversal turned on a single word.** S2E10, the season finale: at the press conference Kendall first **recites the compliant version in full**, then says one word: **"But."**
> You must first make him deliver the compliant version without missing a word, so that the people in the room and the audience truly believe he has accepted it.

**D3 The trigger must be a word established earlier.** The trigger in S2E10 is **NRPI** ("No Real Person Involved"): in S2E9 it is an internal company-document term; in S2E10, in his father's cabin, it becomes a word that comforts the son — the father thinks he is pardoning him, but he is actually classifying his son as "not a real person." **The pardon and the insult are the same sentence.**
> The trigger must be a word established earlier, and the speaker's intention must change between its two uses.

**D4 Comic foreshadowing recovered as evidence.** "Mo Lester" is an inside joke in the early episodes of S2; at the S2E9 hearing, when a senator reads it back word for word, it becomes an accusation; the more the defendant tries to escape with "it was a joke," the more he proves systemic acquiescence.
> Every time you write a joke, think once about "how it would be read in court." The villain's strongest weapon is an old joke.

**D5 Give the power maxims to supporting characters.** At least one quotable maxim per episode, preferably given to a supporting character, while the protagonist is responsible for not listening (Frank: "You are what you do.").
> The epic sense is carried by the characters, and the cost is exposed by dialogue. Let an outsider translate your family epic into a few extra cents a share.

**D6 Four kinds of denial are four miniature character studies.** The cut card-table scene in S2E10 is written not as a confession but as four kinds of denial, and Shiv's "I did not know what I was laughing about" is the most precise character definition of the season.
> Write the ensemble's complicity as several different modes of denial — four people's ways of denying are four character profiles.

**D7 Let the protagonist lie successfully.** In S1E9 Tom asks, "Are you fucking around on me?" and the bet is written in the stage direction: "If she looks away, he'll know. But she doesn't." She lies successfully, and so Tom voluntarily hands her every detail of the cruise scandal.
> A successful lie drives the plot better than getting caught — the lie becomes next episode's debt.

**D8 A polite question as the turn.** The biggest turn of the whole season in S3E9 (Matsson proposes the acquisition) is written as a question that leaves the other person a way out, and Logan replies, "You're not fucking serious."
> Write a major turn as a question that leaves the other person a way out. The other person's "You're not serious" absorbs all the shock for you.

**D9 Let the opponent speak the protagonist's interior monologue.** In the next line of the same scene, Matsson speaks Kendall's interior monologue for him — saving an entire scene of introspection while establishing the new opponent's intellectual rank.

**D10 Comedy carries tragedy.** Frank Rich's principle: "drama or comedy" is a false question; "The show is a human comedy, not a satire" — **you laugh with them first, and then you cry with them**. Three ways of executing it: (a) the darkest confession is answered with a joke inside the family (S3E9's script note to itself: "It is so dark that it is kind of funny"); (b) downgrade an undebatable big issue into a debatable small one; (c) use comedy to complete mourning.

**D11 A quarrel escalates to the most vulnerable point.** Every line in the S4E07 balcony fight between husband and wife gets closer to the other's most vulnerable spot, landing on "you are maybe not a good person to have children" — and the audience knows Shiv is pregnant; Tom does not.
> Let the audience hold a piece of information that one of the parties does not know, so that the cruelest line does double damage; leave the reaction to the actor.

**D12 Omission is the theme.** In S4E09, after the two old retainers finish talking about Logan, they look at each other — **"Right?" "Right."**
> Let the heaviest moral information in the whole show stop inside an ellipsis. The omitted line is the theme.

**D13 When a relationship has no new lines, rewrite it as a physical action.** In S4E10, when Kendall hugs Roman, he grinds the stitches open ("the stitches tear a little, blood seeps"); in the show's final passage, Tom holds out his palm, "**no squeeze. Just contact.**"
> Rewrite it as a physical action whose meaning both parties take for granted, then fix the relationship with a single line of definition, offering no explanation.

## V. The Writers' Room and Production Layer

For the general process, see `sw-writers-room`; this section records only Armstrong's distinctive practices and numbers. For quotations and the footnote evidence table, see Section V of reference.md.

1. **Write long**: aim for **70+ pages per episode** (a standard one-hour show runs 60 pages; some are squeezed to 50). The reason: shooting a few extra pages of dialogue "returns exponential rewards," and it leaves room to refocus in the edit. **Draft counts: about 15 drafts for the pilot; thereafter at least 30 drafts per episode, usually 50.**
2. **alts**: a day or two before shooting, select 0 to 10–15 lines that might be "funnier or better or truer" and send them to 3–5 writers; narrow 10–20 options down to 5–10 to keep in your pocket, and feed them to the actors after the scripted version has been shot.
3. **freebie take**: after the scripted version, shoot one improvised take. Its value lies not in the occasional line but in what it gives every take: "a spirit of freedom and collaboration."
4. **The wall and the mega-chart** (Prebble): the writers' room has 8–12 people, a mix of British and American, a mix of comedy and tragedy backgrounds, and runs 5–6 hours a day. Large sheets of white paper are headed with **character names**, and ideas are written under the names; the entries that survive go onto the **mega-chart** — characters on the horizontal axis, episodes on the vertical.
5. **Enter the room with a fixed season ending and no path**: before S3 began, only three things were locked (the siblings would ally, Tom must inform, and "how we got there was totally up for grabs").
6. **Start the pitch from the trashy version, then pull it back** (Prebble): open with "This is the trashy version," not "This is shit but –." "**Pitching is the way in which you work out what the show isn't, as well as what it is.**"
7. **Two rules of precedence**: **truth over myth; the funniest may occasionally win**.
8. **The "does two things" cut test**: a scene must accomplish at least two things at once, and **anything with fewer than two is cut**.
9. **Page → set → edit, and the latter two have the right to veto the first**. The universal principle of the cutting room: "the edit generates its own powerful centrifugal force – throwing off material which is further from the show's core questions and concerns." (For the eight pieces of footnote evidence, see the footnote evidence table in Section V of reference.md.)
10. **Leave the final image to the set**: from the S4 introduction, "You won't find the end of the show, as it went to air, written in these scripts. **That we found on the day.**"
11. **Support systems and firefighting**: consultants are assigned by domain (business maneuvering / media / law / New York social circles / politics each have a specialist); firefighting depends on a fixed handful of writers — "to help me write – and to rewrite – when we had an episode that wasn't firing."
12. **What a showrunner is**: "essentially a writer. But a writer empowered to protect the scripts through the rooms where they are conceived and written and rewritten, performed, captured and edited." Running the writers' room = **throwing a party**; the room was in London rather than New York for the sake of "a sense of having been forgotten about"; **2–3 writers are kept on set every day**.
13. **Song-placement discipline**: the scripts wrote many needle-drops, and almost all were cut from the finished show (in S1 only the end of E6 remains); the author found his own music suggestions "far too direct or arch." **A song in the script is only a declaration of intent.**
14. **Characters are rewritten because of actors** (Frank Rich): Gerri was originally a man, "Jerry"; several supporting characters were given more scenes because of their performances; the precondition was "the series' architecture could support it without buckling the overall design." The reverse also holds: an actor can veto what the script implies.

## VI. Ending Engineering

For the complete evidence chain (including the eight steps of the off-screen death, the regression game, the ghost phone, and "Logan's Dinner"), see Section VI of reference.md.

1. **The engine-loses-pressure thesis** — what decides when to end is not the characters. It was not creative exhaustion ("the motor in the room was still running hot") but **"the big plot engine… was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure."**
   > Stress-test the material engine: list the deals, positions, and resources the show runs on, and ask, "How many more seasons can they lose pressure?" Characters' emotions can never be finished; the engine can.
2. **Choose one of three season shapes, by vote of the writers' committee.** On the wall: "one final season of ten episodes, or two of six or eight episodes"; four writers each stated a preference, and Prebble made the call — "**the ten-episode season was the muscular way to go out.**"
   > Before the final season starts, post 2–3 season shapes, let the whole writing staff state a preference and record the reasons, then have one person decide; once the shape is set, it does not change.
3. **3–5 anchors; leave the rest to the process.** S4's four: ① Logan dies early and suddenly; ② the children experience it by phone at a wedding; ③ the GoJo deal **must go through** — "That was the case in the world, so it had to be the case for us." ④ Tom wins.
4. **The eight steps of the off-screen death** (S4E03): an unreliable single source → others turn "saying goodbye" into an action → three "by his ear" monologues (each ≤8 lines, no response) → a staircase of denial → even "telling them" is an act of power → physically separate them from the body → the officialization of death is a series of technical nodes → irony by making each person do what his or her job requires. For the quotations step by step, see Section VI of reference.md.
5. **Omission is the theme** (see D12). The method is to establish the setup, hold the tone, build the world, and then "**step back from the mechanism, let it run**." His definition of satire is one sentence — "Taking a hard look at the world as it is" — and "afflicting the comforted… will go better if you avoid announcing your intention too clearly at the door."
6. **The regression game.** The high point of the series finale is **a regression game with no plot advancement** (S4E10, the three siblings in the kitchen mixing the new king a "meal fit for a king"). Four points: for the first and last time, all three are in the same intention, and the jokes are all **collaborative**; the mother's stinginess supplies the material for impersonation; the one time someone tries to say the tenderness out loud, it is immediately interrupted — **this unfinished pause is the preview of the ending**; the spit is the smallest rehearsal of betrayal.
7. **The ghost phone: retire the unanswered question of the dying wish instead of answering it.** On the terrace in S4E10, Kendall raises a pretend phone to answer, and Shiv picks up the game; then the same prop carries real feeling — "He fucking promised it to me. Promised. When I was seven."
   > Do not reveal "whom the father really meant to give it to." Let the heirs mock the question together, using the same prop first to mock the opponent and then to hand over the truth.
8. **Let the show not know it is ending**: keep the multi-season version alive throughout writing and shooting ("Almost so that the show itself didn't know it was ending"), which avoids both a finale that is "freeze-dried and pre-packaged" and "the metal gears of LA business" crushing every possibility in advance.
9. **Write the most moving passage as a background layer that can be cut.** The S4 appendix "Logan's Dinner" began as "a bit of additional flavour… which might or might not make the cut" on a background television in E10; each guest gets one "party piece."
   > Write it as a background layer first, and it carries no structural risk; use the actors' real skills to get something you could not write. In the finale, play a recording of the dead man's amateur performances: each performance is a character summary, and the reactions of those watching in the room complete the emotional resolution without a line of dialogue.
10. **Open writing is a method, not vagueness.** Quoting Darcey Bussell — what draws people is not perfect execution but "the fear that she might be on the verge of falling"; a showrunner's power should be used to protect the gaps, or else the writing becomes "closed-off, invulnerable… every scene sleek and sealed" — "**the cracks are where the light gets in**."

## VII. The Set-Piece Template: Shared Rules for Ten Big Scenes

For the ten-scene comparison table (space / who sets the rules / mechanism / exit), see Section I of reference.md.

**Nine shared rules**: ① **The scene takes place somewhere "whose turf it is" has an answer** — turf determines who can change the rules. ② **There is always a visible physical constraint**: phones collected / no shoes allowed / no signal / a list / an oath / phones refused. ③ **There is always a forced order of speaking**: "One by one!", a roll call, a witness list, "argue the brief," a speaker list, a board roll-call vote. ④ **There is always an outsider who states the logic of the game** (Greg, Willa, Kira). ⑤ **Give the audience a list before departure**: Tern Haven's Dos & Don'ts (forbidden topics, smart-casual, two drinks maximum, the code name Operation "Let Them Dream Their Beautiful Dream"), Hugo's six A's (Apologize / Accept / Accountability / Advocates for change / Assist victims / Ally with good actors) and **DUMI** (Distance Undermine Minimize Isolate), Gerri's "Plausibly Paper Ignorant" — **the whole scene is watching the list be violated item by item; the suspense costs nothing**. ⑥ **A gift is self-exposure**: in S2E8, Roman's perfunctory recording, Connor's excessive sincerity, Kendall's rap, Roman giving the wrong team (Logan is a Hibs man, not a Hearts man); in S3E7 the three gifts correspond to power, love, and family. ⑦ **Food and drink = an obedience test**: in S3E2, doughnuts dissolve the alliance; in S3E6, Mencken shows up uninvited and offers a Coke ("Anointed with a Coke."); in S3E8, he has his grandson taste the cheese first (a poison test); in S2E10, Tom takes a bite of Logan's chicken and puts it back — **the one who gives food is the benefactor; the one who consumes is the subordinate**. ⑧ **Public spaces for lines, service spaces for the truth**: the plane bathroom in S3E1, the urinal in S3E7, the parking lot in S3E9, the freight elevator in S2E4, backstage in S4E09, the karaoke room in S4E02 ("the Shame Palace"). ⑨ **Afterward there must be a private reckoning coda** (a corridor, a car, a bathroom, a card table, a loading dock), converting public humiliation into specific interpersonal damage.

## VIII. Workflow: Writing a Succession-Style Ensemble Drama

1. **Fix the "simple impulse" first, then build the world.** Armstrong: "the better films and TV shows I've been involved with have at their heart a quite simple impulse around which the more subtle layers are spun" — In the Loop is rage at the Iraq War, Peep Show is male friendship, and **Succession's simple thing is Brexit and Trump**: "what happens when something as important as the flow of information in a democracy hits the reductive brutality of the profit calculation," then watch it "rebound emotionally and psychologically" inside a family considering succession.
2. **Start from real prototypes, then fictionalize.** He resisted fictionalization for five years (believing that a fictional family would "never have the power of a real one"), and finally accepted it because only fiction could produce a "propitiously balanced fictional hybrid media conglomerate," pouring the best material from Murdoch, Redstone, and Maxwell (plus Conrad Black, Hearst, and Beaverbrook) into one man. What the prototypes share: **a refusal to think about death** (both prototypes responded to succession planning with "I'm not dying"), a need for control, manic deal energy, a love of gossip and power relations, ruthlessness in hiring and firing, and **forward motion plus a lack of introspection**.
3. **Settle on a single pitch line.** "Festen-meets-Dallas," "No stars, Dogme 95 camerawork." What a good pitch feels like: "the air thickens… the enthusiasm you are trying to project transforms into an enthusiasm you are actually feeling."
4. **Keep working on the names until they are "lived in"**: "they're a tell-tale sign of whether I'm living inside it."
5. **Write the engine question first, then the season shape.** The engine question must **never be resolvable** (inheritance), and each season answers only one "today version" of it.
6. **Draw the mega-chart**: characters on the horizontal axis, episodes on the vertical, each cell reading "this episode's want → the state at the end of the episode."
7. **Give each episode a container**, with protocol nodes as act breaks; when no ready-made protocol exists, print day one/two/three or timestamps on the page.
8. **Write one core question for each episode, and answer it within the episode.**
9. **Put the seven wants into the container and let them block one another**; check whether each line contaminates another line on the same occasion.
10. **Cut communications**: add a visible physical constraint and give the audience a list to watch being violated item by item.
11. **Lay out the humiliation chain**: Logan → Roman → Greg, Tom → Greg; every scene passes on the same humiliation.
12. **Move the initiator of the climax off the scene** (stuck in traffic, elsewhere, off-screen).
13. **Write the subtext of stage directions**: one note for the real intention, dialogue that says the opposite; keep the maybe and the square brackets; an important decision can have no dialogue at all (S1E7, Kendall accelerates away from the gate; S1E10, after the car crash, he walks toward the water three times and turns back each time).
14. **At least one power maxim per episode, given to a supporting character**; let an outsider translate the epic into a few cents a share. For a list of maxims ready to use, see Section VII of reference.md.
15. **Write long, to 70+ pages**, then ask 3–5 writers for alts, and after the scripted version is shot, do one freebie take.
16. **Use the "does two things" test to cut scenes**; cut the most complete but most detachable subplot first, and put it in the material bank to wait for a structural gap in the next season.
17. **Season finales: fewer scenes, longer**: 3–5 long pillars; the all-hands confrontation in one room comes last, **no act out, only a reckoning**.
18. **A season finale both closes and opens**: every line is settled, but each settlement generates next season's debt (Greg's photocopy, Tom's cruise scandal, Roman's manslaughter, Shiv's dual loyalty); **do not resolve the conflict — redraw the alliance lines**.
19. **Leave the final image to the set.** Write only as far as the character's last action.
20. **Do not announce your intention at the door.** Establish the setup, hold the tone, build the world, then step back and let the mechanism run.

## IX. The "Succession-ize" Checklist

1. Does the script have day markers or timestamps? Can you point to one transfer of information or power within each story day?
2. Does each episode have a container (a public occasion with a fixed protocol)? Do the protocol nodes replace act breaks?
3. Is there a visible physical constraint in the container? Does anyone **refuse** to observe it?
4. What is this episode's core question? Is the syntax "Can X do Y today?" Is the answer given by the end of the episode?
5. What small want does each of the seven main characters have this episode? Is there any pair whose wants do not collide?
6. How does the humiliation chain run? Does each humiliated person pass it on to someone weaker in the next scene?
7. Is there an outsider (the weakest one) who names the logic of the game, and is that line both the joke and the theme?
8. Has one power maxim been given to a supporting character? Does the protagonist fail to listen?
9. Do the stage directions write subtext, or do they write facial expressions? Is there a maybe? Is there one place where the inner life is written as a question?
10. Is there a scene whose important decision has no dialogue at all?
11. Is the initiator of the climax present? What is the reason for the absence?
12. Does the season have one repeatable quantity (a price, a line, a tune) used once at the start and once at the end?
13. Does a family memory have three versions? Does the third party's version trigger a plot turn?
14. Is each character sent back once this season to a place that makes him smaller?
15. Are the opponents more logical than the protagonist? Are the three opponents' reasons three different kinds (fiduciary duty / money / accumulated grievance), and is the final blow a whisper?
16. Has a comic plant been recovered once as evidence ("how would it be read in court")?
17. Is there a reversal accomplished with a single word? Was that trigger word established earlier?
18. Does the heaviest moral information in the whole show stop inside an ellipsis ("Right?" "Right.")?
19. When a relationship reaches the point where there are no new lines, have you rewritten it as a physical action both parties understand?
20. Does the season finale have fewer and longer scenes? Does it redraw the alliance lines rather than resolve the conflict?
21. Is the person who ends up on top the most replaceable person? Is the reason for his appointment spoken by an enemy?
22. Have you announced your intention at the door?
