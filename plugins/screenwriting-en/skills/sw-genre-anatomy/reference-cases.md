# Genre Anatomy: Film-by-Film Beat Breakdowns

> To be used with SKILL.md and reference.md. For each genre it takes one to three works that the book breaks down beat by beat, listing what is done at each beat, so that you can compare "how someone else hit this beat" against your own draft. All from John Truby, *The Anatomy of Genres* (2022); film titles are kept in their original English.

---

## I. Horror

### Alien
- Ghost: the company orders the creature brought back through the android Ash; MOTHER displays "Crew expendable"; the past crime is the company's license to kill.
- Hero as Victim: the ordinary-good-person opening.
- Three-stage attack: Kane looks at the egg and is facehugged; the facehugger seems dead and Kane recovers; at the dinner table the chest bursts (fear → false alarm → real scare).
- Concealed attacks: the opponent grows and forms a hierarchy; Ash is a fake ally.
- Cyclone: the ending's six steps in rapid fire — the self-destruct cannot be stopped; the Nostromo explodes as the escape pod leaves; the alien is found in the corner; she puts on the spacesuit, takes up the harpoon, and opens the airlock; the alien grabs the door and is shot; she climbs back along the line to the thruster, ignites it, and blasts the alien into space.
- Battle—Safe Haven: the escape pod is both a cocoon and possibly a coffin.

### Psycho
- Protagonist replacement: Norman kills the first protagonist, Marion, early on and becomes the central figure with an unspeakable desire.
- Ghost: ten years earlier, Norman killed his mother and her lover because his mother had "abandoned" him; he stole back the corpse, speaks for his mother, and gives half his life to her; when he is attracted to a woman, the "mother" side goes mad and kills.
- Double: the fractured mind lives in the killer; write Norman as gentle and vulnerable first, and the horror doubles when the truth comes out. The skeleton in the cellar is the number-one example of "the crime of the parents crawling up."

### Get Out
- Story World: writes class and race at once; the modern plantation owner uses medical technology to keep Black people permanently enslaved.
- Hero as Victim: Chris visits his white girlfriend's country estate, and her father keeps declaring his love for Obama.
- Machine Horror: the Black servants are like zombies or Stepford wives.
- Reveal / Monster becomes the hero: the girlfriend is only bait; the real monster is the white father.

## II. Action

### Mad Max: Fury Road
- Desire: Max first wants to escape the Citadel; Furiosa flees with the harem toward the "Green Place," finds it a dead end, and turns back to seize the water supply. The structure is a straight line out to a dead end and a straight line back.
- Opponent: Immortan Joe, who uses women as breeding machines, cuts off the water to torture people, and sends a high-speed kill convoy. Four-corner diagram: Max / Immortan Joe and the War Boys / Furiosa / Nux, People Eater, Bullet Farmer.
- Improvisation: Max pierces Furiosa's chest cavity and rigs a tube and needle to transfuse his own blood.
- Battle: the convoy races back to the Citadel, the vortex point at the canyon mouth; the death sequence is also a vortex, minor members dying first, and Furiosa kills Joe by locking his mask to the wheels.
- Farewell: Max walks away like Ethan Edwards, with a nod from Furiosa before he goes; Furiosa stays on as leader.

### Die Hard
- Story World: the fortress arena.
- Strategy vs. Tactics: McClane is a tactical genius, and can also construct a series of actions to defeat the terrorists and save his wife and the hostages.
- Improvisation: starts with no weapon and uses whatever is at hand as a tool; under helicopter fire, he ties a fire hose around his waist and leaps, swinging into a lower window.
- Four-corner diagram: John / Hans / Holly-Ellis / Karl.

### Rocky (Sports)
- Training: punching sides of beef in the meat plant; Apollo's trainer Tony grows more worried the more he watches, and the audience realizes that fighting this "bum" may be Creed's biggest mistake.
- Sports Self-Revelation: reading the score, he calls for Adrian, does not care whether he wins, and knows he is not a bum.

### Raiders of the Lost Ark
- Defining Crisis: the booby traps, Satipo's betrayal, the boulder, Belloq taking the idol, jumping into the river and onto the plane; a string of cliff-hangers defines Indy's courage, resourcefulness, athleticism, and whip.

## III. Myth

### The Lord of the Rings
- Story World: Middle Earth is a modern cosmology of Great Britain; land is forest, river, and mountain; the man-made is castle and village; technology is animals and magic.
- Value opposition: good = care for living things, with sacrifice as the highest love; evil = the will to power, domination of living things, obsession with the ring.
- Subworlds: three utopias (the Shire, Rivendell, Lothlorien) against Mordor and Isengard; Moria is the "descent to the underworld."
- Technology: medieval — beacon fires on the peaks, swords, horses, flying reptiles, catapults.
- Two protagonists each twist one archetype: Frodo is the small man twisting the warrior type, his weakness being small, weak, and afraid; Aragorn is the dispossessed warrior-king, his weakness not believing he deserves to be king.
- Talisman: Anduril is broken and must be reforged; only the rightful heir can wield it.

### The Wizard of Oz (Female Myth)
- Two Myths, two arenas: the Kansas farm and Oz, enclosed by the Deadly Desert.
- Female Myth markers: a country of women (Auntie Em runs the farm, Miss Gulch is the opponent), with Glinda the strongest and most positive character.
- Weakness-Need: she thinks her family does not care; the true need is to go home and love her family. Talisman: the ruby slippers, power unknown. Desire: first over the rainbow, then after landing changed to I want to go home.
- Allies: the three allies are each looking for something they already have (the Scarecrow wants a brain but is the best problem-solver); the Wizard is a humbug, yet uses the test "bring back the witch's broom" to make Dorothy prove herself.
- Opponent: the witch appears only at the beginning and the end, and multiple threats in the middle make up for it. Underworld: walking the corridor to see the Wizard is the gauntlet; being imprisoned and the hourglass turning over is the visit to death and the apparent defeat.
- Battle: no fight; a bucket of water accidentally melts the witch. Self-Revelation purely personal: "There's no place like home."

### Avatar (Male Myth turned Female Myth, Ecological Myth)
- Flaws: unobtanium is a MacGuffin; the Colonel and Selfridge have no complexity.
- Four rebirths: taking his twin brother's place to enlist; the avatar "born" underwater in a womb; the cultural rebirth of initiation into the tribe (the overhead shot of the whole tribe in a circle is the visual expression of combine and grow); the fourth rebirth at the Tree of Souls, followed by marriage.
- Talisman changed from a male object to a female one: Neytiri is about to shoot her arrow when the seed of the sacred tree lands on Jake. "The arrow… is who Jake is now. The seed is who he will become."
- Self-Revelation: public = calling the tribes to unite; cosmic = communion with Eywa.

### Black Panther (Origin Story)
- At once the Origin Story of a Black superhero and the Creation Story of Wakanda; its success lies in the synchronized moral growth of the hero and the nation.
- Flaw: accepting Wakanda's policy of secrecy toward the world. Battle: defeats Killmonger in the vibranium mine. cosmic revelation: publicly reveals Wakanda's true identity at the United Nations.

## IV. Memoir and Coming-of-Age

### The Liars' Club (Mary Karr)
- Frame: Texas at seven, Colorado at nine, returning to Texas seventeen years later to see her dying father. The author's comment: the frame ends too early, and the last two sections lose some of their power.
- Story World: the mother's belief system that she "must be a successful suburban housewife."
- Ghost: she is molested twice, but the parents' own ghosts matter more (the mother's secret serial marriages and two children who disown her; the father's World War II and alcoholism).
- Double Desire: in the present, to understand what happened to her parents; in the past, to make her mother love this family.
- Plan 1/2/3: be a good girl and collect Green Stamps; go back to Texas with Lecia to live with their father; as an adult, confront her mother.
- Battle: the wedding ring in the attic, the confrontation, the mother admitting to the serial marriages and the two children.
- Self-Revelation 1/2: at the pool table she knows who she is; the mother's knot absolves them both, and the "black crimes" were only a myth pieced together from fear.
- Moral Decision: forgive her mother and herself, no longer allow secrecy, and leave her hometown.

### Into Thin Air (Jon Krakauer)
- Storyteller Structure: in medias res, just after summiting, "The biggest problem with climbing Everest isn't getting to the top, it's getting back down."
- Story World: the mountaineering culture of "summit no matter what"; the fact that he is writing a report adds pressure.
- Moral need: the journalist's presence makes others make bad judgments in pursuit of favorable coverage.
- Plan: turn back by two o'clock; neither guide keeps to it.
- Self-Revelation 1/2: he should have stepped forward and led; after writing it he realizes that his role as a journalist may have encouraged the guides to break the rules.
- Moral Decision: write the book and take part of the responsibility; understanding rather than forgiveness for the guides; the heaviest criticism kept for himself.

### Cinema Paradiso (Personal Myth + Künstlerroman)
- Three techniques: the rise and fall of the protagonist, the cinema, and the town match one another; put all three into the stage of the town's modernization and decline; the storyteller structure spans fifty years.

### The Graduate (Coming-of-Age Frame)
- The false trap (parental expectations, school, "plastics"), discovering that he is actually free, bursting into the wedding and taking the girl; in the back seat of the bus the ending says "we are responsible for this," and the real struggle has only just begun.

## V. Science Fiction

### Star Wars (original trilogy)
- Weakness-Need: Luke is an impulsive boy who must acquire self-discipline.
- Desire / vortex: the opening crawl explains that Leia carries the Death Star plans; the vortex point is firing the missile into the two-meter-wide exhaust port.
- Opponent: Vader and the Emperor; the hierarchy of opponents keeps bringing the opponent back throughout the journey.
- Plot—Subworlds: the desert planet Tatooine establishes the weakness; the Empire re-establishes it on the ice planet Hoth, then the film crosscuts between the Dagobah swamp (training) and the cloud city of Bespin.
- Reveal: Luke turns off the targeting computer and shoots by the Force. Battle: the trench run's three obstacles, "There is no tighter convergence of space and time than this."

### Arrival (Female Myth/SF)
- Shape: a circular Vortex Street in both space and time. The opening is Louise with her daughter who dies young; after deciphering the heptapods' language she can project time forward, and her daughter is not in the past but in the future.
- Battle reversal: difference is handled without physical conflict; Louise prevents the clash that would have set off a world war.
- Self-Revelation: personal and global at once, "a woman's ability to see holistically instead of divisively is matched by the circular story structure."

### The Hunger Games (Myth + SF + Horror)
- The Myth source is Theseus and the Minotaur, changed into twenty-four teenagers fighting until only one is left.
- SF layer: pushes American capitalism to its logical extreme; the players are pawns of a large corporation.
- World-building order: build the total stage first (the totalitarian society); then the fundamental contrast (Capitol vs. District 12); then, inside the macro stage, the walled arena, with its pressure-cooker effect.
- Strongest technique: turns the Theseus setup into the most terrifying prisoner's dilemma in the history of story.

### Nineteen Eighty-Four and Brave New World
- Both are modern slave states of the fourth stage. Brave New World controls by keeping people perpetually happy (Soma, promiscuity); Nineteen Eighty-Four by surveillance, Newspeak, and thoughtcrime. When Winston faces the rat cage he betrays Julia, and she betrays him — the classic case of the prisoner's dilemma.

## VI. Crime

### The Dark Knight
- Inciting Event / Ingenious Crime: the Joker robs the bank, has the robbers kill one another, and drives off in a school bus himself, "whatever doesn't kill you makes you stranger."
- Opponent: the Joker is the Dark Philosopher; the whole film is a chain of increasingly difficult moral dilemmas he constructs.
- Obsessive Cop: in the interrogation room Batman crosses the line, and the Joker says "You … complete … me."
- Apparent Defeat: the Joker gives the wrong address, and Rachel is blown up.
- Gate-Gauntlet: the two-boat prisoner's dilemma; the author thinks the ending, in which neither boat presses the button, violates the human reality established by the whole film.
- Self-Revelation (mixed version): Batman has Dent die as a hero and takes the blame himself; society regains its certainty through a lie.

### The Usual Suspects
- The hero is the master criminal Verbal, essentially a master storyteller. Four levels of deception: improvising the story from the bulletin board; posing as an ally to fool Keaton, Kujan, and the audience; pretending to be weak and stupid; inventing the opponent Keyser Söze.
- Four revelations at the end: the whole story was invented; he is not the weak mouse; the opponent is his invention; he is Söze. The author calls it the greatest reveal in the history of Crime.

### In Bruges (Crime Black Comedy)
- Code established: the hitman's code is that taking the money gives you the right to settle the account, but there must be no collateral damage; killing a child is the greatest debt. Harry says early on: "if I had killed a little kid … I'd've stuck the gun in me mouth. On the fucking spot."
- Attack by Ally: Ken to Harry, "The boy has the capacity to change."
- Ken's decision 1: on the tower he pushes the gun toward Harry; Harry only shoots him in the leg, and the account is squared.
- Harry's decision 1: hearing that Ray is downstairs, he immediately breaks the deal to kill Ray and hits Ken in the neck by mistake.
- Ken's decision 2: he jumps from the tower to his death, saying "Harry's here. Take my gun." The gun breaks in the fall.
- Harry's final decision: mistakenly believing he has killed the dwarf actor dressed as a schoolboy — "Ah. I see." — and by his own code puts the gun in his mouth, "You've got to stick to your principles." Poetic justice: McDonagh precisely reproduces the moral ambiguity of Ray's accidental killing of the boy.

### Breaking Bad (season-level moral sequence)
- The ledger: what Walt believes he is owed (his share of the company cheated away from him, being a genius who deserves to be rich, having cancer so he has the right to cook to support his family); what Walt owes (high-purity meth harming people, Combo killed, letting Jesse's girlfriend die, killing countless people).
- The premise is the change: "Mr. Chips into Scarface," from nebbish to Heisenberg.
- Season by season, the moral decisions are arranged in a negative sequence: from killing with a reason, to watching Jesse's girlfriend choke, to premeditated mass murder. Mastras: "How far can we push this guy toward darkness and not lose the audience?"

## VII. Comedy

### Groundhog Day
- Hero: a narcissistic weatherman of the Trickster type.
- Nightmare structure: the whole film is a repeating nightmare, a new version every day.
- Plan—Scam: uses information from a previous date (Rita wants to live in the mountains, studied French poetry) to fake being simpatico; each time the narcissism ruins it; the book quotes the same exchange twice — the first time he blurts "What a waste of time!" and the second time he recites French poetry and answers "Oui."
- Self-Revelation: the best self comes from helping others; the curse lifts, and he is more successful than when he was selfish.

### Little Miss Sunshine (Comic Myth)
- False goal: Olive wants to win the beauty crown, structurally the same as Don Quixote chasing Dulcinea.
- Three techniques: tell the audience the destination at the start (going to the pageant) and keep it a single line; take the family on the road, with the main opposition in the father; the family's political system is "an incompetent dictatorship," and when it is overthrown the family is renewed.
- Character types: the father is the Know-It-All, the pageant director is the Aristocrat. Nightmare: the van breaks down and the whole family pushes it. The final battle is a battle of chaos.

### Tootsie
- Demonstration of the four rules of disguise: what the chauvinist least wants to wear is a dress; taking it off means losing the soap-opera job; the audience is in on it the whole time.
- The battle is the big unmasking: admitting on live TV that he is a man. Types of ultimate nightmare: fear of the opposite sex, getting caught cheating.

### Seinfeld (Satire)
- "the karma show. Cosmic justice always happens, but in a very roundabout way."
- Four corners by comic drop and gender: Jerry the machine against George, Elaine, and Kramer the children; "all four are equal characters."
- Plot: each of the four drives a line, three or four predicaments cross in every episode, and at the end they are unexpectedly tied together. "The Marine Biologist": George claims to be a marine biologist, Kramer practices golf on the beach, and a Titleist is pulled out of a whale's blowhole.

## VIII. Western

### Butch Cassidy and the Sundance Kid (Anti-Western, Goldman)
- Two opening scenes: Butch cases the bank and the iron bars and gate clang shut, "That in a nutshell is the entire movie"; Sundance is accused of cheating at cards, and Butch's "I can't help you, Sundance" makes the accuser back down; Butch laughs and says "Like I said, over the hill."
- Scene strategy: set up a false crisis so the audience thinks death is certain; the crisis defines character in an instant; the two bicker like an old married couple in the face of death; the scene funnels to a "thematic vortex point" line.
- Opponent: E. H. Harriman, who never appears, hires an all-star posse.
- Apparent Victory: Etta's monologue "I won't watch you die."
- No Self-Revelation: Bledsoe says it for them, "Your times is over and you're gonna die bloody," and they cannot hear him.
- Final scene: the small room shrinks and shrinks, and this time the crisis is real; the audience knows the Bolivian army is on the roof and the characters do not; "Good. For a second there, I thought we were in trouble." Freeze frame, three volleys.

### McCabe & Mrs. Miller
- Story World: the wet, green, muddy mountains of the Northwest; the technological symbol is the vacuum cleaner bought for the brothel.
- Hero: a gambler opens a brothel and builds the country; the townspeople think he is a famous gunfighter but have no proof; he believes the lawyer's empty talk of the American dream.
- Battle: he cannot shoot, and uses a derringer hidden in his sleeve; no one sees in the heavy snow, and the townspeople are saving a church no one attends. The book says the film plays out the entire modernization process from wilderness to oppressive city in a single story.

### The Wild Bunch
- Values: Pike, "If they move, kill 'em."
- Apparent Victory: the green utopian village of Agua Verde.
- Battle—Massacre: the four survivors demand Angel; after Mapache cuts his throat they seize machine guns and rake the entire army; their nobility is precisely the reason for their destruction.

### Classic Westerns in Brief
- Shane: reluctantly uses his gun to make the wilderness safe for family life, then leaves as the eternally lonely man; "A man has to be what he is."
- The Searchers: Ethan stands in the doorway with the wilderness behind him, turns, and walks away.
- Once Upon a Time in the West: puts the showdown at the opening, "Looks like we're shy one horse." "You brought two too many."
- 3:10 to Yuma: more guns and fewer allies until he goes to his death alone; the book judges it to be false moral accounting.

## IX. Gangster

### The Godfather
- Structure: Gangster + Myth + fairy tale, the youngest of three brothers becomes king: Sonny the enforcer and Tom the negotiator both fail, while Michael has both toughness and smarts; it tells "the birth of a new godfather."
- Five fake allies: the old godfather's driver; the two Sicilian bodyguards, whose car bomb kills his wife by mistake; Tessio going over to Barzini; Carlo setting up Sonny's death.
- Battle reversal: during the baptism he orders his men to kill the heads of the five families, Tessio, and Carlo.
- No Self-Revelation: Connie accuses him, Michael denies it to Kay, and the door closes in Kay's face; the revelation is transferred to Kay.

### The Godfather Part II
- Social-fractal crosscutting: Vito builds a business in the neighborhood from 1917 to 1925, set against Michael expanding the empire internationally in 1958–1959.
- Story World (advanced version): cutting the cake in Cuba, Roth says "real partnership with the government… we're bigger than U.S. Steel."
- Inciting Event: Vito steals a carpet. Drive: Michael buys a senator.

### Goodfellas
- Rise and Fall in three sections: young Henry learns the system's rules and passes his initiation; the voice-over tells how the restaurant is sucked dry by the mob "as real vampires," "Business bad? Fuck you, pay me."; the long take from the back door through the kitchen to the front table, with twenty dollars pressed into every hand along the way.
- Battle reversal: testifying in court. No Self-Revelation (Black Comedy technique): "I'm an average nobody … like a schnook."

### The Great Gatsby (Eastern)
- The whole character web is a set of variations on fake vs. real; Nick is the observer hero and Gatsby's enabler.
- Self as Buyer: buying Daisy means buying "the ability to become anyone"; "Her voice is full of money."
- Invisible Mouth: the valley of the ashes.
- Battle: Gatsby's death is a class war of the rich (Daisy runs over Myrtle, Gatsby takes the blame, Tom points the finger, George shoots, and Tom and Daisy go on vacation in Europe).

### Mad Men
- Designing principle: tracking the characters' slow liberation in the 1960s; creed: Whatever sells wins; Men rule women at work and lie to them at home.
- Hero: Don stole a name and an identity, and his business is precisely "remaking" — selling the American dream itself, which makes him King of America.
- Opponent: each season he resists a large corporate acquisition. Plan: Kodak "Carousel." Moral Argument: Lucky Strike "slow roasted"; Joan trades for a Jaguar; Dow napalm.
- Self-Revelation: after meditating at Esalen he comes up with Coke "Hilltop"; the counterculture is absorbed by consumer culture, which the author calls "may be the best final moment in television history."

## X. Fantasy

### Alice in Wonderland (called in the book "the greatest Fantasy ever written")
- Story World: the underworld is "not the land of death. It's the land where causation and process are upside down, where easy thinking goes to die."
- Weakness-Need: strict logic and manners, constantly asked "Who are you?"; the need is to be open to surreal logic.
- Character Web: all illogical creatures, striking directly at the obsession with "everything in its proper place."
- World direction: from a chaos of broken connections to the Queen's tyranny of instant beheading. Battle: Alice against the Queen and the guards.

### Mary Poppins (Traveling Angel)
- Seven-step division: the revelation goes to the main opponent, the rigid father; he learns fun, play, living with style, and ends the film flying a kite in the park. The battle is the father standing up to the bank chairman.
- Super Magical Moment: the ceiling tea party and the rooftop chimney-sweep dance.

### It's a Wonderful Life (Fantasy Political Epic)
- Changes the causal starting point (George was never born), and Clarence builds another present. Plan (negative world): after his failed suicide, George enters Pottersville.
- Two leaders: George is the altruistic capitalist, Potter the pure capitalist; Bedford Falls is a guilt culture, Pottersville a consumer and fear culture.
- Minor characters contrasted one by one: Ernie (cheerful driver vs. abandoned by his wife); Violet (kind woman vs. prostitute); Gower (druggist vs. a drunk who spent twenty years in prison); Harry (war hero vs. dead young); mother (loving vs. bitter); Mary (lover and mother vs. timid old maid).
- Definition of utopia: no minor character is absolutely free or successful, but each is the best and freest version of themselves within their abilities and position.

### Pleasantville (Paradise on Earth)
- Structure: "from surface utopia to true utopia." Passageway: the television and the magic remote.
- Learning the rules on entry: a seemingly perfect black-and-white suburb, rigid conformity, men rule. Trigger: Mary Sue gives Skip his first taste of sex, and he sees color for the first time.
- Battle: in the courtroom Bud makes the Mayor see his own emotions and defeats him.

### Game of Thrones (tournament structure)
- Premise: "The Sopranos in Middle Earth"; what is compared is the way of leadership, governance, and war.
- The four great houses form the base four corners (Stark / Lannister / Baratheon / Targaryen), with more corners inside each house, narrowing after a chain of strategic mistakes.
- Four types of leadership in the final: Cersei (the consistent tyrant), Daenerys (supported by the common people but turning despotic), Sansa (the best combination of leadership skills), Jon Snow. The ending picks Bran, who never entered the contest; the author calls it "like picking the killer in a whodunit based on the one person who was incapable of committing the crime."

## XI. Detective and Thriller

### Vertigo (Cosmic Detective)
- Opponent's Plan: Elster learns that Scottie has vertigo and hires Madeleine to pose as his wife and draw out his love; she fakes suicide and runs up the tower, Scottie cannot follow, and Elster throws his real wife from the top.
- Final Reveal: Judy wears the necklace from the portrait.
- Self-Revelation: deeply negative — he discovers that he cannot truly love, and bears part of the responsibility for the death of the person he loved.
- Poetic Justice reversal: Scottie drags her back up the tower, she recoils and falls to her death; the real murderer goes free.

### Murder on the Orient Express
- Revelation Sequence: the AISY ARMS paper → twelve stab wounds → the English-only victim speaking "in French" → the woman in the white dressing gown → Hector's father was the prosecutor in the Armstrong case → the princess's connection to the Armstrong family → Hildegarde knew the dead maid → the conductor's uniform in the trunk.
- Questioning: have Andrenyi sign and ask whether she has been to America; scold Miss Debenham in front of the colonel to make him rush in.
- Battle of Stories: two explanations — a Mafia enemy or the twelve passengers acting together; the detective takes the higher moral path.
- Least Likely Killer and Poetic Justice: twelve stabs correspond to a twelve-person jury.

### L.A. Confidential
- fake utopia: the opening voice-over montage exposes the Consumer/Fear Culture beneath the city's surface.
- Three flawed detectives: Ed is self-righteous, Bud is quick-tempered and soft on women in trouble, Jack will do anything for fame and fortune.
- Final Reveal: "Rolo Tomasi" is the name Ed gives "the man who killed his father and got away with it"; when Smith asks about the name, Ed knows he killed Jack.
- Poetic Justice: at the end Ed shoots Smith in the back and uses the standard Smith set out at the beginning to prove he deserves to be a detective.

### The Conversation (transcendent Thriller)
- Two-line reveal: "He'd kill us if he had the chance" on the recording → the violence and scream in the hotel room → discovering that Ann is not dead → reinterpreting the same line: Ann and Mark killed the Director.
- Flashback Memory: the whole case depends on correctly hearing the stress in one line, and it is too late. Poetic Justice is applied to the investigator himself.

### Rear Window (Thriller Battle)
- Jeffries, with his leg in a cast, blinds Thorwald again and again with the flashbulb; pushed out the window, he hangs by his fingertips until the police arrive and catch him. Two in One: Jeff, who refused to marry Lisa, promises to marry her after they solve the case together.

## XII. Love

### Silver Linings Playbook
- Ghost: Pat has just left the mental hospital and is still obsessed with his ex-wife, who has taken out a restraining order against him.
- Mutual Need: Pat is fixated on the old relationship, Tiffany uses sex in place of intimacy; they argue while jogging, each jabbing the other ("Your husband's dead!" / "Where's your wife?").
- Meet-cute reversal: "I'm not flirting with you… How'd Tommy die?"
- Moral Decline: Tiffany forges Nikki's reply.

### 500 Days of Summer
- Gaze: he sees Summer for the first time at a staff meeting. Meet-cute: The Smiths in the elevator, "Holy shit."
- Apparent Victory: before their first time, Tom walks through the city and strangers congratulate him. New Equilibrium: Farewell.

### When Harry Met Sally
- Riffing: arguing about the ending of Casablanca on the drive across America.
- First Dance + Returning to the "No": a slow dance on New Year's Eve; with ten seconds to go, Harry says he needs some air.
- First Kiss → First Sex: Sally cries over her breakup and asks Harry to hold her; the kiss dissolves to a naked, laughing Sally and a stunned Harry.

### Heartbreaker
- Scam: Alex's profession is breaking up mismatched couples; he is hired to pose as a bodyguard to stop Juliette from marrying an Englishman; first he has his assistant steal her car, then "heroically" recovers it. The scam lasts to the end.
- Battle of Words: a closed disco restages Dirty Dancing in full, postponing the first date and the first dance to the climax.

### The Philadelphia Story (four-corner opposition, Comedy of Remarriage)
- Tracy Lord, proud moralist / Dexter, worldly ex-husband / Mike, blunt reporter (passionate talk: "You're lit from within, Tracy.") / George, priggish social-climbing fiancé.

### Pride and Prejudice (four marriages in contrast)
- Jane and Bingley (the old contract, but they will be happy); Charlotte and Collins (the old contract at its extreme); Kitty and Wickham (built on deception); Elizabeth and Darcy (formally the old contract, in substance the closest to the new contract).
