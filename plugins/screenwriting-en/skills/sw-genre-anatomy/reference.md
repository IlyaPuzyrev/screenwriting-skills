## II. Horror

### Complete Beat Table

| # | Beat | Gloss | Key points | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ghost—Sins of the Past | Ghost: the sins of the past | The most important beat; a sin of the past connects through the hero's weakness to a sin in the future; "the debt must be paid" | Elm Street: the parents burn Freddy to death; Alien: the company's "Crew expendable"; Psycho: the murder of the mother |
| 2 | Story World—Haunted House and Closed Society | The haunted house and the closed society | The haunted house externalizes the weakness; a rotting upper class feeds on ordinary people; the American version hides beneath the shell of a small town; the cellar buries the crime, the attic confines the madness; it can expand into a ghost town | Usher; the modern plantation of Get Out; Les Revenants |
| 3 | The Monster Attacks | The monster attacks | Extremely powerful and a driver of the action; it strikes from inside and outside, plunging the story into the pressure cooker almost at once | General rule |
| 4 | Hero as Victim | The hero as victim | A fall from a high place (Frankenstein) or an ordinary good person who stumbles in (Jaws, The Exorcist) | In the latter case, the opening must first make the character intensely human |
| 5 | Weakness-Need 1—Slavery of Mind | Slavery of the mind | Binary thinking is the fundamental defect of the mind; fundamental fears and unsociable desires; the hero is degraded to the animal (the body, loss of control) or the machine (loss of identity) | Jekyll/Hyde; Dracula is the animal, Body Snatchers the machine |
| 6 | Weakness-Need 2—Shame and Guilt | Shame and guilt | Shame is measured against public standards, guilt against private obligations; atoning for a crime that is not one's own is a losing battle | General rule |
| 7 | Desire—Defeat the Monster, Defeat Death | Defeat the monster, defeat death | The starting point is flight; the monster attacks because the hero hides a forbidden desire | In Psycho, Norman replaces Marion at the center |
| 8 | Opponent—the Monster | The extreme Other | A nonhuman outsider invades the community; at the same time it is the double | See Techniques |
| 9 | Ally—the Rational Skeptic | The rational skeptic | Doubts on the audience's behalf and offers a natural explanation for the supernatural | The scientist in Dracula; the young man with a record in Elm Street |
| 10 | Crossing the Barrier to the Forbidden | Crossing the barrier | An outer wall + an inner taboo, "It always fails"; crossing the line starts a slippery slope; the Law of Necessary Cost of Living | Pet Sematary, cat → son; the full price in Dracula |
| 11 | Plan—Reactive | Reactive plan | Flight; the opponent is also inside the mind, so there is no escape | Halloween, Aliens |
| 12 | Drive—the Monster Attacks Escalate | The attacks escalate | "pressure, pressure, pressure"; brief relief is followed by a harder blow | See Techniques |
| 13 | Battle—Safe Haven | Safe haven | A place that seems safe; "The hero must attack the monster in herself" | The escape pod in Alien: "cocoon or coffin" |
| 14 | No Self-Revelation | No revelation | Shared with Gangster, Black Comedy, and the Anti-Western | General rule |
| 15 | The Double Ending—Eternal Recurrence | The double ending | The monster returns after apparent death; the negative version of Nietzsche's eternal recurrence | Carrie, Elm Street, and the comic version in Groundhog Day |

### Techniques

- **The Double**: use a doppelgänger to reveal the hero's deeper self in another character; "the hero learns through the opponent." Jekyll/Hyde splits the mind into two personalities but "makes for a thin plot"; Psycho first writes Norman as gentle and vulnerable, so the horror doubles when his true face is revealed.
- **The order of the monster's attacks**: fear → false alarm → real scare (the facehugger, the false death, and the chestburster in Alien).
- **Increasing the Pressure Through Deception**: confinement, no way to call for help, injury, and deception on top; "keep as much of the opponent's real power and ability hidden from the hero and the audience as you can."
- **Methods of the Hidden Attack**: a house with many rooms; an opponent that multiplies (Aliens) or grows (Alien); one that returns from the dead (Friday the 13th); a fake ally (Ash).
- **The Horror Reveal**: identify the hero's greatest fear and use it repeatedly in different forms, each more extreme than the last.
- **The Cyclone Effect**: the nightmare accelerates toward the battle (the six-step barrage at the end of Alien).
- **The Monster Becomes the Hero** (a transcendent route): "a structural flip between human and inhuman, between hero and monster"; "We are the monster."

### Subgenres
The book lists names only: Ghost Story (A Christmas Carol turns "a ghost in space" into "a ghost in time"), Vampire, Werewolf, Slasher, Occult, Paranormal, Gothic, Horror/Myth, Horror/SF Epic, Superhero, Savior, Comedy.

### Path to Transcendence 1: Horror/Myth = Religion (Religion Story Beats)

| Beat | Key points |
|---|---|
| Story World—the Underworld and the Afterlife | Heaven and hell are story worlds located in space and time, or a testing ground on the road to heaven |
| Hero—Avatar of the Reader | The reader is the true hero |
| Desire—Salvation and Immortality | "I want to live forever"; the path is a moral code |
| Opponent—the Devil-Satan-Iblis | The temptation to do evil and the experience of it; "the measure of man's inability to live the ideal" |
| Self-Revelation—Epiphany | "An epiphany is a rebirth; it's the realization of immortality while alive." |

Christian Story Beats: from Christmas (the birth of a new hero, Myth) to Easter (death and rebirth, Horror); a composite hero; the moral code condensed in the Beatitudes; public and cosmic revelation at birth and death respectively; the direction of sacrifice is reversed, as God sacrifices a god to humanity.

### Path to Transcendence 2: Horror/Science Fiction Epic (Four Types)

| Type | Approach | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Re-create a human being | "not about creating life, but about creating a human being"; the tyranny of the father-as-god | Frankenstein, The Shape of Water, Get Out |
| Human from animal | The master scientist's weakness grows out of his strength; the monster becomes the hero; betrayal of the father | Rise of the Planet of the Apes |
| Human from machine | Sex and love added on top of the Turing test; the range of the human includes the willingness to murder | Ex Machina |
| Higher human, new species | "Advanced Horror is about constructing a person. SF is about constructing a society. The ultimate Horror/SF Epic is about creating a new species." | Westworld |

The standard: "To reach its full potential, Horror must become as big as possible, by having social, political, and universal ramifications."

### Techniques
- **Four-Point Opposition and Hierarchy of Opponents**: four reasons Action loses plot (back-and-forth conflict hits the same beat; the hero moves at full speed and has no time to turn; set pieces freeze the plot; the audience knows everything will be settled in the final battle). The book provides four diagrams of four-corner opposition: Fury Road (Max / Immortan Joe and the War Boys / Furiosa / Nux, People Eater, Bullet Farmer); Die Hard (John / Hans / Holly-Ellis / Karl); Casino Royale (Bond / Le Chiffre / Vesper / Carlos-Dimitrios-White); The Hunt for Red October (Ryan / Ramius / Tupolev / Padorin).
- **Strategy vs. Tactics**: tactics win a battle and mean acting efficiently; strategy wins the war and means acting effectively. McClane is a tactical genius who can also build a sequence; Maximus makes the gladiators into a team.
- **#1 Combat Technique: Deception**: "Successful combat always involves maximum deception."
- **The improvisation paradigm of the Suicide Mission**: a sentry falls ill, the replacement is unpredictable, and the mission cannot be postponed.

### Subgenres
Action Epic/War; Suicide Mission (Seven Samurai, Dirty Dozen, Saving Private Ryan); Adventure (not developed); Buddy Story (Action + Love + Comedy, ending with a bickering renewal, "marriage without the pain"); Sports; Caper.

### Path to Transcendence 1: Action Epic / War Story (11 Beats)
Writing notes: find a closed arena ("a journey lets pressure escape"); if travel is unavoidable, reveal the overall vortex point at the start; set the story at a historical turning point that shaped the nation; give the characters a key role in nation-building; add texture through social change.

| # | Beat | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The World of War | The battle is the vortex point of land, people, and technology; "bullets and bodies" |
| 2 | Technology of Weapons | Weapons drive changes in strategy and tactics |
| 3 | Action Epic Hero | Has a sense of humor; the mission is a burden he does not want |
| 4 | Desire | A concrete physical endpoint that leads to the great battle |
| 5 | Opposition | The opponent must be human and have defensible values; "the evil opponent is always a mistake… a black box of badness" |
| 6 | Character Web—Chain of Command | Horizontally, army against army; vertically, the chain of command; write from either the commander's or the soldier's point of view |
| 7 | Collecting the Allies—Selling the War | A romantic sales pitch: "it's fake immortality. The man is dead." |
| 8 | Game Plan—Tactics vs. Strategy | Attack vs. defense, fighting vs. moving, the mass army vs. the point of attack |
| 9 | Battle—Grinding Slaughter | A long string of battles, ending with a great decisive battle that one's own side almost always wins |
| 10 | Self-Revelation—Humanity Is Hopeless | The darkest revelation in any form |
| 11 | Moral Decision | Beau geste: duty vs. the personal; the alternative version chooses the self |

### Path to Transcendence 2: Caper and Sports
Caper has no separate beat set: "How can someone defeat a 'real' world that is closed, hierarchical, and lawful?"; "The creative brilliance of the leader is the real object of interest"; The Thomas Crown Affair connects Caper to Love.

Sports, 11 beats: Story World—the Vortex ("stretching the field") → Warrior Hero at Play—Who's Best → Weakness-Need—the Need to Prove Greatness → Desire—To Score and Therefore to Win → Character Web—Individual vs. Team Sports → Opponent—Nemesis ("each player should be the nemesis of the other") → Game Plan—the Four-Corner Strategy (attack the weakness, force the extreme corner, "the ball moves faster than the runner") → Moral Argument—Cheating → Battle—Last-Second Victory → Self-Revelation—Winner, Loser, and Higher Victory → New Equilibrium—Long-Lasting Truth (The Hustler: "anything can be great… bricklaying can be great, if a guy knows"). Coaching principle: "A good coach teaches you how to play; a great coach teaches you how to win."

### The Seven Steps of World-Building (Story World)
1. Define the arena (a unified field with "walls"); 2. define land / people / technology; 3. **establish the characters' value oppositions** (values form clusters, each cluster a culture); 4. build subworlds from those value oppositions (utopia = all three in balance and the hero rises; dystopia = imbalance, a "state of nature," and the hero falls; "The world exists in service of the characters"); 5. define the social system; 6. define the technology and magic systems (six questions: what the magic is, who has it, its limits, whether others can obtain it, whether it is tied to a place, and whether it can be ended); 7. define the direction of the world's development, beginning with a world of slavery: "The world of slavery is an expression of your hero's great weakness."

### The Five Archetypes (Strength / Shadow)

| Archetype | Strength | Shadow | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Warrior | The practical enforcer of justice | "kill or be killed"; the weak must be destroyed | Achilles, Luke, Katniss, Aragorn |
| Searcher | Seeks what is truly valuable; pursues truth and justice | Aimless, unwilling to commit | The Grail knight, Jason, the detective |
| Teacher | Spreads knowledge so others can live better | Forces students to think his way | Yoda, Mary Poppins, Lecter |
| Rebel | Takes action against the system of slavery | Cannot offer an alternative | Prometheus, Neo, Holden |
| Trickster | Mocks the powerful and attacks the oppressive system | Lies, deceives, and steals | Odysseus, Hermes, Ferris Bueller, Joker |

### Symbol Table (Drive—Symbolic Objects)
Animal = a template for the path to enlightenment or hell; machine = efficiency without a heart; journey = the span of a life; labyrinth = the belly of the beast / death / confusion before enlightenment; ladder = the stages of enlightenment; tree = life and knowledge; cross = the tree of human rebirth; Shadow = a profound flaw that is also the source of growth; sword, bow, shield, cloak, seed of the sacred tree = right action; Underworld = death and the unexplored self; Garden = union with natural law.

### Other Techniques
- **External = Internal Journey**; **The Journey's End Point** (without an endpoint, the story wanders).
- **The Opponent and Self-Revelation**: each opponent represents one side of the hero that must be overcome.
- **Family Trip** (the best opponent technique): take the family on the road; the family is a continuous intimate opponent (Little Miss Sunshine).
- **The Ongoing Main Opponent**: at least one opponent runs through the whole story, and the more personal the better.
- **The Mythic vs. the Tragic Ending**: Myth grants immortality (if only through telling), while Tragedy cuts life off with no rebirth.

### Subgenres
Monomyth (Campbell's hero's journey, a "universal male warrior Myth" whose extension to all protagonists narrows it); Male Myth (linear, divide and conquer); Female Myth (circular, combine and grow); Origin Story (the best expression of "creating plot from character"; in Black Panther the hero's growth and the nation's moral growth move together); Comic Myth.

### Paths to Transcendence
- **Creation Myth / Myth-SF Epic**: "A Creation Myth is not the story of creating the physical world. It is about creating consciousness and culture." Naming comes first, since self-consciousness begins with names, and then the focus narrows to the family. 2001 has three major flaws: it is dull; it has social stages but no cultural stages; and "cosmic vision is pictorial and symbolic, not moral."
- **Female Myth** (the differing beats derived from Oz and Avatar): a nation of women; each ally has a journey of recovery; the Talisman is a shoe or a seed, not a sword; battle can be absent; the revelation can be purely personal; multiple rebirths; a movement from shame culture toward guilt culture.
- **Ecological Myth**: the hero changes from a technological person into a natural one, ending in "a marriage of science and myth"; its negative version is The Machine in the Garden.

### Evolutionary Code vs Story Code
An isolated system at equilibrium ↔ Weakness-Need / Desire; encounter with a new challenge from the environment ↔ Opponent / Plan; an individual with a special ability responds and survives or dies ↔ Battle / Self-Revelation; reproduction of a new generation and temporary equilibrium ↔ New Equilibrium.

Coming-of-Age has two beats: **Frame—Weakness-Need to Self-Revelation** (bad faith → seeing oneself clearly, taking responsibility, and recognizing that one can affect others positively; in The Graduate, in the back seat of the bus, "the real struggle is just beginning"); **Self-Life Stages** (the archetypal situations: Birth, Child to Adult, Marriage, Creating Children, Deeper Adulthood, Becoming Godlike).

### The Nine Stages of the Self (Locating the Arc)

| # | Stage | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Childhood Forever | Peter Pan, Toy Story 3 |
| 2 | Child to Adult | Huckleberry Finn, To Kill a Mockingbird, Moonlight ("one step forward, two steps back") |
| 3 | Child to Adult in Anti-Coming-of-Age | The Catcher in the Rye (antiplot, "Plot is the sugar of story") |
| 4 | Fantasy Bildungsroman | Harry Potter |
| 5 | Adult to Leader | Built into Myth; Saving Private Ryan, Schindler's List |
| 6 | Leader to Visionary | Moses |
| 7 | Machine to Human and Superhuman | Westworld S1 (the four elements of consciousness: memory, free will, moral choice, suffering) |
| 8 | Hero to Artist: Künstlerroman | CODA (choosing between the family fishing boat and Berklee) |
| 9 | Hero to Artist of Another Person | The Intouchables (Traveling Angel Comedy) |

Shakespeare's use: ground tragedy in the weakness peculiar to a stage of life (youth in Romeo and Juliet, early adulthood in Hamlet, midlife ambition in Macbeth, the confidence of old age in Lear).

### Techniques
- Determining the Frame; Unifying the Plan; Using Detective Reveals (three kinds: it was not her fault / she was not responsible / she was responsible, but in a different way); the Biopic use of Storyteller Structure (Milk).
- **Five rules for Myth/Drama**: hide the Myth source; give the hero a strong goal as the spine; take the family on the road; pair every drama scene with three or four Myth scenes; escalate the moral tests. The shape is vortex street. The key work is Oedipus Rex: "Investigating the moral element in the story is the crucial connection between the Mythical and the modern dramatic story."
- **Personal Myth**: first describe the whole life in mythological terms, then crosscut mythological scenes with dramatic ones.
- **Three techniques from Cinema Paradiso**: Social Fractals (matching the rise and fall of the hero, the cinema, and the town); Social Fractals in Time; Storyteller Structure.

### Subgenres
The True Story family: Autobiography, Biopic, True Story, Reportage, Documentary. Coming-of-Age variants: Anti-Coming-of-Age, Fantasy Bildungsroman, Künstlerroman (fixed beat: the choice between family and artistic success), Traveling Angel Comedy.

### Paths to Transcendence
- **SF/Myth Epic**: the island laboratory (The Tempest, Robinson Crusoe); the psychohistory of Foundation is both structure and plot device.

### Techniques

### Techniques
- **The Leapfrog**: the subgenre skeleton → one story scene right after the inciting event → alternate story scenes and gags → never run several gags in a row.
- **The Dangerous Desire**: one overall goal, with all the trouble self-inflicted; Intense Desire; Intensity vs. Incompetence (10 against 1); Expose the Incompetence (the goal forces the hero to do what he is worst at).
- **Sustainable contrast in four-point opposition**: by drop type (animal / child / machine); by power status; by sex; by values. Four ways to strengthen it: the opponent also has a comic gap; menacing but not deadly; sharp contrast (big/small, country/city); attach the contrast to value clusters (big = forcefulness, power; small = tact, scheming; country = honesty, hard work; city = fast-talking, "me first"). "Push the oppositions to the extremes."
- **The Comic Cyclone**: first ask what the hero is most afraid to do, then repeat it in different forms, each more extreme.
- **Three rules of the Scam**: a con about desire (Br'er Rabbit); a campaign spread over many scenes; drawn out until near the end.
- **Four rules of Disguise**: the drop follows the drop type; the garment the hero least wants to wear (Tootsie); he cannot get it off; the audience knows.
- **Creating the Ultimate Nightmare**: Start from the End Point; Battle of Chaos.
- **Drive: Immoral Actions**: a small wrongdoing produces social consequences.
- Television: at least four equal characters, each able to drive a story; each episode crosscuts three or four predicaments and ties them together unexpectedly at the end (the "karma comedy" of Seinfeld).

### Subgenres
Romantic Comedy; Shakespearean Comedy (comic types, scams, disguise, miscommunication, physical gags, wit, self-reflexive); Comic Myth / Epic Comedy (an antihero pursues a false goal; Myth sets up the Comedy and the Comedy delivers the punch line); Fish out of Water (Crocodile Dundee crashes the wilderness into the oppressive city); Farce ("comedy-as-game," everyone pursuing the same worthless object with extreme intensity); Screwball; Buddy Story; Traveling Angel Comedy (which the author calls the most consistently successful subtype, and which is also Comedy + Fantasy); Parody ("the abstraction of an abstraction," rarely sustains more than one scene; the only transcendent example is Young Frankenstein).

### Path to Transcendence 1: Black Comedy (10 Beats)
1. A group of characters inside an organization; 2. one of them demonstrates the system's rules and logic in detail; 3. the hero has a negative desire; 4. opponents within the system compete for the same goal; 5. someone realizes the logic is absurd; 6. everyone uses extreme means; 7. the battle is extremely destructive; 8. no one has a self-revelation; 9. the survivors immediately resume pursuing the same goal; 10. the downward cycle never stops. Shape: branching. Examples: Catch-22, Network, Dr. Strangelove, Goodfellas, Parasite.

### Path to Transcendence 2: Satire (13 Beats)
1. The hero is inside a social system; 2. someone explains the system's values; 3. the hero has an intense desire; 4. he resolves to climb to the top; 5. at least three opponents (a peer at his level, the authority at the top, and a rival system); 6. each explains his beliefs; 7. those beliefs drive people to do stupid things, the main source of comedy; 8. an apparent defeat and a revelation, in which the hero sees that both systems are absurd and moves toward "nothing has value"; 9. the hero tries to bring both systems down; 10. the main system collapses in the battle; 11. the hero may have a revelation about spontaneity and truth; 12. a moral action; 13. a marriage of friendship or love, occasionally founding a new system. Examples: Groundhog Day, Tootsie, American Beauty, Seinfeld, Fleabag, VEEP.

The philosophical structure of Groundhog Day: "really Phil's search for his best self"; the best self comes from helping others, and it succeeds more than the selfish self did.

### The Eight Steps of the Story World
1. Land / people / technology; 2. one metaphor carries the whole allegory; 3. define the culture by the characters' values (shame culture in the wilderness/village, guilt culture in the town); 4. a system of rules that is exposed the moment the hero enters; 5. the technology and magic systems (the six questions; the greater the magic, the less useful it is); 6. a free world or a world of slavery; 7. subworlds; 8. a double arena: "The fantasy world should represent a dream or nightmare come true for the reader"; "Even a positive fantasy world has a duality."

### Techniques
World vs. Weakness (can be reverse-engineered from "the place I most want to go"); the list of six needs; The Slow Open; the three steps of world vs. desire (a concrete, urgent desire line; do not explain the world at the start; hang the world on the desire line); the two Plans (in the positive world, an internal negative trigger at the end; in the negative world, use the tools of reality and become more and more nightmarish); The Opponents' Powers; Enjoy the Passageway ("when it happens, it's gold"); The Fantasy Reveal (it comes from how the world works or how the opponent controls the world — who is "he" in Field of Dreams).

### Subgenres
Portal Fantasy (the classic form); Urban Fantasy (no portal; almost inevitably mixes Crime / Thriller / Detective); Fairy Tale (Fantasy + Horror, with a witch-type opponent); the rest are listed by name only: Traveling Devil, Romantic, Space Fantasy, Historical, Magical Realism, Sword and Sorcery, Dark, Alternate History.

### Paths to Transcendence: Three Kinds of Social Fantasy
- **Traveling Angel Fantasy**: turns deus ex machina into strong structure; the seven steps are split (the person helped takes the weakness and the revelation; the angel takes the desire and the plan); the angel is a super-ally and ideally has a weakness of his own (Clarence must win his wings); he does not do the work for the person.
- **Paradise on Earth**: a detailed model of how a happy society works; borrow the circus or the garden; the negative variant is paradise lost. Pleasantville: "from apparent to real utopia," and also a Rejuvenation Myth.
- **Fantasy Political Epic**: the three elements of system, causation, and process; the two versions of the same town in It's a Wonderful Life, "The main way the writers show utopia and dystopia is by comparing who each minor character becomes under different leaders and value systems"; the utopian standard is "each person is the best and freest version of himself within his own abilities and position"; "Real freedom is a social structure that puts its people in the best position to win the game of their lives." Three techniques from Game of Thrones: Tournament Structure ("The closer we get to the vortex point, the fewer the strands and the faster the narrative drive"; the constant death of major characters is a structural cause); Same Goal and Vortex; Story Fractals with Multiple Four-Point Oppositions (the four great houses form the base four-corner opposition, with further corners inside each house). The risk of tournament structure: the winner must be worthy of the audience's years of investment and must embody the theme.

### The Differing Beats of Thriller (10 Beats)
T1 The opponent's plan (the crime more often has not yet happened); T2 Story World (the facade of an everyday family; in the Political Thriller, a corrupt black hole); T3 A threatened amateur investigator, the Woman in Jeopardy; T4 Weakness Matches Crime (the ghost matches the opponent's crime but is lighter, Shutter Island); T5 Adding Emotion to Intellect (susceptibility to physical harm: Michael Clayton's gambling debt); T6 Solve the puzzle while fleeing attack; T7 Opponent: Attack, Emotional Attack (guilt and fear, with gaslighting as the extreme); T8 Continue the investigation under attack; T9 Double-line Reveal (evidence of guilt and evidence of innocence cross, and the ending gives innocence first, then guilt); T10 Battle—Opponent's Final Attack (the flashbulb in Rear Window).

Detective vs. Thriller: the murder has already happened / has not yet happened; a professional investigator / an ordinary person; can fight and carry a gun / cannot fight and carries no gun; many suspects / one main suspect. What they share: "Investigating provides the spine in both of these genres."

### Checklist of Techniques
The Opponent's Plan First (three requirements of the criminal: a false alibi, at least one other person with a motive, and planted evidence); The Ingenious Opponent; The Opponent's Plan Determines Investigation; Unique Detective; The Weakness Story (Tana French's The Trespasser); The Weakness-Crime Connection; Two in One (solving her personal problem helps her solve the case, and vice versa); Faith vs. Skepticism (attach love to the thriller: believe or doubt); Change Perspective; Iceberg Opponent ("how you parse out the reveals is what makes or breaks your plot"); More Suspects; Decreasing Suspicion ("take the stink off"); Emotional Attack; Determine Reveals Up Front; Techniques for a Great Revelation Sequence (interleave true and false, escalate, accelerate, keep it close to loved ones; the sequence in Defending Jacob); Red Herrings; The Double Clue (the key card in The Killing; the salt water in Chinatown); The Least Likely Killer.

### Plot Shapes
Spiral (circling back to the same event: Vertigo, The Conversation); Frames (a sequence of different minds: Rashomon, Citizen Kane); Chinese Boxes (a mind within a mind: Shutter Island, Memento; a story within a story: Frankenstein, Heart of Darkness).

### Subgenres
Police Procedural (the battle takes place in the interrogation room), Cozy Mystery, Locked Room, Political Thriller, Woman in Jeopardy, Epic Thriller (Shadow of a Doubt); the rest are listed by name only.

### Paths to Transcendence
- **Cosmic Detective/Thriller**: mixes Myth / Epic with Drama; three layers (psychological, social, cosmic); "Tracking the external opponent, the murderer, must eventually lead the detective to discover her internal weakness." Its sources are Oedipus Rex (detective and murderer are the same person) and Hamlet.
- **Story of the Mind and Truth**: REVEAL 1, the mind works through stories; REVEAL 2, the first story is a character called "I"; REVEAL 3, the mind's success lies in creating a methodology for examining itself. Techniques: Matching Structure with Mind; Stories of the Self (metafiction); Point of View and Multiple Truths ("Truth and lying exist on a spectrum"); Events Outside the POV (the fountain in Atonement); The Unreliable Narrator (in Rashomon, each person tells a different lie).

### Techniques
The Ghost Cycle; The First Fight ("the intimate opponent does the most damage"); Mutual Need; Character Potential (eight kinds: Quirky Personality, Vitality, Determination, Values, Special Ability, Moral Strength, Intellect, Good Heart); Extending the Allies; Connecting Ally to Hero; **the four techniques of the main opponent**: Creating an "Odd Couple" ("Always look for a difference that lasts"), Family Opponents, Mistakes (every great Love story is about trust and forgiveness), The Love Test (three layers of conflict stacked: personality → status → morality); Forced Togetherness; The Second Suitor; Comparing the Suitors (Austen's three suitors: the dull rich man, the charming empty man, the man of character in the background); The Love Scam; Extending the Scam; Conflict vs. Communion (Two Plot Sequences; Red Herrings in Love); Returning to the "No" (the New Year's Eve countdown in When Harry Met Sally); First Kiss; Love Then Sex.

### Plot Shapes

### Subgenres

### Paths to Transcendence
Classic Love ends with marriage; the transcendent form begins with marriage, "an art form of duration, not crescendo."
- **Tragedy of Traditional Marriage**: the four clauses of the old contract (a man is attractive because of power and money; a woman because of appearance; the man provides resources; the woman cares for the children), ending with the man turned into a machine and the woman into a child. Madame Bovary, Revolutionary Road, A Doll's House.
- **Comedy of Remarriage** (Cavell): each is trapped in the past at the start; forced togetherness reveals a lighter side of the other; the double reversal teaches them to build a marriage that does not depend on social rules. "you must come up with something new and deeper about the ex that allows each to fall in love again."
- **True Love and the Art of Marriage** (8 Beats): Communion—Wedding → New Lover—Exploring Love Day by Day → Weakness-Need—Love Is Settled and Differences Between the Sexes → Desire—Life Success → Opposition: Obstacles to Loving Day-to-Day (parents and children, work and love, larger forces) → Moral Argument—Issues of Loving Long-Term (good lies vs. bad lies, children first or spouse first) → Self-Revelation—Love Is an Entire Way of Living (a series of revelations) → New Equilibrium—Higher Love. Techniques: Opposition Before Desire; Creativity in Everything. The new contract: each is attractive because of "how each cares for the other and makes the other believe in his or her potential"; both provide resources and both care for the children. The Thin Man's nine features of a happy marriage: work together, have money, have no children to distract them, love play, make each other laugh, respect their differences, protect each other, each show their childish side, and enjoy each other's company.
