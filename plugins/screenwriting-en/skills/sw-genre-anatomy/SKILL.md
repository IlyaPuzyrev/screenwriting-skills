# Genre Anatomy (类型解剖)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see Section 9 of [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations. Genre names and beat names stay in English, glossed on first occurrence.

This skill is distilled from John Truby's *The Anatomy of Genres: How Story Forms Explain the Way the World Works* (2022). It forms a pair with the same author's `sw-truby-anatomy` (the seven key steps, the twenty-two steps): that skill handles the skeleton of the story, while this one handles the **genre layer** — which additional beats a given genre requires on top of the skeleton, what the hero, opponent, and world should look like, how several genres mix, and how to go beyond the genre itself. For the full beat tables, techniques, and sub-beat sets, see [reference.md](reference.md); for film-by-film beat breakdowns, see [reference-cases.md](reference-cases.md).

## I. What a Genre Is

### 1. Three Rules (Rules of Play)

- **Rule 1: a genre is the unit of commerce, and it is a beat system.** Every major genre has fifteen to twenty beats of its own (genre beats) — "these plot beats have more to do with the success of a story than any other element by far." Miss one beat and the core audience will notice (a Love story without the first dance). A trope (a single character, device, or line) is merely "sprinkles on top"; the beat sequence is the cake. Self-check: remove every trope — does the plot sequence still hold?
- **Rule 2: contemporary popular stories mix three or four genres.** The watershed is 《星球大战》 (Star Wars): Fantasy + Science Fiction + Western + Myth + samurai film (Action), its beats alternating in rapid fire. Two conditions: you must know all the beats of every genre you mix in, even if you do not use them all; and you must be able to use another genre's techniques to write your own. Prefer mixing genres that usually do not go together (SF + Caper is 《盗梦空间》 (Inception); Gangster + Myth + fairy tale is 《教父》 (The Godfather)).
- **Rule 3: you must transcend the primary genre.** Hitting every beat is necessary, not sufficient. Three paths: twist the beats (change the order or the method, not delete them); write the genre's life philosophy into the theme; explore the "story form of life" unique to that genre. See Section XVI.

### 2. Families

Myth family (Myth, Action, Western); Crime family (Detective, Crime, Thriller, Gangster); Speculative family (Horror, Science Fiction, Fantasy). Mixing within a family is easy but not new; crossing families is more likely to produce something new.

### 3. The Ladder at a Glance

Truby arranges the genres into a "ladder of enlightenment" according to "the primary flaw the hero must overcome, the quality of the life philosophy, and the art form explored," and at the end of each chapter says what the genre lacks and what the next rung supplies. Use: **the hero's primary weakness determines which genre you are actually writing.** Write Action but have the hero overcome "the inability to love," and the theme has already jumped to Love.

| Genre | Fundamental concern | Life philosophy (original wording) | Hero's core weakness | Opponent | Plot shape |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Horror | Religion | Confront death and face your ghosts from the past. | The mind enslaved: binary thinking, a fundamental fear, an unspeakable desire, plus shame and guilt | monster, the hero's double | linear |
| Action | Success | 90 percent of success is taking action. | The will to violence in a shame culture; can only fight alone | nemesis, the second-best warrior | linear, the fastest |
| Myth | The Life Process | Seek immortality by finding your destiny in this life. | Fatherless; needs to "become a hero" without knowing it | a succession of strangers | meander |
| Memoir / Coming-of-Age | Creating the Self | Examine your life to create your true self. | Shame and guilt grown from family injury; bad faith, self-deception | family members or members of the group | storyteller structure |
| Science Fiction | Science, Society, Culture | Make the right choices now to ensure a better future for all. | unevolved, flawed on "what is human" | authorities, the system itself | branching |
| Crime | Morality and Justice | Protect the weak and bring the guilty to justice. | Cop: poor, ordinary, lonely; criminal: believes himself above the law | super criminal | linear |
| Comedy | Manners and Morals | Success comes when you strip away all facades and show others who you really are. | comic gap; a bumbler who thinks he is cool | authority figures with pretense | linear (Comic Myth uses meander, Black Comedy uses branching) |
| Western | Rise and Fall of Civilization | When you help others make a home, you create a civilization where everyone is free. | The loneliness of extreme individualism + shame | the same kind of man: the bad cowboy | linear (Anti-Western uses rise and fall) |
| Gangster | Corruption of Business and Politics | Don't be enslaved by absolute power and money or you will pay the ultimate price. | Extreme ambition + a compartmentalized moral code | six corners firing at once | rise and fall |
| Fantasy | The Art of Living | Discover the magic in yourself that makes life itself an art form. | repressed and blinded; does not know how to play | the authoritarian father/mother who runs the system | meander / branching |
| Detective / Thriller | The Mind and the Truth | Look for the truth and assign guilt in spite of the danger. | Limited point of view, begins in ignorance; Thriller adds vulnerability | the killer hidden among the suspects | spiral / frames / Chinese boxes |
| Love | The Art of Happiness | Learning how to love is the key to happiness. | cannot love | the lover themself | linear (multi-strand uses branching-in) |

## II. Division of Labor and Disagreements with Existing Tools

1. **Snyder's ten story types** (Section III of `sw-industry-business`) divide by "what the story is about"; Truby's fourteen genres divide by "beat system and life philosophy." They are not mutually exclusive: Monster in the House ≈ Horror's enclosed space, Golden Fleece ≈ Myth's journey, Buddy Love ≈ Action's Buddy Story. **First use Snyder to locate "what the story is about," then use this skill to check "which beats to hit."**
2. **崔石焕 (Choi Seok-hwan): "genre is a promise made from the poster onward; at most 5 to 10% minor innovation"** (Section II of `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`) is kept alongside Truby's "twist the beats": Choi is talking about the audience promise, which cannot be broken (a comedy's laughs cannot stop); Truby is talking about changing the method while still hitting every beat. The two are compatible: do not delete beats, but every beat can be reordered or done differently. For commercial genre films, follow Choi first; when the script is mediocre, use Truby.
3. **The seven key steps of `sw-truby-anatomy` are this skill's foundation**; for the table mapping the seven steps onto each genre, see Section I of reference.md. This skill does not rewrite the seven steps or the twenty-two steps; the general method of the moral-argument chain is in Section VII of that skill, and here we write only Crime's eight Morality beats and the variants for each genre.
4. **Series**: the book focuses on film and novels, and only four conclusions are specific to series: Crime's season-level moral sequence (Section VIII, item 5), Gangster's epic of the everyman (Section XI), Comedy's four equal protagonists in opposition (Section IX), and Fantasy's tournament structure (Section XII). For everything else, when transferring it to series, mark it "inferred"; for structure go to `sw-series-structure`, for story type go to Section V of `sw-series-engine-bible`.
5. **Chinese opera projects are not covered by this skill**; Chekhovian/Ozu-style anti-climax projects use only Sections I, II, and XVI, and do not apply the beat sheet.

## III. Horror: Religion

**In one sentence**: "a unique monster relentlessly pursue a victim," driving the audience into an ever-smaller box until they are buried in the ground. Key question: What is human and what is inhuman?

**Template**: The hero is the victim, with two ways to start (a fall from a high place; an ordinary good person who wanders in, made "intensely human" from the start). The opponent is the monster, the "Other-in-the-Extreme," who is also the hero's double. The ally is the Rational Skeptic, who doubts on the audience's behalf. The world is the haunted house and the closed society, "the flawed mind made physical." Shape: linear.

**Beats**: 15 beats; for the full table and techniques, see Section II of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. The ghost is the first beat and must be connected in a single line to the monster: "a crime from the past is connected, through the psychological and moral weakness of the hero, to a horrifying crime in the future." Self-check: delete the ghost — do the monster's attacks still mean anything? If they still stand, the ghost is decoration.
2. The monster must be the hero's double. Self-check: name which of the hero's fundamental fears or unsociable desires the monster corresponds to; if you cannot, you have scares but no human being.
3. The barrier is at once the outer wall and the inner taboo, and it is certain to be breached; after the crossing, the cost of every step that postpones death escalates (the Law of Necessary Cost of Living). Self-check: list the costs; if they do not grow, you have not got it right.
4. The only standard for the middle act is "pressure, pressure, pressure," rising and never falling; three ways to apply pressure: enclosed space, no way to call for help, injury; then add deception, hiding the opponent's true power. When a single monster keeps hitting the same beat, use four concealed attacks: a house of many rooms, the opponent multiplying or growing, death and resurrection, a fake ally.
5. Write the reveal as a chain of escalating nightmares, faster and faster (the Cyclone Effect); the last nightmare is the battle, set in a place that seems safe, fought from inside and outside at once. Failure condition: the hero goes on the offensive in Act One and gains the upper hand — that is Action.

**Transcend**: Horror/Myth gains the five Religion beats; Horror/SF Epic performs a structural reversal of hero and monster ("We are the monster"), enlarging the frame to the social, political, and cosmic level.

**Boundary**: Basic Horror ends with No Self-Revelation plus a Double Ending; if you want to give the hero a revelation, you must take the transcendent path.

## IV. Action: Success

**In one sentence**: "life is about doing well, not doing good"; the hero is a fighter; "You win or you die." Key question: Fight or flee?

**Template**: The hero is driven by the will to greatness; his weakness comes from his strength (he is strongest fighting alone, and therefore cannot enter a community). The opponent is the nemesis, "the second-best fighter and the character best able to attack the hero's biggest internal flaw." The world is a community in danger, and the arena divides into enclosure (the fortress, a pressure cooker) and chase (the chase, speed but pressure released). Shape: linear, the fastest of all genres.

**Beats**: 16 beats; for the full table and techniques, see Section III of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. Open with a crisis that can be unrelated to the main plot to define the hero's fighting ability and character. Self-check: delete the opening sequence — does the audience still know he is brave, resourceful, and what he is good at?
2. Action's biggest problem is a lack of plot (back-and-forth conflict "hitting the same beat"). The solution is four-corner opposition and a hierarchy of opponents: one main opponent plus at least two secondary opponents, hide one or two of them or hide their connection, and have each attack in a different way, in rapid succession. Self-check: can you draw a four-corner diagram in which every character is opposed to the other three at least once?
3. The plan must be ingenious and timed to the second; the middle act must contain one event that wrecks the plan and forces the hero to improvise a new tactic on the spot, "the single most important plot beat." If you cannot find that scene, the hero is merely executing.
4. Good tactics are the baseline; the best hero also understands strategy (sacrificing immediate efficiency to win the whole war).
5. The final battle has six elements: the tightest space, a unique arena, multiple parties fighting, convergence into one-on-one, the fastest pace in the film, and the hero outthinking the opponent. The ending must answer join or withdraw: farewell, communion, a buddy renewal, or the female hero staying on as leader — choose one of four.

**Transcend**: "Showing loss is the main way writers transcend the Action form." Scale up into Action Epic/War (11 beats; the opponent must have defensible values — a purely evil opponent is "always a mistake"); abstract it into Caper and Sports (11 beats).

**Boundary**: Crime handles justice, Action handles winning and losing; Thriller looks for the opponent, Action fights him.

## V. Myth: The Life Process

**In one sentence**: "life is a journey in which one's ultimate goal is to understand oneself"; the destination is discovering what one was born to do, "destiny is life's version of immortality."

**Template**: The hero is a Searcher; his weakness is determined by the archetype's shadow plus the loss of the father, "Becoming a hero is a need, not a specific, conscious desire." The opponent is a series of Successive Strangers, each corresponding to an aspect the hero must overcome. The ally is often the Teacher. The world is the natural world plus two cultures that are opposites, "the most important major story element in the Myth form." Shape: meander.

**Beats**: 16 beats; for the full table, the seven steps of world-building, the archetype table, and the symbol table, see Section IV of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. The seven steps of world-building cannot be taken out of order: define the arena; define land/people/technology; **define the characters' value opposition first**; then build the subworlds from it (drawing the map first and adding the culture afterward is "the biggest mistake Myth writers make"); define the social system; define the magic system (six questions; "the more magic power a character has, the less useful they are to you"); define the direction of development — the enslaved world of the opening must be an enlargement of the hero's weakness.
2. The talisman has two conditions: no one knows its true power; it is the material expression of one side of the hero's identity. Missing either makes it a MacGuffin. The type of talisman declares gender: sword/bow = Male Myth, shoe/seed = Female Myth.
3. Every stop on the outer journey is the visible form of an inner change, and the journey needs a concrete destination. Self-check: list each location and write down what changes inside the hero there; cut any stop you cannot write.
4. Meander must add a private main opponent who runs through the whole story, or take the family on the road (Family Trip). Self-check: count the acts in which the main opponent appears; if he appears only at the beginning and the end, add several threats in the middle.
5. Self-revelation divides into public (recognizing that you are the king) and cosmic (a moral vision for the whole society, which must be concrete, not merely images and symbols). At the end the hero goes beyond the old creed rather than improving it; granting immortality is Myth, cutting off life with no rebirth is Tragedy.

**Transcend**: Creation Myth/Myth-SF Epic (writing the creation of consciousness and culture); Female Myth ("the Male Myth is about divide and conquer, while the Female Myth is about combine and grow": multiple rebirths, allies each with their own journey of recovery, a final battle that can be nonviolent, a revelation that can be purely personal); Ecological Myth.

**Boundary**: The Monomyth (the hero's journey) is only a subform of the universal male warrior Myth; generalizing it to all heroes is a narrowing.

## VI. Memoir and Coming-of-Age: Creating the Self

**In one sentence**: "Memoir and Coming-of-Age are the nonfiction and fiction story forms of the individual's journey to create the self." The Memoir hero is "the detective of her own life."

**Template**: The Memoir hero carries a wound from family injury, "The wound is not the weakness. Weakness is the internal flaw that grows from that wound," and must list both psychological and moral weaknesses. The opponent is a family member or a group that is like family, a knot of love and hate, with a system of enslavement underneath that no one sees. Shape: Storyteller Structure (a present-tense event triggers memory, and the memory leads irreversibly back to the present). The Coming-of-Age hero begins in bad faith (self-deception, blaming others, living up to others' expectations).

**Beats**: Memoir 15 beats; Coming-of-Age two beats plus nine stages of the self; see Section V of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. Define the final conflict scene first, then work backward to the frame's starting point. "Telling it chronologically from beginning to end" is a sign that the frame is not set.
2. Storyteller Structure needs a present-tense triggering event; it frames the whole piece, which distinguishes it from flashback.
3. Double Desire: the present-day author wants to find meaning (the frame); the past author can have only **one** goal running through most of the story (the spine). The past hero having several parallel goals is the direct cause of fragmentation; the remedy is to mix in a Detective or Thriller structure.
4. The moral argument has five questions: the central moral question; how the opponent forces the hero to face it throughout; the biggest mistake; ten or more major decisions; the revelation at the end about how to live well. Tracking only "how I felt at the time" is the biggest mistake; a hero who is all good and a family that is all bad is a defense brief.
5. Two self-revelations; the second "must contain new information that only came to the author because she wrote this book." The moral decision at the end is forgiveness or farewell; both are growth.

**Transcend**: Myth/Drama (for every drama scene, three or four Myth scenes; hide the Myth source, take the family on the road); Personal Myth; social fractal (matching the rise and fall of the hero, the family/institution, and the community).

**Boundary**: Coming-of-Age usually covers only "child to adult"; "adult to leader" belongs to Myth. For material about real people, first go through the four questions on rights and adaptation in `sw-workflow`.

## VII. Science Fiction: Science, Society, and Culture

**In one sentence**: "Science Fiction is concerned with how to create society, and in particular, how to create a better society"; it is almost always written from the negative side: the dystopia that choosing this way now will produce.

**Template**: The hero is unevolved, his weakness located in "what is human," "connect the hero's lack of evolution with the planet's lack of evolution." The opponent is the authorities; in the fourth stage it is the system itself. Secondary characters (ordinary people, species, robots) are the primary components of society-building. The world is built in three layers: Rules of the Universe → Society-Culture → Über Tech. Shape: branching, converging toward a vortex point.

**Beats**: 10 beats (the author himself calls it the "broadest and loosest" genre); for the full table and the four-stage society table, see Section VI of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. Define the social stage first, then build the hero from that stage's fixed character types: Wilderness (warrior) → Village/Town (ordinary person) → City (ordinary person, fighting for justice or fleeing the bureaucracy) → Oppressive City (antihero). Each of the four stages has its culture (shame → guilt → consumer → fear). Failure condition: a wilderness world whose characters are driven by guilt.
2. Society must be "both enslaving and free": all three ingredients — real choice, apparent choice, direct restriction — must be present. A totalitarian state that relies only on force and has no deception is a "poorly written totalitarian society."
3. The world's rules must carry lethal penalties; put in elements that exist now before layering on new rules; the detail must cover politics, work, love, play, and language. Failure condition: the reader becomes a "clinical observer."
4. Use the vortex to cure the propulsion problem of multiple worlds: at the start, tell the reader the rough destination; at the end, everything converges on a single point in time and space, and crosscut faster as it gets closer. Self-check: delete a subworld — is the overall desire line affected? If not, it was tourism.
5. The public/cosmic revelation cannot be merely a plot point or a pretty image (the giant baby in *2001* is a counterexample); it must give a detailed moral vision of how the members live together.

**Transcend**: SF/Myth Epic (the island laboratory); SF/Horror Epic is dystopia, adding a hidden underlying system of enslavement and a flawed species doomed to self-destruct. "If you want to write a popular Science Fiction story, your best bet is to write it as a space Western."

## VIII. Crime: Morality and Justice

**In one sentence**: "people who break the law and those who catch them"; the premise is that all people have equal rights; the key question: "Who is allowed to live above the law?"

**Template**: The police hero is a master cop, an ordinary man who lives deeper than anyone, poor, mediocre, lonely, yet believes most in society's values (the prototype is Porfiry in 《罪与罚》 (Crime and Punishment)). The opponent is the super criminal, who believes himself a cut above everyone. The two are mirrors of each other, "both cop and criminal are the best at their respective jobs, and both are equal in ability." Shape: linear.

**Beats**: 14 beats, plus the eight Morality beats and the classic moral code; see Section VII of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. Crime's desire is "catch the criminal"; Detective's is "find the truth." If the hero still does not know who the criminal is in Act Two, you are writing a Detective story.
2. The crime must be ingenious; "The more deception the criminal uses, the better"; both sides hide their overall attack plan; the reveals are planned from the start and linked to one another. The cop crossing the line is the default move, the audience expects it, and the surprise must come from a deceptive attack.
3. The criminal hero must be at least partly right, so that the cop is torn between "wanting to catch him" and "wanting him to succeed."
4. The moral argument runs through plot, not dialogue: break the law → break it again to cover up → the counterattack forces him into a tighter moral squeeze → he errs again. The moral decision at the end must rest on a code stated clearly at the start (《杀手没有假期》 (In Bruges) rests on Harry's early line "if I had killed a little kid … I'd've stuck the gun in me mouth. On the fucking spot."); the decision must affect others and carry a personal sacrifice whichever way it goes.
5. Legal accounting is not moral accounting; "the criminal is punished" only settles the legal account. The transcendent version settles the moral account with poetic justice (like repays like, a penalty unique to this crime, executed by the criminal's own hand). For series, arrange the moral decisions in a negative sequence season by season, with at least one moral test per episode; the control is "How far can we push this guy toward darkness and not lose the audience?"

**Transcend**: Epic Crime Tragedy and Crime Black Comedy, which share the eight Morality beats and the "three anchor points of the moral code" (the value at the start, the means in the middle, the decision at the end).

**Boundary**: Action starts from the individual and speaks of superiority; Crime starts from society and speaks of equality — their themes are opposite.

## IX. Comedy: Manners and Morals

**In one sentence**: "Laughter occurs when someone is reduced instantaneously from high ideal to low real, but not too much." Three kinds of drop: animal, child, machine. "Comedy is the only form that works by undercutting every beat of the Story Code."

**Template**: The hero is a bumbler who does not know it, "she thinks she's cool"; the first step is to build the comic gap (high is pretense, low is weakness). The opponent must have pretense, "Without pretense he is simply powerful and cannot be dropped," and must be menacing but not deadly. Cast characters by the nine comedy types, assigning high/low/need. Shape: linear.

**Beats**: 11 beats, plus the nine-types table, Black Comedy 10 beats, and Satire 13 beats; see Section VIII of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. Story scene first, then gag, then alternate to the end. "The biggest mistake in comedy writing is to string together too many jokes or gags at the beginning." Self-check: count how many gags run together in the first ten pages without a story scene between them.
2. Desire strength 10, ability 1, and the goal forces the hero to do what she is worst at; all her trouble is of her own making.
3. Two-point opposition is not enough; you need four corners, and every opponent has a comic gap; the contrast must be hung on a value cluster and pushed to the extreme. TV comedy has to last for years, so it needs at least four equal characters, each able to drive a story (《宋飞正传》 (Seinfeld)).
4. Define the ultimate nightmare first (five kinds: death, failure, public humiliation, fear of the opposite sex, getting caught cheating), then lay back a chain of escalating variants of the same fear, faster and faster; the final battle is often a battle of chaos.
5. The plan is a scam postponed until near the end; the four rules of disguise: drop according to the type of drop, wear what the character least wants to wear, be unable to take it off, and let the audience in on it. The middle act advances through small evils; the greatest punishment is public shame and ostracism. The "publicly humiliated self-revelation" is a cliché the author singles out.

**Transcend**: Black Comedy (no one has a revelation; the survivors immediately pursue the same goal) and Satire (at least three opponents: the same level, the top, and a rival system).

**Boundary**: For the mechanics of jokes and the half-hour structure, see `sw-sitcom-comedy`. The drop cannot go too deep; if the audience actually sees death, the comic tone shatters — Black Comedy is the exception.

## X. Western: The Rise and Fall of Civilization

**In one sentence**: "a wandering cowboy fights criminals trying to stop people from building their homes out of the wilderness"; the cowboy "can lead his people to the promised land, but can never enter it himself."

**Template**: A double hero unique to the genre: the primary hero is the cowboy, the secondary hero is the farmer. The cowboy's desire is to **leave** civilization; protecting the builders is incidental, and self-destructive. The opponent is the same kind of man, "the moral gunfighter versus the immoral gunfighter," and the weakness he attacks is the shame culture and the reluctance to kill. The world is "a fragile community in its infancy," and technology is a sacred symbol (the horse, the fence, the six-shooter, the badge). Shape: linear.

**Beats**: 13 beats, plus the showdown ritual recipe and Anti-Western 7 beats; see Section IX of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. The farmer must have his own desire line. If the cowboy himself is clearing land, building a house, and settling down, you are writing a homestead drama.
2. Give the supercowboy a concrete desire that can only be won by fighting; the plan is an escalating face-to-face confrontation (from fists to guns); if it advances through scheming or negotiation, it is Detective or Gangster.
3. The showdown is a ritual, "by far the most important story structure step." Required elements: a public setting, a prior humiliation (cheat/sissy/coward), everyone knowing someone will die, the good cowboy not drawing first, and that one second stretched out. Miss any one and it is just an Action fight.
4. The first revelation comes before the showdown (he understands he must kill); the revelation at the end is that he cannot enter the home he carved out himself. If the hero stays in town and starts a family, he violates the Doomed Man.
5. To write an Anti-Western, invert every beat: the opponent becomes a faceless corporate boss, the Code fails, apparent defeat becomes Apparent Victory, the showdown becomes a massacre without ritual, the hero has No Self-Revelation, and the technology shifts from horses and six-shooters to trains, cars, and machine guns.

**Transcend**: There is only one path: the Anti-Western. The author judges the classic Western "artistically a dead form walking"; its surviving form is the Space Western.

**Boundary**: The Western later divides into three "sons": Anti-Western, Detective, Gangster. For Western case studies, see Section I of `sw-american-case-studies`.

## XI. Gangster: The Corruption of Business and Politics

**In one sentence**: "Every story of a bad king is a gangster story." Society itself is the criminal; the gangster is the cowboy who comes to the city and becomes a criminal, his direction changed from "west" to "up."

**Template**: The hero is a gang boss and killer, "the killer who loves his family," with psychological and moral contradictions heavier than those of any other genre's hero, and a highly compartmentalized moral code. Six opponents fire at once: the boss, the family as a whole, rivals within the family, the police, a rival family, and his own brother. The world is the corrupt city, and the organization is enslavement (lifelong debt, death on leaving). Shape: rise and fall.

**Beats**: 14 beats, plus the transcendent 13 beats; see Section X of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. If the hero wins the game by intelligence rather than becoming king through organization and violence, you are writing Crime's criminal-as-hero.
2. The trick of the plot is gang members changing sides: "every time one of them shifts sides it creates a revelation and more conflict" (five fake allies in 《教父》 (The Godfather)). Self-check: list all the fake allies and their turning points; a middle act without turning points has no revelations.
3. The drive talks only strategy, never morality, "the only genre with little or no discussion about the morality of the hero's actions." This is the genre exception to the two steps "ally's attack" and "moral decision" in `sw-truby-anatomy`.
4. The inciting incident is a small crime; the immediate goal is the deal, the ultimate goal is the empire.
5. There are only three endings: death in a hail of bullets, a Pyrrhic victory, or enslavement; the hero has no self-revelation, which can be transferred to another character (Kay) or deliberately denied through Black Comedy (Henry Hill's "schnook" monologue). If the hero sees the light at the end and is saved, you are writing a redemption drama.

**Transcend**: Gangster Epic (Epic of the Everyman: let the family affairs of "the king in a democracy" mirror the fate of the nation, 《黑道家族》 (The Sopranos), 《广告狂人》 (Mad Men)); Eastern (negative Rags to Riches); Economic-Political Epic. For business-political material: first define how three sets of oppositions interweave (seller vs. buyer, entrepreneur vs. company, boss vs. worker), and never write business and politics separately; the hero needs a "need becomes greed" moment; advertising itself is written as a story by the seven steps.

---

## XII. Fantasy: The Art of Living

**In one sentence**: "a repressed hero explores a heaven or hell," in a fixed sequence A (the weakness of the ordinary world) → B (the fantasy world that challenges the hero) → A2 (both the hero and the ordinary world have changed). "Fantasy is mainly about being someone more."

**Template**: The hero is an Explorer, his weakness repressed and blinded. The opponent is the authoritarian father/mother who runs the system, and **the opponent must have a revelation that loosens the rules**. The world is a double arena, "The success of any Fantasy depends mainly on the quality of the story world." Shape: meander or branching.

**Beats**: 12 beats, plus the eight steps of the story world and the three forms of Social Fantasy; see Section XI of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. The fantasy world must in some way be the opposite of the hero's great weakness (World vs. Weakness). Self-check: write the weakness in one sentence, write the world's most prominent value in one sentence; if they are not opposed, you cannot test the weakness.
2. The first scene in which the hero enters the fantasy world exposes the rules; all six magic questions must be answered. "The biggest mistake Fantasy writers make is failing to adequately define the rules."
3. A positive fantasy world must have a dark side, "utopian with a dark side"; the trigger for going home must be internal and personal (Josh in *Big* sees the childhood he will miss), not an external force driving him back.
4. Classic Fantasy must have a passageway; the passageway itself is a subworld, and its job is to flip the audience's psychological filter from "people can't float" to "isn't it wonderful that people can float here." At least one super magical moment; the self-check question: "What is the perfect expression, in action, of this particular world when all is right?"
5. The world hangs on the desire line; do not explain the whole world at the start. "Killing narrative drive is the worst sin in popular storytelling, in every genre." The reveal comes from a secret about "how the world works" or "how the opponent controls the world"; an identity reversal alone is a Detective reveal.

**Transcend**: Three kinds of Social Fantasy: Traveling Angel (the person helped gets the weakness and the revelation, the angel gets the desire and the plan); Paradise on Earth (from surface utopia to true utopia); Fantasy Political Epic (two versions of the same town, "comparing who each minor character becomes under different leaders and value systems"; tournament structure).

## XIII. Detective and Thriller: The Mind and the Truth

**In one sentence**: Detective hides the opponent of the seven key steps until the end, "forces the detective and the reader to solve the puzzle together," and therefore "has the most complex plot of any genre story." Thriller is "a form of Detective story in which the investigator is in mortal danger."

**Template**: The detective is a searcher plus a magician, "a person whose every job begins in ignorance"; the series detective is a traveling angel with no weakness, and only a standalone, more advanced work gives him a serious weakness. The opponent is the killer hidden among the suspects; the fight is over "whose story is believed," and the attack on the detective's one great weakness should be written into the opponent's original plan (the vertigo in 《迷魂记》 (Vertigo)); the opponent's flaw is hubris. The Thriller hero is a non-professional and must have a weakness that leaves her vulnerable to physical harm, and a ghost that matches the opponent's crime but is lighter. Shape: spiral, frames, Chinese boxes.

**Beats**: Detective 20 beats, Thriller 10 beats of different execution; see Section XII of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. **Write the opponent's plan first, then the detective's plan**: list the killer's sequence (killing, covering up, false clues, the attack on the detective) and satisfy the three requirements (a false alibi, at least one other person with a motive, and planted evidence). The author says this is "the best plot technique for any story."
2. Every clue must support at least two answers (the Double Clue); the revelation must be triggered by concrete physical evidence; a "flash of insight" is cheating.
3. The revelation sequence has four rules: true and false interleaved, intensity escalating, faster as the end approaches, and closer and closer to the detective's intimates; fix all the revelations at the start of writing.
4. The three requirements of a real suspect: motive, opportunity, no alibi; the killer is the least likely person, and you must give him cover that removes suspicion. If there is only one credible suspect, the reader will guess it.
5. The Thriller's drive is two lines, "evidence of guilt" and "evidence of innocence," crossing with equal weight; at the end, innocence first (apparent defeat), then guilt; after the attack, the hero must keep investigating rather than only reacting; in the final battle the hero must improvise a new move (the flashbulb in 《后窗》 (Rear Window)). The final battle is a battle of stories: "Both should be logical. But only one is true."

**Transcend**: Cosmic Detective/Thriller (tracking an external killer ultimately leads to the detective's own inner weakness; poetic justice can be applied to the detective himself); Story of the Mind and Truth (metafiction, multiple points of view, unreliable narrator).

**Boundary**: If the hero can fight, carries a gun, and is trained, you are writing Detective or Action, not Thriller.

## XIV. Love: The Art of Happiness

**In one sentence**: "two people whose attraction and affection cause them to form a 'marriage'," the premise being "each person grows through another person"; the lover must be necessary to the hero. "This is not a Love story; it's a stalking story" is the verdict on "a confident man chases a beauty until she says yes."

**Template**: Neither knows how to love; ideally they are two variants of the same need (Mutual Need); weakness and potential are established at the same time, and the potential must be seen by the other. **The main opponent is the lover themself**; the suitor and the family are only secondary opponents; the true inner opponent is love itself (the Ghost Cycle: the fear of love that persists in the present). The ally is the love adviser, "almost always wrong." The smallest world is the two bodies and minds; you also need an exotic subworld; both utopia and dystopia must be written. Shape: linear.

**Beats**: 24 beats, plus True Love 8 beats; see Section XIII of reference.md.

**Hard rules**:
1. Remove the suitor — is there still ongoing conflict between the two? If not, the opposition is not located in the couple. Find a "difference that lasts," still operating in Act Three.
2. Every beat is yes/no: after the dance, after the kiss, after sex, immediately return to "no," reminding them of differences in money, status, morality, and way of life. Without the fallback, the middle act collapses.
3. Establish the life desire first, then the love desire; split the love desire into gaze → meet-cute → longing. If they fall in love in the first ten minutes and there is no career line, the story will not last ten minutes.
4. The plan is a scam and must survive at least the battle, "Along with the first dance, the scam is the most important beat in Romantic Comedy." The middle act alternates fighting and initiation, the first explorations going from shallow to deep. Sex comes after love, "If you show sex too early, you virtually kill the possibility of the audience feeling that these two people have fallen in love."
5. The Double Reversal needs evidence in action: each gives up the thing they are most selfish about; the ending is one of three: Communion, Farewell (which must have a higher purpose), Farewell and Communion. Austen's three-suitor rule: the dull rich man, the charming empty man, and the man of character in the background.

**Transcend**: Classic Love ends with marriage; the transcendent form begins with marriage, "an art form of duration, not crescendo": Tragedy of Traditional Marriage; Comedy of Remarriage ("you must come up with something new and deeper about the ex"); True Love (a decrescendo of eight beats, working backward from the opposition to the desire).

**Boundary**: If post-marriage material still uses the single goal line of courtship, it falls back into a replay of courtship.

## XV. Genre-Mixing Worksheet

1. **Define the primary genre** (the first three questions of Section XVII), list all its beats, and tick them off one by one.
2. **Define one to three secondary genres**, preferring cross-family and "usually don't go together" combinations. List all the beats of each secondary genre too, marking the ones you will use and the ones you knowingly will not.
3. **Check beat incompatibility**: "Sometimes the beat from one genre will prevent the writer from being able to use the beat from another." Check pair by pair, and state which beat you chose, which you dropped, and why. Common conflicts: Horror's No Self-Revelation vs. Love's Double Reversal; Action's speed vs. Fantasy's 360-degree world.
4. **Decide which is the underlying structure**: most popular mixes borrow the Myth structure as their base (Space Western, Comic Myth); the underlying structure determines the plot shape.
5. **The theme can only be carried by the primary genre's life philosophy**; secondary genres supply beats and techniques, not the theme. If the two Mind-Action story views fight, go back to step one and change the primary genre.
6. Write it into the story bible below the character section: `Primary genre | Secondary genres | Beats dropped and why | Underlying structure | Dominant worldview`.

---

## XVI. Three Paths to Transcend the Genre (Self-Check)

After hitting every beat, do at least one of these:

1. **Twist the beats**: change the order or the method, not delete. The audience must get both "the beat they expected" and "a method they have never seen." Self-check: list which beats you twisted and how.
2. **Write the genre's life philosophy into the theme**: compare it with the one sentence in the Section I master table — is your controlling idea (Section II of `sw-premise-theme`) a concrete version of it, an inverted version, or untouched? If untouched, you have a competent genre film.
3. **Explore the genre's "story form of life"**: Horror's religion, Action's war and sports, Myth's life process, Memoir's self, SF's science-society-culture, Crime's morality, Comedy's everyday manners, Western's history, Gangster's business and politics, Fantasy's aesthetics, Detective's mind, Love's marriage. For the operational beat sets of each, see reference.md.

One-line transcendence route for each genre: Horror does a hero/monster reversal; Action writes loss; Myth writes the creation of culture or the Female Myth; Memoir uses a Myth/Drama ratio; SF writes a society that is "both enslaving and free"; Crime does a lifetime of moral accounting; Comedy does Black Comedy or Satire; Western inverts into Anti-Western; Gangster writes the epic of the everyman; Fantasy does Social Fantasy; Detective makes the investigation lead to the detective himself; Love writes the art of marriage.

## XVII. Diagnostic Checklist (12 Genre-General Questions)

1. What is the **primary weakness** the hero must overcome? Which genre does it correspond to in the Section I master table? Does it match the primary genre you claim?
2. Have you ticked every beat on the primary genre's beat sheet? For the beats you are missing, can you say why the book says they exist?
3. Is the opponent the form this genre requires (Horror's double, Action's nemesis, Love's lover themself, Detective's least likely person, Gangster's six corners)?
4. Is the plot shape right? In a linear genre, has 360-degree world-building killed the drive? In a meander genre, is there a private opponent running through the whole story?
5. Is the story world an enlargement of the hero's weakness (the enslaved world) or the opposite of the weakness (Fantasy)? Which did you choose, and is it consistent?
6. Is the middle-act propulsion mechanism the mechanism of this genre (Horror's pressure, Action's improvisation, Comedy's ladder of nightmares, Love's alternation of fighting and initiation, Detective's revelation sequence, Gangster's side-switching)?
7. Does the self-revelation at the end fit the genre? In No Self-Revelation genres (basic Horror, Gangster, Black Comedy, Anti-Western), have you forced in an epiphany?
8. Does the new equilibrium fit the genre (Action's join or withdraw, Western's Doomed Man, Comedy's marriage, Love's communion or farewell)?
9. For a mixed secondary genre, do you know all its beats? Have you written down why the dropped beats were dropped? Are the two story views fighting?
10. Is the theme a concrete or an inverted version of the primary genre's life philosophy? If you cannot say, it is a competent genre film.
11. Which beats did you twist? Simply deleting them does not count.
12. This book uses films and novels as examples and draws only four conclusions specific to series; have you marked the parts you applied to series as "inferred"?

---

## XVIII. Workflow (Interface with `sw-workflow`)

1. **Stage 0**: after the entry path has been determined, use the first three questions of Section XVII to define the primary and secondary genres, and write them into the bible's project information.
2. **Stage 1**: after the premise and controlling idea are finalized, compare them with the one-line life philosophy for that genre in the master table and see whether the controlling idea is a concrete or an inverted version; if it is neither, send it back.
3. **Stage 2**: after the BS2 board or the seven-step skeleton is finalized, run the genre's beat sheet and fill in missing beats; run Section XV.
4. **Stage 3**: check the character list against the genre's hero/opponent template; for Comedy, cast from the nine-types table; for Action/SF/Gangster, draw the four-corner or six-corner diagram; for Love, write the three suitors.
5. **Stage 6**: run Section XVII; when the script is "competent but mediocre," run Section XVI.

**When not to use this skill**: Chekhovian/Ozu-style anti-climax and multi-protagonist projects do not use the beat sheet; Chinese opera projects are not covered; short films and commercials follow the accordion principle in `sw-truby-anatomy`, running the seven steps only and taking one or two genre beats as needed.

## XIX. Anti-Patterns

- Piling up tropes as genre: there is a vampire and a haunted house, but no line connecting ghost and monster.
- Mixing three genres but only mixing tropes, not knowing the secondary genres' beats; turning "twisting the beats" into "deleting the beats."
- Forcing an epiphany into a No Self-Revelation genre; letting a Love story that must have a Double Reversal let only one side win.
- Letting a secondary genre carry the theme, so two Mind-Action story views fight.
- Opening with world-building, so narrative drive dies in the first thirty pages (frequent in Fantasy and SF).
- The hero type out of step with the world stage (a character in a wilderness world driven by guilt).
- Lifting the book's film conclusions straight into a series without marking them "inferred."
