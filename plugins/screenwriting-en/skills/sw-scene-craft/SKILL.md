# Scene and Sequence Craft (场景与段落)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

A scene is "the smallest unit of dramatic meaning" (陆军 / Lu Jun) and also a "miniature story" (McKee). This skill covers every decision in a scene from choosing the location to setting down the words: **why here and now, what value is at stake, who wants what and by what tactic, how beats stack up to a turning point, how props and details do their work, how suspense is set up and delayed, and how to enter and leave a scene**. For analysis examples and tool tables, see [reference.md](reference.md); for dialogue, see `sw-dialogue`; for overall structure, see `sw-story-structure`.

---

## I. What Is a Scene

- **McKee's definition**: an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value, with perceptible degrees. **No scene is without a turn**: mark the value's positive or negative charge at the start, mark it again at the end; if it is the same, ask "why is this scene here?"—the answer is almost always exposition → cut the scene and weave the information in elsewhere. A lovers' breakup that crosses four locations—bedroom, kitchen, garage, highway—is still one scene (test it by whether it can be written "as a whole").
- **Mei Feng's value test** (梅峰 / Mei Feng): when you set up a scene, the only thing you must determine is **what value is at stake in the scene and whether it flips or gains a new layer by the end**; subplot values can be woven in, but both the main value and the subordinate values must be shown accurately on both their positive and negative sides. Change can come only through conflict, and conflict only through character choice. The restaurant scene in 《双虎屠龙》 (The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance): the same value pair (civilization vs. savagery) shifts several times within one scene—the law book / the Western code / literacy / the black suit / the stumble / the drawn gun / the steak picked up off the floor / the thrown bottle.
- **Field**: A scene is "the place where the story is told"; its two elements are place and time, and a change of place or time makes a new scene (at about ten thousand dollars a minute); first fix the **purpose** (the one piece of information that moves the story forward), then the place and time, then find the **ingredients inside the scene** (the waiter in the restaurant, the neighboring table, an old friend).
- **Hicks**: Every scene must do two things at once—give the reader new information that advances the plot, and reveal more about the protagonist. Cut a scene in which nothing happens (shaving in a hotel room).
- **Henson**: A scene = a complete dramatic action happening somewhere, with consistent time, objective, and characters; the bases for a scene change are a shift in place or time (the most frequent), adding or removing characters, and changing the dramatic objective.
- **Lu Jun**: A scene is not just two lines of "time and place"—"why does the story happen here and now rather than there and then?" 老舍 (Lao She): "To show a cook, simply putting him in a kitchen to cook makes it hard to get any drama; place him laying track in a vast desert, or in the middle of a fierce battle when everyone desperately needs water and food, and his role becomes much greater."

## II. Five Steps of Scene Design (McKee)

1. **Define the conflict**: Who drives the scene, and what do they want (state it as an objective)? What does the force of antagonism want? The two must be directly opposed, not merely tangent.
2. **Mark the opening value** as positive or negative.
3. **Break the scene into beats**: a beat = one unit of action/reaction; label the subtextual action and reaction with emotionally charged present-participle phrases ("lying at her feet" / "ignoring his plea"); only a clear change in behavior marks a new beat.
4. **Mark the closing value** and compare it with the opening (same = non-event).
5. **Survey the beats** for the arc and the turning point: each beat builds on the one before until the value charge changes at the turning point; a turning point can only be created by **action or revelation**, and it has a fourfold effect: surprise → increased curiosity → insight → new direction.

Supplementary notes:
- **Scene objective and superobjective**: what each character wants right now (when the scene objective is satisfied, the scene stops) plus a subconscious superobjective (often the opposite of the characterization); background desires (the web of constraints) limit what can and cannot be said.
- **The five steps of action**: desire → awareness of opposition → choice of action → action → expression. "A scene does not reside in the act of speaking but in the action the character takes by speaking."
- **The earliest sign of a badly written scene is too many repeated beats** (the same tactic in different words).
- **The four diseases of a poor scene**: desire has no opposition and therefore no conflict; repetition cycles without progress; the turning point comes too early or too late and unbalances the scene; the writing is on-the-nose and lacks credibility. To fix it, test the beats against the scene's goal: change the behavior to fit the desire, or change the desire to fit the behavior.
- The six forms of conflict each have their own way of writing (balanced / comic / asymmetric / indirect / reflexive / minimal); see `sw-dialogue`.
- **Hoxter**: a scene beat = the moment of dramatic progress or shift in weight (《雌雄大盗》 (Bonnie and Clyde): guessing professions → "I just got out of the state prison"); every important character has a goal, and the actor chooses a tactic (flirt / threaten / cajole / reason); polarity reversal; August's six questions and four exercises: What needs to happen in this scene? What if it were cut? Who needs to be present? Where could it happen? What is the most surprising thing? Long or short? → think of three openings → run the scene in your head → draft → full scene.

## III. Entrances, Exits, and Pacing

- **Enter late, leave early**: Goldman—"Never enter a scene until the last possible moment"; 《唐人街》 (Chinatown): the hydrogen peroxide in the bathroom → cut to bed afterward (Field). Hicks: **leave the scene before the conflict is fully resolved**—the feeling that something remains unfinished creates tension (《小木屋》 (The Cabin): "I came all the way out here and all I got was a raccoon!"); get into the scene at once and detach just before it ends, jumping to the next scene with energy. Walter's "start late, end early" applies to scenes and to a single line of dialogue (a restaurant scene jumps straight to the main course).
- **Pacing** (McKee): 40–60 scenes averaging 2.5 minutes; pair a one-minute scene with a four-minute scene; a two- or three-minute shot exhausts a location's expressive possibilities; 《十二怒汉》 (12 Angry Men) relies on more than 80 French scenes (a new scene whenever a principal actor enters or exits); as you race toward the act climax, shorten scenes to increase speed and **earn the right to pause**; the scene of a major reversal is itself long, slow, and tense.
- **Speed** = the level of activity within a scene (whispering in bed vs. a riot).
- **The principle of transition**: the third element between two scenes = something shared or opposed—a character trait (a mischievous child → a childish adult), an action (foreplay → afterglow), an object (greenhouse → jungle), a line of dialogue, the texture of light (dawn shadow → sunset glow), a sound (waves → snoring), an idea (blank canvas → dying old man).
- **Sequence**: Field—"a series of scenes linked by a single idea," the spine of the screenplay (wedding, chase, homecoming, bank robbery); fix the sequence idea (the cup) first, then fill in the content (the coffee). McKee: 2–5 scenes with impact escalating to a peak; give each sequence a title. Hoxter: a sequence = a group of scenes that achieves one or more story goals, unified by time or place; decide which stretch of beats gets extra weight (the argument in 《冬天的骨头》 (Winter's Bone) is the longest); two ways to pace: control scene time (when to enter and exit), and divide the sequence by beats.
- Mei Feng: between two heavy scenes, insert a short scene featuring Peabody—no conflict, a slower rhythm, a break between the heavy scenes, a way to solve a time gap—giving the whole film its rhythm of tension and release.
- Henson: action paragraphs read like a list, not a description; include the "given circumstances" (time, season, and weather; the environment that affects action; physical details that provide clues)—"early morning, a very blue sky, harsh sunlight, heat shimmering upward, the Stars and Stripes fluttering over the stands" = the Fourth of July; change it to "gray new snow" = Veterans Day; "change one image and you change the whole story."

## IV. Action Over Dialogue (Walter / Henson / Hicks)

- **Principle 30: drama is action**; **Principle 31: any action is better than no action; the best action is action organically one with the story**. Tennessee Williams: "Make them stand on their heads, make them take off their pants, make them do something—anything—but never nothing."
- Film's strongest property is the magnification of very small things: 《金发女郎之恋》 (Loves of a Blonde)—a wedding ring slips off and rolls across the floor; the camera crawls under the table past rows of legs and feet, catches the ring, and rises to find itself in the middle of a crowd of women; 《克莱默夫妇》 (Kramer vs. Kramer)—French toast (at first like being lost in the jungles of Borneo, at the end waving pots and pans like a master chef); 《玫瑰纹身》 (The Rose Tattoo)—wringing out a stocking; 《扪心问诊》 (In Treatment)—a handmade potholder; 《无耻混蛋》 (Inglourious Basterds)—a fountain pen drawing up ink. "Small, ordinary, everyday actions magnify what film can mean."
- **Static situations**: restaurants, bars, cafés, cars, and dinner tables are wisely avoided unless they are indispensable to the whole—telephones (《大西洋城》 (Atlantic City): searching for the package in a phone booth; 《铁钩船长》 (Hook): throwing the phone out the window), cars (《第三类接触》 (Close Encounters of the Third Kind): signaling the "car" behind to pass), restaurants (《今晚大件事》 (Big Night): refusing to make two main courses = a metaphor for the conflict between commerce and art), offices (an inspector confronting his superior → list a dozen alternative locations → a swimming pool: the superior swims laps on doctor's orders while the inspector shouts from the poolside, chlorinated water splashing onto six-hundred-dollar crocodile shoes). **For every scene, list a dozen alternative locations and choose the one that expands the story**; "give the actors something to do, and use detail to bind story and character together."
- Hicks: action paragraphs should be written as action with cause and effect, a beginning, middle, and end, giving the reader **a sense of pace**, not a word-for-word description of the visual sequence (the snowcat chase is a counterexample); sex, fights, and chases are the hardest to write and the easiest to cliché, yet the screenwriter must still provide a scene outline to keep the thread (《灯塔楼梯》 (The Lighthouse Staircase)); write less setup for phone, eating, and driving scenes.
- Henson's "story-doing": replace states with actions—"I'm sad" → "I cry" → the son comforts her or mocks her; "Mary, you're so rebellious" → Mary hires an assassin to kill Elizabeth I; when a script stalls, the character is stuck in an internal state instead of acting; use the "I want..." exercise to get out of it; write reactions whose intensity is the opposite of expectation (Jones draws a gun on the samurai; Dunbar kills himself when he learns of the amputation; the tiger and leopard leap off the cliff).
- Contrast (Field): when playing anger, smile gently; talk about flowers while falling in love; talk about love in front of a shark; decide to commit a robbery in a pool hall and have a policeman come in and look around. Comedy = create a situation and have the character respond to it with absolute seriousness.
- McKee: the best advice about dialogue is not to write any; before each scene ask, "How can this be written purely visually?"

## V. Ample Interest and Charm (意趣要足, yìqù yào zú): Suspense, Delay, and Delight (Lu Jun)

- **意趣 (yìqù — interest and charm) = novelty, surprise, interest, and ingenuity**, realized as **suspense and delight**—情趣 (qíngqù), which is itself the anticipation of delight. 李渔 (Li Yu): "The two words 'wit' and 'interest' are indispensable to the playwright."
- **Setting up and destroying suspense**: set up, resolve, then set up a new one (《傻女婿卖布》 (The Foolish Son-in-Law Sells Cloth) has seven suspense hooks linked ring by ring); if the opening is a big suspense (solving a crime), the ending must be unexpected enough to bear the weight of the opening; open by leading the audience onto a false trail, toss out one mystery after another, and finally untie the big bundle (《赶不走的媳妇》 (The Daughter-in-Law Who Won't Leave)).
- **Revealing the answer and keeping the secret**: Chinese opera usually hands the key to the plot to the audience (in 《十五贯》 (Fifteen Strings of Cash) we know from the start that the villain is 娄阿鼠 (Lou Ashu); in 《九只铜板》 (Nine Coppers) three lines give it to the audience first); **Archer: a playwright should never keep a secret from the audience—you cannot keep a secret**; curiosity is "a delicacy for one night," while the enduring pleasure of drama lies in **foreknowledge**—the audience watches like a god watching mortals play blindly. Two approaches in 《杨门女将·寿堂》 (Women Generals of the Yang Family · The Birthday Hall): keep it secret from both the characters and the audience (the momentary effect of surprise) vs. let the audience, 穆桂英 (Mu Guiying), and 柴郡主 (Princess Chai) know the bad news before the birthday banquet and force themselves to look happy (the charm of "interest," far more moving).
- **The suspense of character and fate**: true suspense is the suspense of character and fate—the father's secret poisoning is revealed to the audience from the start, and suspense remains (two bowls of egg-fried rice, the chopsticks untouched); a small play does not chase surface spectacle but carves the small joints in fine detail.
- **Delay for release**: before a high jump, squat; before a punch, pull back; in 《小酒店的一夜》 (One Night at the Tavern) terror reaches its extreme before the idol appears "seen but not heard," and then the delay continues as it calls out names one by one; in 《莱欧尼达先生遇见"反动派"》 (Mr. Leonidas Meets the "Reactionaries") three layers of buildup in the middle widen the gap between panic and false alarm—"Only when tension and seriousness reach their extreme and people then discover that it was nothing at all do they receive aesthetic satisfaction."
- **Four sources of delight**: an interesting situation (Molière's formula: the contradiction comes straight to the point → the insulted party lays a plan → the mocked party takes the stage → the lashing is delivered in full → the schemer exposes it); an interesting character (in 《小九老乐》 (Little Nine, Old Joy), a henpecked husband's "fear is love," muddled in small matters but clear in big ones; the 戏眼 (xìyǎn — the one-line core attraction of an episode) makes the character blaze three times); interesting language ("I told her to stand still and she—you can walk wherever you please"; 《逼婚》 (The Forced Marriage): two philosophers); interesting detail (in 《镀金》 (Gilded), the details of showing off wealth are stacked in four layers: before the meeting / after the meeting / when more visitors arrive / behind the scenes).
- McKee: interest = curiosity (intellectual) + concern (emotional, the center of good); mystery, suspense, and dramatic irony are three audience relationships (three ways to write the man with an axe in the corridor); avoid cheap surprise and false mystery.

## VI. Exquisite Detail (细节要妙, xìjié yào miào) (Lu Jun)

- Detail = the smallest unit in the depiction of character, event, and environment; "detail decides success or failure" (three steps at a subway exit; 东乡平八郎 (Tōgō Heihachirō) noticing clothes drying on a gun barrel); "a story can be invented, but a detail cannot be fabricated" (a sparrow straddling two rows of tiles; a turkey crossing an era).
- **Mr. A vs. Mr. B**: Mr. A says that 金相 (Jin Xiang) "slaps his own face swollen to look fat" (puts on a show of wealth), and the phrase is too general to raise a laugh; Mr. B says that after dinner he sits at the gate picking his teeth with a matchstick, "his teeth stuffed with ham," a ten-yuan note hanging from his belt, and that he buys half a salted pig's head and carries it from the east end of the village to the west—**A states a generality through a concept; B portrays individuality through detail**.
- Examples: 阿 Q (Ah Q) signs his confession and "draws it in the shape of a melon seed"; 洪承畴 (Hong Chengchou) brushes off spider silk and smooths his clothes (he still wants to live); in 《合法婚姻》 (Legal Marriage) the girl plays hopscotch in the sunset (portraying her state of mind / revealing the theme / advancing the relationship); 《外套》 (The Overcoat): moths flying at night; 严监生 (Yan Jiansheng) and the two lamp wicks; 《变的旋律》 (The Melody of Change): eight details of the daughter returning from military training.
- **The five functions of detail in 《打铜锣》 (Beating the Gong)**: establish the environment (entering with the gong: three strikes, four lines); advance the conflict (catching the duck); portray character (coaxing 蔡九 (Cai Jiu) and tricking the gong mallet away → performing a magic trick and producing a second mallet); build the climax (killing the duck to check the grain); highlight the theme (giving the mallet with a message).
- **Five requirements**: truth; "close to the flesh" (fitting "this one" work, character, and scene); a sense of weight (prefer details that bear the birthmark of the era and the stamp of society: steamed bread dipped in human blood, a bill for 620 yuan, a dud firecracker from the "Criticize Lin, Criticize Confucius" campaign, finding the key to kill the ox and celebrate); **do not show off** ("wasted expression" said once is enough; seven or eight times is disgusting; "this sword is rusty" passed around the room is mere fuss; the detail of beating the crown prince's pants is good, but stopping to explain its "source" is not; Snow Mother with a five-minute dance—**the test of necessary repetition is whether it advances the plot, develops the conflict, and shapes character**: a bag of rice that appears three times is necessary); **visibility** ("killing the duck to check the grain" said in passing is weaker than taking a knife and splitting it open).
- There is only one source: observe life the way a waiter reads chopsticks ("You tucked both chopsticks toward your chest before eating, so I can tell you are poor").
- Walter's "Conk": not "a small town in the Midwest" but "Springfield, Ohio"; not "expensive clothes" but "a double-breasted chocolate-brown Armani suit"; not "various activities" but "gluing popsicle sticks together and painting with his fingers." McKee: concrete nouns, active verbs, and never use is/are anywhere.

## VII. Precise Props (道具要精, dàojù yào jīng) (Lu Jun)

- A prop is a small object through which feeling is exchanged (the paper fan, handkerchief, keys, and buttons at a blind date); "a well-chosen prop often does the work of much flat, dry language"; quality matters more than quantity (许仙 (Xu Xian)'s umbrella, Othello's handkerchief).
- **Six functions**: ① structural core (《追蛋》 (Chasing the Eggs): two eggs; 《一分钱》 (One Penny); 《训"草人"》 (Taming the "Scarecrow"): the scarecrow; 《上了锁的箱子》 (The Locked Box)—the wife throws the key onto the floor instead of placing it in his hand; 《玩偶之家》 (A Doll's House): the IOU); ② conflict booster (the donkey-hide glue advances the conflict every time it changes hands; the handkerchief like a relay baton); ③ characterization (周朴园 (Zhou Puyuan) uses a pocket watch to check the clocks; in 《皮九辣子》 (Pi Jiu Lazhi) one soft-shelled turtle kills two birds with one stone; Beating the Gong: two gong mallets; 《赵氏孤儿》 (The Orphan of Zhao): three sticks, "a hard job being a man either way"); ④ symbol of the soul and fate (the magic chair: who is chosen to sit + what is said when seated; 《罗汉钱》 (Arhat Coins); the prize savings certificate); ⑤ theme window (the cradle, the calming pill, the nanmu coffin, the door plank covered with slogans, the mischievous alarm clock); ⑥ carrier for song and dance (the stone mill, the pot).
- **Six techniques**: magnify (a foot warmer like a huge iron pot), miniaturize (a signboard like a playing card), repeat (a paper ring made of glued introduction letters; a dozen stools of chicken heads and feet), transform (jumping into a vat and walking while holding it), virtualize (Snow Mother, the Bodhisattva), substitute / live prop (a person dressed as a yamen runner ghost singing the inner monologue). "Drama is ultimately drama"—"a bright moon shines over Suzhou and other places" is scientifically true, but where is the poetry?
- 尹迪克 (Yin Dike): the protagonist's weapon = a symbol of identity (a lightsaber, a divine sword; it can be a car, a boat, a computer); let it play a role in the climax; Campbell: symbolic motifs (a handkerchief, a statuette) recur but are not abused. McKee: the image system—a category of image that recurs from beginning to end, consistent yet not without variation, and must remain latent (water in 《恶魔》 (The Devil); blindness / water / corruption in Chinatown); once it is noticed, it becomes neutral curiosity. Snyder's "recurring" (the flyer in 《回到未来》 (Back to the Future)) and "repeated action" must reverse in the second half.
- **The prop through-line** (孔尚任 (Kong Shangren)'s 《桃花扇》 (The Peach Blossom Fan)): one fan changes identity seven times—a scented fan pendant → a poem-inscribed palace fan (a token of love) → evidence for refusing marriage → "a sword for self-defense," stained with blood → the blood spots painted into peach blossoms, giving the fan its name, and sent home as a letter → after delivery, a relic → torn in public. **Rule**: every change of identity is caused by **someone else's action** (the poem, the peach blossoms, the sending, the tearing are each done by another person), and each is pinned to a value turn. **Self-check**: if no second person does anything to the prop in a given appearance, cut that appearance; it does not affect the plot. **When it fails**: if the prop is not a physical object that others can seize, destroy, alter, or pass on, this pattern does not apply.
- **Public substitution** (the Yuan zaju stage cue 〔换科〕 (huànkē — the cue for a swap in full view); 关汉卿 (Guan Hanqing)'s 《救风尘》 (Saving the Wind and Dust), Act IV, 〔正旦换科〕 (zhèngdàn huànkē — the leading female role performs the swap), switching the divorce papers): the substitution is written in the stage direction (科介, kējiè — stage-action cues) in two characters, **with no explanation added**—the audience sees it; the deceived person does not; the reveal is withheld until the middle of the third aria ("what you bit to pieces was the fake divorce writ"). **Self-check**: how many stretches of the play separate the substitution from the reveal? If they are too close, the audience has no time to worry for the protagonist. **When it fails**: if the audience does not clearly see the action at the moment of substitution, all the later tension collapses; switch to the other route, "keeping the secret from the audience."

## VIII. Apt Setting (场景要当, chǎngjǐng yào dāng) (Lu Jun)

- **O'Neill's "deliberate arrangement"**: 《天边外》 (Beyond the Horizon) alternates outdoor acts (the horizon = desire and dream) with indoor acts (the horizon disappears) to create rhythm; 《榆树下的恋情》 (Desire Under the Elms): two elms "like two exhausted women resting their sagging breasts, hands, and hair on the roof"; 《琼斯皇》 (The Emperor Jones): the forest and the drum (the drum from matching his pulse to a mad frenzy, the hallucinations becoming scene after scene); 《漫长的旅程》 (Long Day's Journey into Night): the fog outside the window, from bright sunshine to thick as a wall.
- **Chekhov's wholeness**: the water clock, the sound of woodcutting, a snapping string, an owl, a fire, a faint sigh, a whistle, a pause—portraying a state of mind and a way of life (see `chekhov-dramaturgy`).
- **The contrast method in Chinese opera**: a birthday hall turns into a mourning hall; the Tower of Chastity and Filial Piety; the Lu family's New Year couplets vs. 祥林嫂 (Xianglin Sao) in the snow; a chase on the moonlit dock.
- **Don'ts**: rural plays always in a small courtyard, urban plays always in a living room. **Three criteria**: serve the theme (a niche beside a tunnel where only two people can stand); serve character (a mud pit halfway up the mountain brings out three layers of historical, political, and economic background, letting 赵大爷 (Uncle Zhao) vent → win face → open his heart); serve conflict (the elevator itself participates in the conflict; scaffolding is a force for resolving the contradiction; 《张古董借妻》 (Zhang Gudong Lends His Wife): Moon City and the Yue family's house echo each other; 《姐妹易嫁》 (Sisters Exchange Marriages): three spaces intersect, dust from upstairs falls into the wine glass downstairs).
- **Walking the same road twice** (in the Yuju opera 《朝阳沟》 (Chaoyang Gully), Act II's "Road Song" / Act VII's "Looking Back"): the same space walked twice, the second time in the opposite direction, **recovering item by item** the objects pointed out the first time (the canal, the terraced fields, the martyrs' cemetery, the fruit trees), and using the same tune and the same opening sentence pattern ("Walk a ridge and cross a mountain" / "Walk a ridge and cross a gully")—the same thing on the same road means something different the second time. **When it fails**: if the first pass did not point out each object one by one, the second has nothing to recover and only lyricism remains. **Self-check**: did everything pointed to in the second pass appear in the first?
- **Software and hardware**: hardware = physical elements; software = literary elements that serve the scene's inner meaning (a recurring song, a folk story, the origin of the place—《第七棵是玉兰树》 (The Seventh Is a Magnolia): the theme song three times, the origin of Love Plaza, the character of the tree planter).
- Hicks on genre texture: in interpersonal drama, too much "space" destroys the tension of forced face-to-face encounter; in comedy the whole world is like a giant banana peel; horror and thriller twist into labyrinthine corridors; detective noir is a rotting urban jungle. McKee: a small, knowable world (《奇爱博士》 (Dr. Strangelove): three settings, eight characters).
- Egri on entrances and exits: they should occur only when the plot needs them and should advance the conflict; writing a character out on the pretext of pouring water is "unforgivably bad." Lu Jun: place a major character's entrance just as the storm is about to break.

## IX. Diagnostic Checklist

1. Why here and now? Would another location make the conflict harder and reveal the characters more? Have you listed a dozen alternative locations?
2. What value is at stake? What is the opening charge, and the closing charge? If nothing changes, it is an exposition scene—cut it.
3. What does each person want, by what tactic, and what does each want subconsciously? Is the opposition directly opposed or merely tangent?
4. Label each beat with a gerund: are there repeated beats? Does each beat move closer to the turning point than the one before? Is the turning point an action or a revelation? Does it arrive neither too early nor too late—and does it arrive at all?
5. Do you enter late enough and leave early enough? Do you leave before the conflict is resolved?
6. Is there a stretch where "nothing happens" (shaving, small talk, ordering food)? Is a character stuck in a state rather than in action?
7. Can the scene work without dialogue? Which small action could be magnified (the ring, the toast, the stocking)?
8. Is the phone / car / restaurant / office absolutely necessary? If used, does it become the center of action?
9. Is the suspense "a delicacy for one night" or the foreknowledge of fate? Have you handed over the key that should go to the audience? Is the delay long enough (three layers of buildup)?
10. Is the detail true, "close to the flesh," marked by the era, and visible? Is there showing off (the same detail mentioned seven or eight times, stopping to explain its source)?
11. Which kind of prop is it—structural core / booster / characterization / symbol / theme window / song-and-dance carrier? Which technique is used—magnify, miniaturize, repeat, transform, virtualize, substitute?
12. Does the scene have its software (song, story, origin)? Do entrances and exits occur because the plot needs them?
13. Does the sequence have a single idea? What is the third element (transition) between adjacent scenes? Are scenes shortening as you race toward the act climax?

## X. Workflow: Writing a Scene

1. In the sequence outline, confirm the scene's **purpose** (the one piece of information that advances the story + the one thing it reveals about the protagonist) and its position within the act (pacing: long/short, fast/slow).
2. Write down the **value pair** at stake and the opening charge; write down each character's scene objective, tactic, superobjective, and background desire.
3. **Choose the location**: list a dozen places, choose by the three criteria (theme / character / conflict); decide the software (song / story / origin).
4. Fix the **prop and details**: what work does one core prop do; two or three details that are "close to the flesh" and visible; which one should repeat, and does the repetition advance?
5. Fix the **suspense strategy**: does the audience know more or less than the characters? Which key do you hand to the audience? How many layers of delay?
6. Write the **beat sheet** (gerund action/reaction + value change), find the turning point, confirm there are no repeats; first ask, "How can this be written purely visually?"
7. Decide the **entrance and exit points**: enter late, leave early; keep the final blow in the last word or the last action.
8. Write: action paragraphs in the present tense, concrete nouns and active verbs, no camera terminology, no "we see"; for dialogue, see `sw-dialogue`.
