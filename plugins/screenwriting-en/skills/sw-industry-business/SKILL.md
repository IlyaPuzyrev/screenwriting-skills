# Industry & Business (行业与生意经)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

The operating logic and survival strategies of the American screenwriting industry. Core consensus: **"This is a business, not a fairy tale"** (Diamond & Weissman); "Writing a screenplay is not about the film industry, but the film industry is related to writing a screenplay" (Walter's Principle 56); "The smartest marketing strategy is to have a good script" (Principle 62). This skill covers: the idea checkup, loglines and pitching, submission and representation, contracts and credits, coverage notes and rewrites, film vs. television, and professional mindset. For the writing process and format, see `sw-format-adaptation`. For the complete self-check lists, query-letter samples, and contract terminology, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. Writing from the Buyer's Point of View (Diamond & Weissman)

- Submitting a script must benefit everyone at every stage — every link in the chain says "this is good for me" (agent → producer → director → executive); "it is the writer who carries the burden of helping everyone else realize their dreams."
- **A movie idea ≠ a script idea**: something that actually happened is not in itself a movie idea. **The three Cs**: character, concept, context ("this person, in a certain situation, in a particular environment... until he does the right thing"); the concept of 《土拨鼠之日》 (Groundhog Day) could be shot as a science-fiction horror-thriller; add "a grumpy weatherman" → comedy; add "Groundhog Day" → tone.
- **Test the idea**: do not write a synopsis first; tell it aloud to people you trust and watch for the reaction: "**I have to have this**" (a career starter), "Okay, I get it" (potential but familiar; it won't pay the bills), or "I don't understand" (back to the drawing board). The cafeteria epiphany: "What would it look like if Disney made our movie?" — examine it top-down from the studio's point of view.
- Blockbuster (larger than life) vs. independent (dependent on execution); "Is this idea right for me?" — write the genre you most love to watch; list many ideas first (choose one out of sixteen).
- **The sample film list**: same genre / similar protagonist / similar engine; list both good and bad films; "being original for originality's sake earns no points"; what earns points is respecting genre conventions and building on what came before.
- **The one-page, nine-step synopsis** (do the reader's work for them; "make the reader the hero"): setup → opportunity/challenge → first act turn → page-45 turn → midpoint reversal → page-75 escalation → worst point at the end of Act Two → an even worse final test → showdown/set piece. Make the film's **argument** explicit (theme = the belief system that wins the debate).
- **The character action chart**: retell the nine steps from each character's point of view (Han Solo appears at minute 45 purely for money → "How much money?" → takes the money and leaves → charges in at the crucial moment); "Can you tell the story from every character's point of view?"
- **A set piece** = a "trailer moment"; one in Act One, one in Act Three, several in Act Two; "What is Act Two? Where are the set pieces?!" — a version without set pieces may still sell, but set pieces make executives "feel as if they have seen the film with their own eyes."
- **Submission**: "Do not let the reader lose interest" is the first principle; open with something distinctive; register with the Writers Guild of America (WGA); rely on relationships (an entry-level job in Los Angeles, a group of ambitious peers, friends first, then one producer and one executive, then an agent); competitions and the Black List are supplements; never pay someone to read your script; "an agent will appear on his own once you have a script worth selling."
- **Notes**: if several people agree, change it; distinguish diagnosis from prescription ("It's the old car, gentlemen"); use the one-page synopsis and character chart as your yardstick; "following bad advice is one of the most common mistakes a screenwriter makes," but ignoring it is also a serious mistake; "being fired means you have already got the job."
- **Resilience**: 47 projects, 15 filmed (0.319); "as screenwriters, the only thing we control is what we write and how we write it"; learn at least one lesson from every project.

## II. The Idea Checkup: The Seven Elements of PROBLEM (Bork)

- The three common missing pieces in rejected work: a good idea / emotional resonance / an enjoyable experience. **60/30/10**: 60% of success depends on the core idea (conveyable in a one-page summary), 30% on structure, and 10% on execution. Before writing, put the idea into a one- or two-sentence logline plus a one-page summary and seek objective feedback.
- **The three elements of a logline**: who the protagonist is and the protagonist's connection to the story / the catalyst / the nature and difficulty of the mission (most important); do not write the inner journey.
- **P — Punitive**: story = protagonist + tree + rock; of 300 paid scripts, only 8 were recommended; the most frequent comments were "the story starts too late / scenes have no meaningful conflict / the conflict does not matter"; the seven traits of "perfect competition"; when adapting a true event, find "the big problem" and "the process full of near-disasters."
- **R — Relevant**: a protagonist who is present in almost every scene and never passive; "how he treats others" determines likeability; a flaw = "not living the best possible life," not "being mean to people"; the first ten pages establish the status quo before the catalyst; eight kinds of story problems; an antihero must face a big enough pile of problems (Walter White is the most sympathetic person in the opening).
- **O — Original**: a familiar genre + one key element changed (Bond → Bourne → Mr. & Mrs. Smith → the spy's child) + the author's voice; when you encounter a competing project, study it; "buyers value freshness less than writers and critics do."
- **B — Believable**: the first 10–15% establishes the rules clearly, then follow "what a real person would do"; a simple concept beats a complex one; the antagonist's motivation must be humanly logical.
- **L — Life-changing**: external stakes come first; the eight-level risk table (life → justice → freedom → home / keeping the family together → the best partner → career prospects → a life-changing award → a chance at happiness); once a life-and-death risk is introduced it must be present throughout; seven kinds of disqualified risk (career success, inner learning, generic military stakes, vague happiness, a single moment of decision, a bastard becoming a good person).
- **E — Entertaining**: ten emotions; a "drama" without life-and-death stakes must layer at least two entertainment elements (humor, wealth and fame, high-end settings, betrayal and manipulation, music, spectacle events, sex and competition, outrageous characters, exotic worlds, uplifting love and family); "being hugely entertaining is the trump card."
- **M — Meaningful**: the theme is a competing value with no simple answer, carried by an influential character / the B story; make the audience feel it rather than telling them.
- TV procedural dramas are limited to heroic / high-stakes / highly entertaining jobs (police officers, doctors, lawyers, and their variants); Levitan's "two characters, one line" method builds a web of conflict; characters need a credible mechanism that forces them to coexist; each episode's small problem can be solved, while the core big problem and the "unmet need" are never solved.
- Finding ideas: make long lists and cross-pair them ("aliens + baseball?"), fifteen minutes a day, quantity before selection; "talent depends almost entirely on attitude and practice."

## III. Loglines, Genre, and Pitching (Snyder / Hicks / Hoxter / Masterclass)

- Snyder's four elements (an ironic hook + a visual image the listener can picture + audience and cost + the title) plus an adjective for the protagonist, an adjective for the antagonist, and the original goal; "anyone standing still is a pitch target"; high concept is not dead (international markets account for 60%); do not say "X plus Y" when pitching; locate the story within the ten story genres (classified by "what it is about"; for the beats each genre must hit, see `sw-genre-anatomy`).
- Hicks on pitching: reduce the script to a few lines ("a cop alone in a building taken over by terrorists"); three reasons (a boss hears dozens or hundreds of pitches a week; movies spread by word of mouth; producers are probing the writer's sensibility); "this business is built on 'no'"; preparation: create atmosphere and mood, clarify the protagonist, capture the basic conflict, one line ("a convoy in space"); in the meeting speak rather than read, do not memorize, watch their eyes, be brief.
- Hoxter's terms: logline ≠ tagline ("They're young, they're in love, they kill people" is post-release marketing); a treatment is a prose pitch; an elevator pitch is ≤30 seconds; coverage, the initial report, goes on file and affects the next script; bake-off = a script-off; gross vs. net (net is "never seen"); the MBA (Minimum Basic Agreement) is renegotiated every three years; & = a team, and = written separately.
- Masterclass (Richards): "You've raised a tiger but you're handling it like a kitten"; put a one-line logline plus two or three comparable films in the script file ("《教父》 (The Godfather) plus 《速激 7》 (Fast & Furious 7)"); he has written 150 loglines; introduce a two-hour film in one minute. Banks: answer twelve story-node questions within five minutes, speak only in archetypes not details, "clearer rather than more complete"; if you are still introducing the background at four and a half minutes, "the investor is already on the phone."
- Walter: **do not send a synopsis to a producer** (if you submit the script and synopsis together, the producer reads only the synopsis; a synopsis of an unfinished script is not taken seriously); if a query letter is met with a request for a synopsis, keep it to one double-spaced page and write it like a trailer.

## IV. The Market's Four Entities and Six Criteria (Hicks)

- **The four entities**: the producer (places bets while trying to avoid risk; a film may pass through more than twelve writers); the director (the first person hired after the script is set, and always has a reason for another revision); the star ("you write for stars and for the stars who might appear"); the public ("they are not like you; they are like you"). All four want the same thing: a good story.
- **The six basic criteria**: an unquestionable single protagonist; a clear problem and external goal; a vivid, focused antagonist; the protagonist is forced to act under a time limit; values are involved in the problem and clearly change by the end; emotional drive takes priority over cleverness.
- The parable of the portrait of his dog Spark: only the first, original painter's signature has the greatest value; "absurd in the portrait world, entirely reasonable and natural in the movie world."
- **Once the writer sells the script to the producer, the script becomes the producer's property**; the average blockbuster costs $60 million; "what appears is not the film the producer wanted to make but the film they were able to complete"; the customer is "the person who goes to work in a suit," and the only question is "How much?"
- Coverage: a reader / story analyst writes a one- to two-page synopsis and evaluates the project by concept, character, craft, and budget; the producer reads the synopsis; most readers are intelligent professionals.
- The WGA receives more than 40,000 scripts a year; Hollywood releases only about 300 films a year.

## V. Agents and Submission (Walter / Hicks / Diamond / Henson)

- **What an agent is not** (Hicks): not an employment agency ("you are the one who sells"), not a nanny, not a banker; **what an agent is**: a businessperson (takes 10%), a team member, a business partner ("Talk to my agent!"), and a source of recognition (most companies do not accept unsolicited material).
- **The query letter** is the only route: the first paragraph is a minimal premise summary that sparks curiosity; the second gives personal background; ask whether they are interested in reading; enclose a self-addressed postcard; target two or three agencies first; **no legitimate agent will ask for a "reading fee."** Walter's experiment: query letters from four classes were sent to agencies on the WGA list; **96% agreed to read**. Sample: "I am an MFA candidate in screenwriting at UCLA. I have written a screenplay called 《幽灵家族》 (Ghost Family), an action/adventure story set in contemporary New York and ninth-century Scotland. May I send you the script for your consideration?" — **no script, no synopsis**, the less said the better; address a specific agent by name (the switchboard name trick); no phone calls, no return receipts, no gimmicks (fortune cookies, carrier pigeons); an old-fashioned paper letter gets noticed (Principle 65).
- **Do not use the phone**: agents ignore most calls but answer all emails; never call to ask whether they received it, never call to say "Don't read what I just sent — I've revised it"; when leaving a message, give your name and number first.
- **Breaking into television** (Walter): write a fan letter to a writer on the show (forwarded through the WGA membership department), praise specific merits, ask about writing habits, **do not say you are a writer and do not ask for an agent introduction** — Principle 63 (every successful writer started with no fame at all), Principle 64 (every writer looks for an excuse to avoid the work in front of him); once a relationship is established, then presume to mention your script.
- Agent commissions are legally 10% (taken only from money actually received; no fees paid upfront); the ninety-day clause; an oral contract is enough; **sign the release without worry** (it has no practical effect); a new writer should choose an aggressive small agency or "a new agent at an old company"; the trap of the big agency's "rotating list"; "be good to the people who helped you on the way up."
- Henson: write at least three or four finished scripts before looking for an agent ("you need a varied inventory"); professionalism is the only standard — "I may be an amateur actor, but my performance cannot be amateurish"; know your script cold and rehearse a one-minute phone summary; it often takes more than three years from script to screen (E.T. took 8 years, 《阿甘正传》 (Forrest Gump) 10).
- Snyder: the order of contact is in-person > phone > query letter > email; after every rejection ask "is there anyone you would recommend?"; the first buyer matters most — take the deal even for little money; do not enter competitions without real judges; the special pitch cases (alarm clocks that ring simultaneously, a radioactive script container) all closed at $1 million.
- The gatekeeper theory: secretaries, receptionists, and readers hold real power far beyond their job titles; "turn them into allies."

## VI. Contracts, Credit, and Money (Hicks / Walter / Masterclass)

- **Option**: an independent producer acquires exclusive rights for about 10% of the future purchase price, then uses the option period to raise money and attach stars and a director; if it expires, the rights revert and the money is kept; **never sign one yourself**: first run it past an agent or entertainment lawyer; always add the line "**the writer must be recognized as a professional writer and covered by the WGA Minimum Basic Agreement**." Walter: the option payment is good for the writer (it publicizes your name, can be collected again and again like an "annuity," and you keep the copyright); reselling = "marked-down goods"; an automatic reversion clause (if unproduced after seven years, the money is refunded and the rights return).
- **Development**: the most likely outcome is that a more experienced writer is hired to rewrite, and you take the full fee but leave the project; or you wait until principal photography begins and are paid in installments.
- **Credit arbitration**: decided entirely by the WGA, regardless of membership; a second writer must contribute more than 50% to be selected; four forms — Written by / Story by / Screen Story by / Screenplay by; "George & Martha" = a team, "George and Martha" = equal contributions but not a team; if you wrote the entire original version you should at least get Story by; **submit supplementary material for arbitration** (Walter's 《美国风情画》 (American Graffiti) lesson: he submitted only the script, not a comparative analysis); the ten creative rights (the original writer has the first rewrite, every revision must be discussed with him, a meeting with the director must be arranged, and he must be invited to the first preview).
- **Money**: contracts customarily specify 5% of net profits — "opportunistic accounting"; **you will never see net profits, only 5% of zero net**; if someone offers gross points, say yes at once; sign a contract that pays cash and sell to the highest bidder; the MBA minimum; separate rights (novel / stage play / sequel) from character / merchandise / ancillary rights, which are negotiated separately; a writer doing work for hire does not own the copyright; a **step deal** has a floor at each stage; an overage bonus; deferred compensation for a beginner = "no payment at all." "Do you need the money, or do you need your name in the end credits?"
- Walter: get as much money as you can ("money is a metaphor with numbers on it, a symbol of how much respect you are given"); **never write for free and never get involved in a project whose copyright you do not own** (a pencil does not write by itself; "a writer should never suffer the humiliation of asking a producer for his fee"; the more the producer pays, the more he values it); leave money matters to your agent; while writing the script, put the contract and the copyright split out of your mind.
- Copyright: exists automatically upon completion; after registration you can seek statutory damages; WGA registration costs $20 and is available to non-members; ideas cannot be protected (Darwin / Wallace); "never be paranoid"; "worry about your characters, dialogue, and story — not about plagiarism."
- True-life stories: consult a lawyer first and sign a "Life Story Option Agreement" — even with relatives.
- The union: join after selling your first script or signing your first paid contract; initiation fee $2,500; "writing the best script you can is the way into the union."
- **Packaging**: the script is the vehicle; the function of commercial elements (stars, directors) is not to make audiences flock but to make banks willing to invest and executives able to deflect responsibility; **Principle 66: the only factor in box-office success is word of mouth.**
- Masterclass (Stantz): partner credit "with" (collaboration) vs. "and" (rewrite); three anonymous WGA judges; "I'm not the boss and you're not the boss — the story is the boss"; film writers do not hold copyright, novelists do; screenwriters have a WGA pension.

## VII. Notes and Rewrites (Multiple Sources)

- Walter's "Yes, but..." truth strategy ("Yes, we can change Latvia, but then we lose this and that"); the fire-hydrant effect (every producer's assistant wants to leave a mark); "the producer subconsciously wants the writer to defend his creative choices... is he a screenwriter or a stenographer?"; nodding along when an executive says "strengthen the protagonist"; red-shirt/green-shirt syndrome and the 5% phantom are both self-sabotage; "keep the anger on the page"; answer a rejection with only "Thank you for your time."
- Masterclass (Richards): gather four or five people into a "jerk circle"; do not defend, only take notes and ask follow-up questions; "your plane never takes off" = there is no real turn from Act One to Act Two; "something strange needs to happen in Act Three" = the protagonist's character is too flat; if several people's feedback differs completely and none will budge = a major problem; "if there is a problem in Act Three, the problem is certainly in Act One"; once shooting begins, defer to the director.
- Banks: "Remember all the edges from the beginning; shrink the weird idea until it fits in the buyer's box, but never reduce the layers of the story"; "bad advice can drive a better idea" (add a dog to the baseball-ghost movie → only children and dogs can see the ghost); **writers who refuse to revise all go broke** (apartments on Hollywood Boulevard vs. Beverly Hills); saying no requires capital (five successful films).
- Hicks: before criticizing a collaborator, say it to yourself first; show it to two friends who will tell the truth; the first professional to read it should be a consultant, not an agent.
- Henson: distinguish nitpicking collaborators (take it as chicken soup) from consistent structural criticism (face the rewrite); if six people read it and five like it, you are safe.
- 梅峰 (Mei Feng) on 《米尔德丽德》 (Mildred Pierce): the producer can determine the direction of the script (flashbacks, adding a murder, cutting the singing career), achieving it by first demanding an outline, then hiring the best writer, hiring a script doctor to write a 62-page critique, and frequently replacing people.

## VIII. Film vs. Television (Bork / Stantz / Walter / Diamond)

- Television is a "series," not a story with an ending: each episode's small problem is solved, while the core big problem and the "unmet need" are never solved; do not make character growth the show's concept; "film is director-driven, television is writer-driven" ("whoever pays the money is in charge").
- Stantz: four narrative modes (anthology / procedural / standalone seasons / serial); end every episode with a 扣 (kòu — a suspense device at the end of a segment); bottle episodes save money to spend at the season finale ("cheap conflict, cheap suspense, but audiences love it"); a style guide unifies multiple directors; television shoots 8–14 days and $3–8 million per episode; television privileges character, film privileges concept (《机械姬》 (Ex Machina) vs. 《西部世界》 (Westworld)); "if you want ten pages rewritten in a day, never hire a film writer"; development changes: platforms order a full season or first three or four episodes.
- Walter: television pays most generously (residuals "flow for the rest of your life"); television treats writers better (a second meeting must be paid $10,000); cable has far more courage than mainstream commercial film; but the freelance writer disappears (writers become employees during development); writing a TV serial requires living in Los Angeles.
- Bork: a one-hour drama without life-and-death stakes or procedural cases especially needs to be "rich, sexy, and glamorous"; the three golden professions of the procedural.
- Diamond: the original-script market has shrunk (173 sold in 1995, 55 in 2010) but it is still the best way for a newcomer to break in; the IP double standard (craving IP yet looking for new voices among unknown writers, then hiring you to write the sequel).
- This section is only an overview from a film writer's perspective. When you actually write a series, turn to the series skills: for the engine and bible, see `sw-series-engine-bible`; for episode and season structure, see `sw-series-structure`; for the writers' room, spec scripts, pitching an episode, job titles, and step deals, see `sw-writers-room`; for half-hour comedy, see `sw-sitcom-comedy`; for Chinese series 立项 (lìxiàng — project approval) / 备案 (bèi'àn — regulatory filing) / review / delivery, see `sw-chinese-series-practice`.

## IX. The Oscars and Market Logic (Mei Feng)

- The screenplay award is more avant-garde than Best Picture and signals potential popular trends; the Academy favors social criticism, a combination of historical sense and humor, low cost with high return, and innovation within recognizable traditional forms; it is wary of horror, comedy, and ensemble experiments; campaign spending ($774,000 by DreamWorks) also affects the screenplay award.
- The year of independent film (1996, the first year no film from the eight major studios was nominated); "it is one thing for a major studio to decide which scripts are good, and another to decide which scripts go into production"; Hollywood is more conservative about scripts than about visuals; for Kaufman-style work, "Hollywood has not yet developed a systematic way to package and sell it."
- A three-way contest (writer / director / producer) determines the final form (《心灵捕手》 (Good Will Hunting) cut its realistic branches to court the mainstream).
- Hitchcock's rights economics: treat each rights acquisition as a challenge to cut costs or even turn a profit; use an intermediary to keep his fame from driving up the price.

## X. Professional Mindset and Habits

- Walter: it is too late to chase the latest trend (Principle 60); the most dangerous strategy is to seek safety (Principle 61); write a script expecting it to sell and you will reap disappointment — "a writer must write for the sake of writing"; do not play the victim; in dry spells, write, write, write, and save; "be grateful for being abused by this industry; far worse than abuse is being ignored"; after a rejection, allow yourself a few days to curl up; "you only need one yes" (《野战排》 (Platoon) was peddled for ten years); being rewritten by someone else is a privilege; "art hurts"; two selves (the artistic and the professional); put yourself inside the heads of agents and producers.
- Diamond: "More haste, less speed" (the script factory wrote eight scripts and sold none); learn at least one lesson from every film.
- Bork: "Stop doubting whether you have talent; remove it from the equation."
- Henson: "Writing is easy; selling is hard"; adapt and improvise + passion; screenwriting is a calling — worry about money later.
- Masterclass: Richards — "you need skin thick enough to take melodramatic blows and sensitivity enough to create"; "making movies is a war with people who do not care about art"; Banks — "they can mistreat me a thousand times; I treat them like my first love"; Stantz — "it took twenty years to become an overnight success"; "the mark of a professional screenwriter is the ability to persuade others to spend money on you" (he does not recommend buying rights with your own money).
- Field: "An overnight success takes fifteen years"; a director can turn a great script into a bad film but can never turn a bad script into a great film.

## XI. Checklists

**Idea checkup before submission**
1. Are the three Cs all present? Have you told it aloud to three people and heard "I have to have this"?
2. Have you gone through five checklist items for each of the seven PROBLEM elements? Are the punitive and the entertaining running neck and neck?
3. Have you written the three logline elements (protagonist / catalyst / mission)? Do you have two or three reference films to go with it?
4. Is it a blockbuster or an execution-dependent independent film? Is it right for me? Have you made the sample film list?

**Before submitting**
5. At least three or four finished scripts? WGA registration? A minimalist PDF cover page?
6. Is the query letter two or three sentences, with no script or synopsis, addressed to a specific agent? Have you targeted two or three first?
7. Have you rehearsed the one-minute spoken summary?
8. Want to break into television? Have you written a fan letter to a writer on the show?

**Before signing a contract**
9. Has the option agreement been through a lawyer? Is the MBA line in it?
10. Will you accept only gross points? Do you want cash and a step deal? Are the separate rights negotiated separately?
11. Are you writing for free or involved in a project whose copyright you do not own?
12. Have you prepared supplementary material for credit arbitration?

**After receiving notes**
13. What is the problem several people agree on? Is it a diagnosis or a prescription? Have you checked it against the one-page synopsis and character chart?
14. Are you ready with "Yes, but..."? Have you kept the edges?
15. After being rewritten or taken off the project: do you still have the sample, and have you started the next idea?
