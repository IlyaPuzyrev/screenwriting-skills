## I. Bork's Complete PROBLEM Seven-Element Self-Check

**Punitive**: ① a big problem that requires the whole story to solve (one that cannot be solved on television at all) ② a goal that is very hard but not impossible ③ in the process of actively solving it, difficulties push the character's abilities to their limit ④ the action produces unforeseen consequences, complexity, and conflict that demand further action ⑤ the problem develops further in the middle until it seems hopeless and only one big showdown can resolve it.
The seven traits of "perfect competition": the contest is extraordinarily difficult and the opponents are evenly matched; every player has a backstory; the value cannot be higher; the home team does not take an early lead and instead runs into unforeseen difficulties; it rallies from crisis with passion and perseverance, but most efforts fail or make things worse; near the end it is clearly behind with no hope, a new plan appears but the goal remains hard; it comes from behind and stands up at the last moment.

**Relevance**: ① the protagonist easily wins favor, is trapped by a problem that earns sympathy, and the process is interesting ② the external life problem is recognized at the most basic, universal human level ③ thoughts, feelings, and desires are clear enough to share ④ each plot line has a specific outcome that the audience supports and feels ⑤ it involves growth and transformation, a complete change, but not simply going from selfish to selfless.
Protagonist flaw list: Luke does not believe in himself or the Force; Josh cannot accept living like a child; George dreams of leaving Bedford Falls and is blind to the happiness he already has; Tess is insecure; Rick believes in nothing; Riggs is suicidal; Elle hides out as a sorority princess; Ray has not made peace with his dead father; William wants recognition from a rock star — the common thread: they are likable but cling to a small, particular way of life that has not come true. Successful protagonists who do not undergo a convoluted change: 《四场婚礼》 (Four Weddings and a Funeral), 《美国之旅》 (Coming to America), 《为人师表》 (Stand and Deliver), 《逃出绝命镇》 (Get Out), 《谍影重重》 (The Bourne Identity), 《我为玛丽狂》 (There's Something About Mary), 《情到深处》 (Say Anything...), 《虎胆龙威》 (Die Hard), 《后窗》 (Rear Window), 《永不妥协》 (Erin Brockovich).

**Originality**: ① follow the basic principles of a viable genre but add interesting, entirely new content ② the central problem or situation has an easy-to-understand yet thought-provoking hook ③ passion for something in the story is my "voice" ④ the concept differs in many ways from similar well-known projects ⑤ the main characters are specific and vivid. Several similar projects in the same market: 1998's 《天地大冲撞》 (Deep Impact) and 《世界末日》 (Armageddon); 1988's 《我又十八》 (18 Again!), 《小爸爸大儿子》 (Like Father Like Son), and 《长大》 (Big); the two Columbus films of 1992.

**Believability**: ① after reading the synopsis you understand it roughly and choose to watch without hesitation ② the characters' traits, decisions, and behavior are true under the specific circumstances ③ the background and "rules of conduct" are explained clearly at the beginning ④ everyone's motivation is clear and connected at the human level ⑤ the entertaining suspense could really happen.

**Life-changing**: ① the risk matches one of the eight levels ② the problem, goal, and action focus mainly on external life ③ the risk exists throughout the story ④ the negative consequences of failure are clear and constantly growing at the personal level ⑤ a once-in-a-lifetime challenge forces fundamental change.

**Entertaining**: ① clear about what emotional entertainment experience is being given to the audience ② fans of the genre get more than their money's worth ③ larger-than-life elements make the story an escapist paradise ④ focus on the fundamental, universal factors that drive emotion ⑤ a continuous experience of surprise, curiosity, and pleasure. Ten emotions: entertainment/laughter, fear, fascination, shock, desire, excitement, awe, romantic love, resonance, eager anticipation.

**Meaningful**: ① explores a universally important question with no simple answer ② the characters are driven by the most fundamental unmet desire ③ the final part makes clear how the character's change makes the journey worthwhile ④ positively affects the audience's lives in a specific way ⑤ does not put attention on theme and growth but reveals them subtly through an engaging plot.

**The Levitan method** (the TV conflict web): choose any two characters → consider their basic life situation → draw a line between them to represent the dynamic "this line is the performance" → ask what happens when the two play a scene together, how the interaction creates problems, and how it reveals each one's big problem → choose another pair and repeat. 《摩登家庭》 (Modern Family) has ten characters, paired in every combination (Manny likes the pretty older sister; Phil wants to impress a father-in-law who never respects him).

## II. Walter's Three Query-Letter Samples

**Sample 1**: "Dear Mr. Lasfarg: I am an MFA candidate in screenwriting at UCLA. I have written a screenplay called 《幽灵家族》 (Ghost Family), an action/adventure story set in contemporary New York and ninth-century Scotland. May I send you the script for your consideration? Sincerely, ×××" — the first paragraph introduces the writer in one sentence; the second gives only background and style; sent to several legitimate agencies; every one replied enthusiastically; Fox paid $4 million.

**Sample 2**: "Dear Mr. Lasfarg, I am a probation officer in Dayton, Ohio. I have written a screenplay called 《孤注一掷》 (All or Nothing). It is a tender comedy about a sailor who goes AWOL to search for his long-lost father. May I send you the script for your consideration?" — "Inexperienced writers are all too eager to tell people what the script is about; tease the agent into wanting to know more. The less you say, the better."

**Sample 3**: "Dear Mr. Lasfarg, on January 4, 2004, when I walked into my apartment, I found my fiancée having sex with my roommate. She was handcuffed to the bedpost with honey smeared on her breasts. Unfortunately, discovering them led to my arrest and imprisonment. Six years later, I still have not escaped the case. But you know, lemons and lemonade are not the same thing." — find the seductive hook in your own unique experience; a two- or three-sentence old-fashioned letter.

**The rules**: no script and no synopsis (if a synopsis is requested, no more than one double-spaced page, written like a trailer); address a specific agent by name (the switchboard name trick: "Please put me through to Mr. Barry Stein" — "We don't have a Barry Stein, but we do have a Harry Steinberg"); no phone calls, no return receipts, no gimmicks; once they agree, send the script with a short note saying only that it was sent at their request; the experiment: 96% agreed to read.

**Hicks's query letter**: the first paragraph is a minimal premise summary that arouses curiosity ("the story of a young man who used a home computer to break into the Defense Department's computers and nearly started World War III"); the second paragraph gives personal background (state specific expertise, not your entire résumé); ask whether they are interested in reading and promise to send it by express mail; enclose a postcard with your address printed and a stamp affixed — "Yes, I am interested in reading your script. ____ signature"; wait six to eight weeks, and if there is no reply after two months, send a polite reminder; target two or three first.

**The TV fan-letter method** (Walter): praise ("Your [show] changed my life") → a specific merit ("When she said... he answered... I nearly fell out of my La-Z-Boy; the remote fell to the floor and my Schnauzer immediately picked it up") → do not say you are a writer and do not ask for an agent introduction → ask about writing habits ("Do you write with a pencil on a yellow legal pad, or on a computer?"); once a relationship is established, presume: "Inspired by you, I wrote a script for one episode; I never told you because I didn't want you to misunderstand..."

## III. Hicks: Market and Contract Essentials

- **The four steps of pitching**: create atmosphere and mood; clarify the protagonist (use metaphor, not lengthy physical description); capture the basic conflict (the problem of the first ten minutes); create a one-liner that hooks ("a convoy in space"). In the meeting, speak rather than read, do not memorize, watch their eyes, work as a pair like a sports broadcast (one narrates the process, one gives in-depth commentary), and be brief.
- **The option agreement** must include: "For the purposes of this agreement, the writer must be recognized as a professional writer and is covered by the minimum basic agreement of the Writers Guild of America." The fee is about 10% of the purchase price; during the term the script may not be shopped; the rights revert at expiration.
- **Development**: ideally the company provides development funds and the producer asks you to rewrite; most likely a more experienced writer is hired to rewrite, and you take the full fee and leave; or payment is staged until principal photography begins.
- **The four credit forms**: Written by (one person created the story and the screenplay); Story by (provided only the story); Screen Story by (provided source material such as newspaper or magazine articles); Screenplay by (wrote the final version but from someone else's earlier material); "&" = a team, "and" = equal contributions but not a team; a second writer must contribute more than 50%.
- **The ten creative rights** (1988 MBA): no dissemination of coverage without consent; the right to restrict forwarding to third parties; the original writer has the first rewrite; every revision must be discussed with him; participation in discussions of style, locations, and casting; a meeting with the director must be arranged; travel first class; see the film before the final cut; an invitation to the first Los Angeles preview; a videotape must be provided.
- **Money**: 5% of net = 5% of zero net; say yes immediately to gross points; MBA minimums (about $45,000 for budgets under $25 million, about $85,000 above that, rising annually); separate rights (novel / stage play / sequel); character / merchandise / ancillary rights negotiated separately; a writer doing work for hire does not own copyright; a step deal has a floor at each stage; an overage bonus; deferred compensation for a beginner = zero payment.
- **An agent is not** an employment agency / a nanny / a banker; **an agent is** a businessperson (10%) / a team member / a business partner / a source of recognition; check the WGA's Agency Franchise List; new writers cannot get into the giant agencies; mid-size and small agencies are known in specific fields; no legitimate agent asks for a "reading fee."
- **Entertainment lawyers** charge $200–300 an hour to review contracts; **personal managers** take 15% and are not bound by the WGA.
- **True-life stories**: consult a lawyer first and sign a "Life Story Option Agreement"; even with relatives; public figures have rights, including heirs; the privacy rights of non-public figures are extremely important.
- **Copyright**: owned upon completion; after registration (two copies of the script, $20, application form) you can seek statutory damages and attorney's fees.
- **The parable of the portrait of his dog Spark**: pay a portrait painter to paint the dog → add color yourself → a second painter covers it with white paint and paints a comic strip → a third cuts a hole and inserts a plaster head with a clown collar — only the first, original painter's signature has the greatest value.

## IV. Diamond & Weissman: Handling Notes and Terminology

- Three kinds of notes: enormously helpful; usable or not; usable, but you need not follow the other person's method. The "old car" story: a Fox executive said the three boys needed their own goals in Act One (a good note), but the specific idea was "an old car" → they politely declined without offering a persuasive alternative → "It's the old car, gentlemen."
- Mark Gordon, on a walk, said bluntly that 《神童》 (Little Man Tate) could not be made: "Twelve-year-olds no longer want to see movies about twelve-year-olds; they all want to see 《生死时速》 (Speed)."
- Submission path: friends first → pick one producer and one executive → a referral to a familiar agent; "not through connections, not through competition, but through relationships."
- Script competitions: the Academy Nicholl Fellowship changes lives; famous film-festival competitions; obscure local competitions do not help; paying to upload is not necessarily bad, but do not go into debt.
- Terminology: Amp Up (a high-frequency Hollywood word); Buzz (can launch a career even if the project is never filmed); Coverage (a new writer's script is first "covered"); "depends on execution" (the excuse for rejecting you); Meld (plot integration); Runner (the comic-relief character); Set Piece; Slugline (scene heading); Spec (an original screenplay; even if it does not sell, it serves as a calling-card writing sample); Tentpole; writing sample.
- Batting average: 47 projects, 15 filmed (0.319).

**The 《美国风情画》 (American Graffiti) Lesson**: he wrote the first draft in nine days; Lucas said not a word; the agency said, "Nine days' work, $7,500 in your pocket — don't think about it anymore"; at arbitration he submitted no supplementary material, and his name did not appear; 1.67% of net profits exceeded $1 million; afterward he became a top writer in the teen genre; "attaching expectations to any action only leads to frustration; if you expect your script to sell while writing it, you will reap disappointment."

## VI. Masterclass: Pitching and Credit (Four Hollywood Lectures)

- Richards: put a one-line logline (two or three sentences is fine) plus two or three comparable films in the script file ("《教父》 (The Godfather) plus 《速激 7》 (Fast & Furious 7)"); he has written 150 loglines; when you bring a one- or two-page synopsis to a financing meeting, you may include a line or two of dialogue — "it should read like the film flashing by; leave space"; WGA registration is $20 per project and available to non-members; copyright registration is $35; 《撒哈拉》 (Sahara) had seven writers who did not collaborate, and the WGA decided credit (each submitted a statement).
- Banks: answer twelve story-node questions within five minutes (situation? genre? main character and archetype? core problem? catalyst on page 10? act break on page 30? drive and theme on page 45? midpoint turn on page 60? end of sequence on page 75? Act Two turn? end of Act Three?) — speak only in archetypes, not details; Group A is still introducing the background at four and a half minutes — "the investor is already on the phone"; "clearer rather than more complete"; writers who refuse revisions live in Hollywood Boulevard apartments; those willing to revise live in Beverly Hills; "remember all the edges from the beginning; shrink the idea until it fits in the buyer's box, but do not reduce the layers of the story."
- Stantz: partner credit "with" (collaboration) vs. "and" (rewrite); three anonymous senior judges rank contributions; "I'm not the boss and you're not the boss — the story is the boss"; film writers do not hold copyright; novelists do; "the mark of a professional screenwriter is the ability to persuade others to spend money on you"; introduce a two-hour film in one minute — tell it to your spouse, practice in front of a mirror, write the outline word for word, anticipate questions; original scripts are hard to finance: keep the required investment low, build around a terrific character, and attract a well-known director or actor.
- Film vs. television production: film $100–300 million, 80–120 days plus reshoots; television 8–14 days and $3–8 million per episode (《权游》 (Game of Thrones) topped out at $40 million); bottle episodes; development changes: platforms order a full season or first three or four episodes.

## VII. Henson: Breaking-In Essentials

- Agent Paul Small: "Watch for the moment and adapt to circumstances; that is always the rule"; "when you go to see an agent, your inventory must be varied" — at least three or four finished scripts.
- Beloff: "Never get money on the brain; if you choose screenwriting, go all the way and worry about money later"; "it hurts to watch a script you love rot, but business is business."
- Duchi: "Only by knowing your opponent can you hit the vital spot"; passion and a sense of mission like "a fire in the chest."
- The phone summary: know the script cold, list the main line and outline, summarize the most exciting scenes, rehearse privately several times; "if you cannot even summarize your own script, how will you handle the whole project?"
- The first contract often states payment on the first day of shooting (E.T. took 8 years, 《阿甘正传》 (Forrest Gump) 10, 《人鬼情未了》 (Ghost) 11); "do not abandon your specialty."

## VIII. Snyder: Breaking In

- Finding an agent: ambition (putting up flyers to get public television to broadcast 《空白秀》 (The Blank Show)) and fate (a girlfriend met in a bar; her friend became an agent).
- The order of contact: in-person > phone > query letter > email; after every rejection ask "is there anyone you would recommend?"; the first buyer matters most — take the deal even for little money.
- Networking: film festivals, producer seminars, writing groups, becoming an expert by writing film reviews, coming to Los Angeles as a script reader, a personal website.
- Don't: script competitions (an echo chamber, unless the judges have really sold scripts); stupid gimmicks (mailing yourself in a wooden crate to William Morris).
- Special pitch cases: 《天才小麻烦》 (Leave It to Beaver) — the original show's lead actress delivered milk and cookies; 《嘀嗒人》 (The Ticking Man) — alarm clocks that rang simultaneously were sent to every executive; 《核子家庭》 (Nuclear Family) — a radioactive script container; 《小鬼富翁》 (Blank Check) — a backpack filled with $1 million in toy money — all closed at $1 million.
- "It is what it is": after doing your best, leave it to fate.

## IX. 梅峰 (Mei Feng): Oscar Data Points

- 《美国美人》 (American Beauty): $5 million invested, $130 million gross; DreamWorks spent $774,000 on campaigning in the four weeks after the nominations were announced (advertising space in three major film publications, twice as much as any other company); in the 1990s only two Best Pictures also won Best Original Screenplay (the other was 《莎翁情史》 (Shakespeare in Love)).
- 1996, the year of independent film: for the first time no film from the eight major studios was nominated for Best Picture; 《法尔戈》 (Fargo) received seven nominations but lost Best Picture to 《英国病人》 (The English Patient), won Original Screenplay and Best Actress, and took Best Director at Cannes the same year.
- 1991: 《沉默的羔羊》 (The Silence of the Lambs) received seven nominations and won Best Picture (unprecedented for a horror thriller); 《美女和野兽》 (Beauty and the Beast) became the first animated film nominated for Best Picture; 23-year-old Singleton was the youngest and first Black director nominated; 《塞尔玛与路易丝》 (Thelma & Louise), with about $2 million in MGM marketing, became a Time magazine cover in its opening week.
- 《纯洁心灵的永恒阳光》 (Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind) cost $2 million; Kaufman had already been nominated three times (losing to 《美国美人》 (American Beauty) in 1999 and to 《钢琴师》 (The Pianist) in 2002); "Hollywood has not yet developed a systematic way to package and sell this kind of film."
- Hitchcock's rights economics: bought 《知道太多的人》 (The Man Who Knew Too Much) for £250 and sold it for twice that; bought 《捉贼记》 (To Catch a Thief) for $15,000 and sold it to Paramount twenty years later for $105,000; used an intermediary to negotiate lower prices: $7,500 for 《火车怪客》 (Strangers on a Train), $11,000 for 《怪尸案》 (The Trouble with Harry), $9,000 for 《精神病患者》 (Psycho).
