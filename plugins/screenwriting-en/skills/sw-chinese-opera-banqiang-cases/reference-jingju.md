## §1 Scene-by-Scene Structure of 《锁麟囊》 (The Jewelry Purse) and the Two Preliminary Tables

**Conventions of this table**: scenes are identified only as “Scene One ... Scene Twelve”; **no scene titles are given** (“Spring-Autumn Pavilion,” “Vermilion Tower,” and the like appear only in the composition notes). Before each aria the score marks “(sings [metrical pattern])” or “(continues singing [metrical pattern])”; **metrical-pattern names (板式, bǎnshì — the metrical pattern of a banqiang aria) are set in 〔〕 and percussion patterns in 【】**; stage business and blocking are written in parentheses beneath the score line, sometimes with footnotes. The percussion patterns (锣鼓经, luógǔjīng) that appear in the body text are: 【撕锣】 (sīluó), 【大锣凤点头】 (dàluó fèngdiǎntóu), 【小锣夺头】 (xiǎoluó duótóu), 【纽丝】 (niǔsī), 【住头】 (zhùtóu), 【回头】 (huítóu), 【冲头】 (chōngtóu), 【乱锤】 (luànchuí), 【长锤】 (chángchuí), 【小锣抽头】 (xiǎoluó chōutóu), 【小锣打下】 (xiǎoluó dǎxià), 【大锣水底鱼】 (dàluó shuǐdǐyú), 【大锣乱锤】 (dàluó luànchuí), 【扑灯蛾】 (pūdēng'é), 【撞金钟】 (zhuàngjīnzhōng), 【扫头】 (sǎotóu), 【快纽丝】 (kuài niǔsī), 【哑笛】 (yǎdí), 【二黄哑笛】 (èrhuáng yǎdí).

**Reading**: four scenes have no singing (1, 4, 6, and 8); **there are only 20 on-the-beat sections (上板段, shàngbǎn duàn — on-the-beat sections; 二六, èrliù — two-six pattern; 原板, yuánbǎn — primary pattern; 流水, liúshuǐ — flowing pattern; 慢板, mànbǎn — slow pattern; 四平调, sìpíngdiào — four-level tune), all concentrated in Scenes 2, 5, 9, and 12**—the four scenes with on-the-beat passages are exactly the four key scenes; the other eight scenes use only sanban (散板, sǎnbǎn — free-rhythm pattern) and yaoban (摇板, yáobǎn — shaking pattern).

### 1.2 Preliminary “Costumes of the Protagonist” (p0006, listed by scene)

Scene 2 (before the wedding): a bean-green embroidered 褶子 (xuézi — a woman's informal robe) and a 点翠头面 (diǎnxuì tóumiàn — kingfisher-feather headdress); Scene 5 (sheltering from the rain at the Spring-Autumn Pavilion): a red cape, with four kingfisher-feather strips and red velvet flowers added; Scene 9 (returning home after the wedding): a pink cape and kingfisher-feather headdress; Scene 12 (begging for gruel after the flood): a pale lake-blue plain robe with embroidered borders, plus a matching head-wrap; same scene (after entering the Lu household as a servant): add a long black vest embroidered with three-green plum blossoms, plus a waist sash; same scene (at the family reunion): a rose-red or date-red embroidered robe and kingfisher-feather headdress.

→ **The suspense device (关子, guānzi) of “change costume first, reveal the name later” is fixed in the script in the form of a “costume table.”**

### 1.3 Preliminary “Props” (砌末, qièmò, p0006)

Two large gongs; four or six large red banners with the double-happiness character; red cloth robes with the double-happiness character worn by the attendants; one pair of flower vases; one Jewelry Purse; one pair of colorful shoes; one white handkerchief; one rubber ball; one pair of scissors; one red paper figure; one yellow paper butterfly; one green paper horse; one rattle drum; one small horse whip; one small trumpet; one small winnowing basket; one large gruel bucket; one iron ladle; one small bamboo basket; five gruel bowls.

→ The paper figure, paper butterfly, paper horse, rattle drum, small trumpet, and ball all serve Scene 12, “soothing Lin'er”; **a single scene claims half of the props table**.

**Closing stage direction (p0090, footnote 1)**: “Both sleeves thrust diagonally upward toward the entrance-door side of the stage front; three steps back; three return bows; turn the right sleeve and help Madam Lu to her feet; hold the pose (亮住, liàngzhù). Exit.” Then 〔the suona plays the 【尾声】 (wěishēng — coda) tune; all exit together.〕 **The curtain falls. The play ends. The entire play has no exit poem, no “Thus it is” (正是), and no spoken couplet (念对儿).**

### 2.2 Metrical-Pattern Statistics (counted by markings, duplicates merged)

| Vocal system (声腔, shēngqiāng) | Metrical pattern | Count | Primary use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Erhuang | sanban | 10 | Servants' entrances, recognizing the purse, the madam's questioning—narrative and shock |
| Erhuang | sipingdiao | 3 | **Given only to Xue Xiangling before her marriage**—spoiled, fussy, pleased with herself |
| Erhuang | manban → kuai sanyan | 1 (a long passage) | **The play's longest lyric self-reflection**; the duoju are here |
| Xipi | sanban | 17 | Flight, begging for gruel, the final revelation—the most common “transition and exposition” pattern |
| Xipi | yaoban | 10 | The poor family's entrances, the return home six years later, recognizing the benefactor |
| Xipi | erliu | 2 | **Only twice: the opening aria at the Spring-Autumn Pavilion and the costume-change entrance in the final scene**—one at each end |
| Xipi | liushui | 9 | Giving the purse, listing its contents, the closing—**each time following erliu or yuanban** |
| Xipi | yuanban | 5 | Once for six years of peace; four sections in the three yields of the seat |
| Xipi | kutou | 2 independent + 6 embedded | Calling names, always attached to sanban or erliu |

**Four rules of arrangement**: ① **the first four scenes divide by vocal system, not by moral good and bad** (the rich side is all erhuang (二黄, èrhuáng — the deeper, more solemn vocal system), the poor side all xipi; from Scene 5 on only xipi remains, and erhuang returns once for “recognizing the purse”)—**vocal system is used to divide characters and scenes, not to grade emotional intensity**; ② **〔二六〕 is used only twice in the whole play, in symmetrical positions**, and both times it leads directly into 〔流水〕; ③ **sanban and yaoban account for 27 of 65 = 40 percent**, while on-the-beat passages number only 20 and are all concentrated in the four key scenes; ④ **the rhyme category changes scene by scene, and the first and last share one rhyme** (the good deed in Scene 5 and the good reward in the closing both rhyme on Yáotiáo, while Jiāngyáng, Yánqián, and Rénchén give way in between).

### 2.4 Six Duoju Passages (垛句, duǒjù — inserted short lines; full specification)

① “Gather up the last resentment / forgo the petulant sulk / start anew / change your nature / do not cling to the passing water / turn back from the sea of suffering / awaken early to the root of karma” (收余恨／免娇嗔／且自新／改性情／休恋逝水／苦海回身／早悟兰因) (3-3-3-3-4-4-4; **24 characters standing in for one second line**; erhuang kuai sanyan); ② “Explain it carefully from beginning to end” (从头至尾仔细的说明) (17 characters; **the only case in sanban**); ③ “The wind breaks, the rain roars, the thunder crashes, the music fades, voices shout” (风声断雨声喧雷声乱乐声阑珊人声呐喊) (xipi yuanban); ④ “A short flowered curtain, an old flowered valance, uneven tassels, broken and incomplete” (短花帘旧花幔参差流苏残破不全) (18 characters; yuanban); ⑤ “Like the cuckoo crying in another courtyard, like the grieving apes of the Ba Gorge, it plucks the heartstrings” (似杜鹃啼别院巴峡哀猿动人心弦) (24 characters; yuanban); ⑥ “Red-gold chain, purple-jade hairpin, white-jade ring, double-phoenix hairpin, eight-treasure bracelet” (赤金链紫瑛簪白玉环双凤钗八宝钗钏) (**26 characters, the longest in the play**; liushui).

**Specification**: **all six fall in on-the-beat passages (manban / kuai sanyan / yuanban / liushui) and are always added to the second line**; three-character beats and four-character beats are mixed; if the duoju are restored to the paired-line scheme, they still form one complete second line (in the words of Cheng Yanqiu [程砚秋], “they look uneven, but in fact they remain unified within the syntactic law of the female role's lyrics”).

### 2.5 List of Conventions

| Convention | Use in this play | Example (page) |
|---|---|---|
| **Entrance poem (定场诗, dìngchǎngshī)** | Given only to chou roles, and **spoken as split couplets**; never given to the protagonist | Young Master of Ceremonies: “A flower stuck in my hair, / a happy event comes to their house” / Old Master of Ceremonies: “I learned to be a wedding MC, / forever busy for other people” (p0007); Meixiang: “All day I spin in circles, / not knowing for whom I am busy” (p0036) |
| **Self-introduction (自报家门, zìbào jiāmén)** | Three instances, all in chou and laosheng roles, **always immediately after the entrance poem** | Xue Liang (p0009); Hu Po (p0042); Lu Ren and Lu Yi in alternating speech (p0042) |
| **Offstage call (幕内叫, mùnèijiào; 帷灯匣剑, wéi dēng xiá jiàn)** | Before the protagonist appears: more than ten lines of “(offstage speech)” + four calls of “I'm coming” (转来) + two five-character poems describing embroidery patterns | p0010–0011: “Mandarin ducks in five colors, / bright feathers through clear waves; / do not embroider the toe of the shoe, / beware of wearing it away in walking”; “The setting must be like a painting, / set off by a red lotus; / the lotus heart in gold thread, / the petals in cinnabar” |
| **Exit poem / ensemble-scene “Thus it is” / spoken couplet** | **Not used once in the whole play**; the final scene instead uses 【流水】 + stage business + a suona 【尾声】 tune | p0090 |

### 2.6 The Actual Blocking of the “Three Yields of the Seat” (p0068–0078)

1. Zhao Shouzhen says, “Give Mama Xue a seat” → Biyu: “In your presence, how could there be a seat for her?” → she slowly drags over a chair and **sets it down hard**; Biyu then frightens her with “The chair is made of wood, wood can make fire, mind you don't get burned” → **Xue Xiangling makes the first yield**.
2. After singing the second section (the shabby bridal sedan) → Zhao Shouzhen: “**Move Mama Xue's seat to the guest's place**” → Biyu: “A servant woman, it's enough to have a seat at all—how could she sit in the guest's place?” → **the second yield; she quickly crosses to the chair at the exit side and sits**.
3. After singing the fourth section (“though the purse is small, it can serve as a spring of life”) → Zhao Shouzhen: “Quickly **move Mama Xue's seat to the seat of honor**” → Biyu: “Come along now, **every step a promotion**. Please sit!” → **the third yield; she takes the center seat**.

**Mechanism**: before each yield, one section of 【西皮原板】 must be sung to the end; each move of the seat is accompanied by **Biyu** stepping forward to make a sarcastic remark (the comedy remains with the supporting role), while the protagonist only performs the stage business of “yielding.”

### 2.7 The “Soothing Lin'er” Scene (p0049–0063, the play's only scene dominated by acting)

Prop chain: rattle drum → small trumpet (both thrown down) → cutting a red paper figure → cutting a green paper horse → **Lin'er demands that Xue Xiangling “be a horse”** → catching a butterfly → cutting a paper butterfly → Lin'er falls asleep → the ball. The whole passage is underpinned by **〔二黄哑笛〕 (èrhuáng yǎdí — erhuang muted flute)**, and **each spoken line is written beneath the score as one measure, not sung**.

Zhao Shouzhen gives Mama Xue four rhymed instructions (p0050): “**Beware the goldfish pond, take care at the Taihu rocks, do not stir up the bees' sting, do not pick at the spider's web**.” Squire Lu eagerly recites them for her, adds “The pine branches have grown long; do not let them scratch the young master,” and only then says the one thing that really matters: “**Only the Vermilion Tower in the east corner... must not be entered. If you disobey my order, you will be punished without mercy.**”

## §6 《沙家浜》 (Shajiabang): “Battle of Wits” Line by Line, Metrical-Constraint Table, Thirteen-Rhyme-Category Table, and List of Rule-Breakings

### 6.1 The Ten Aside Lines of “Battle of Wits” (p0031–0033, complete transcription)

| No. | Singer | Metrical pattern (as bracketed in the original book) | First / second line | Lyric |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Diao Deyi | fan xipi yaoban | **first** | This woman is no ordinary woman (这个女人不寻常), |
| 2 | Sister Aqing | (continues singing; no marking) | **second** | What devilish scheme does Diao Deyi have (刁德一有什么鬼心肠)? |
| 3 | Hu Chuankui | (continues singing, “xipi yaoban”) | **first** | This little Diao doesn't show me the least face (这小刁一点面子也不讲)! |
| 4 | Sister Aqing | (continues singing, “fan xipi yaoban”) | **second** | This straw bag is actually a wall against the wind (这草包倒是一堵挡风的墙). |
| — | [Speech] Diao Deyi (opens his cigarette case) smokes. / Hu Chuankui: She doesn't smoke—what are you doing! | — | — | — |
| 5 | Diao Deyi | (continues singing, “xipi liushui”) | **first** | Her manner is neither humble nor haughty (她态度不卑又不亢), |
| 6 | Sister Aqing | (continues singing, “xipi yaoban”) | **second** | His expression is neither gloomy nor sunny (他神情不阴又不阳). |
| 7 | Hu Chuankui | (continues singing) | **first** | What devil's trick is Diao Deyi playing (刁德一搞的什么鬼花样)? |
| 8 | Sister Aqing | (continues singing, “xipi liushui”) | **second** | Are they surnamed Chiang or Wang after all (他们到底姓蒋还是姓汪)? |
| 9 | Diao Deyi | (continues singing, “xipi yaoban”) | **first** | I mean to sound her out with indirection (我待要旁敲侧击将她访). |
| 10 | Sister Aqing | (continues singing) | **second** | I must watch his words and looks and guard against him (我必须察言观色把他防). |

**Three rules**: ① **every one of Sister Aqing's lines falls in the second-line position** (lines 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10), all five pairs without exception—Fan Junhong: “the main side's lines should always be placed in the second-line position... that shows she occupies the position of striking only after the other has struck”; ② **the lines are paired in antithesis** (5/6 and 9/10 have the same character count, the same pauses, the same structure, and opposite meanings)—“the fiercer the conflict, the stricter the syntactic requirement; the main point is regularity and antithesis”; ③ **metrical pattern carries the distinction between characters**: Diao Deyi opens with **fan xipi yaoban** (cold and sinister), Hu Chuankui always uses xipi yaoban/liushui (the orthodox mode), and Sister Aqing uses both; **fan xipi appears only here in the whole play**.

### 6.2 “Build the Seven-Star Stove” (xipi liushui, 10 lines)

> Build the seven-star stove, / the copper pot boils the three rivers, / set out the eight-immortals table, / entertain guests from sixteen directions. / All who come are guests, / everything depends on one mouth, / meet and smile, / afterward think no more, / once a person leaves, the tea goes cold, / what thoroughness is there to be thorough about. (垒起七星灶，／铜壶煮三江，／摆开八仙桌，／招待十六方。／来的都是客，／全凭嘴一张，／相逢开口笑，／过后不思量，／人一走，茶就凉，／有什么周详不周详。)

**Line pattern**: the first eight lines are all **five-character lines** (the banqiang standard [板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ — a system of paired lines varied by metrical pattern] is seven or ten characters; five-character lines are a rule-breaking variant); the ninth line, “Once a person leaves, the tea goes cold,” is a **three-plus-three** duoju; the last line returns to a seven-character pattern that fits the tune. **Numerical parallelism**: seven-star stove / three rivers / eight-immortals table / sixteen directions—four numbers in four lines, with marketplace idiom going straight into song. **The whole passage does not answer the question**; it only lays out the rules of the teahouse; the closing line throws the question back with a same-character rhetorical question. After the song, the opponent comments in speech: “**Sister Aqing, you are truly worthy of being a teahouse keeper—not a drop leaks from what you say. Admirable! Admirable!**”—**a three-part pattern of song—speech—commentary**.

**Addendum on technique (not included in SKILL.md Section 2, item ⑥)**: **famous passages often come from places that “do not sound like revolutionary language”—preserve the character's trade idiom and marketplace idiom.** Wang Zengqi (汪曾祺) said that this passage survived “by outwardly complying while inwardly opposing.” *Failure condition*: the trade idiom must be the character's real professional language; a proverb pasted on will give itself away. *Self-check*: if this aria were given to another character on the same side, would it immediately stop working?

### 6.3 The Interrogation Passage (#18): One Yaoban Cut into Five Segments by Speech

> Hu: I ask you about this New Fourth Army— / A: Oh, the New Fourth Army. Yes, yes, yes! (sings “xipi yaoban”) **Commander, why ask in such detail? Many New Fourth Army troops have stayed here.**
> Hu: They stayed here—were there wounded? / A: Yes! (continues singing) **There were quite a few wounded, some seriously and some lightly.**
> Hu: Where did they live? / A: (continues singing) **In our village, every household has housed many people. Even in my little teahouse, people often come to drink tea, fill their kettles, and wash their towels.**
> Diao: And now? / A: Now! (continues singing) **At one assembly order, off they went in a mighty column, out of the village!**
> Hu: The wounded left too? / A: The wounded? (continues singing) **The wounded have vanished without a trace—flown far away and hard to find!**

**Each time, Sister Aqing first repeats the other's question with a single spoken word (“Yes!” “Now!” “The wounded?”) before going on to sing**—the repetition both takes up the conversation and gives her a place to start singing. **Yaoban (in the sanban family) has no fixed beat or eye (板眼, bǎnyǎn — the metrical beats), so it can start and stop at any time; if it were changed to yuanban or erliu, the cutting would be impossible.**

**How to write “jiàosàn”**: it is marked in the middle of the last line of an aria (“The Communist Party is like his own mother (jiàosàn)!” / “It is like boiling water poured on an anthill (jiàosàn), fire scorching a beehive!”). There are five instances in the play, and the pattern is always “... (jiàosàn) + two to four characters”—**the playwright must leave a verb-object structure in the last line that can be prolonged in the melisma.**

### 6.5 Table of the Thirteen Rhyme Categories (十三辙, shísān zhé) as Used in the Play

| Rhyme category | Broad / risky | Number of passages | Where used | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jiāngyáng | broad | 15 | Recalling suffering, “Morning Glow,” all the duets in “Battle of Wits,” denouncing the enemy, the surprise attack | **The play's main rhyme**; confrontation, denunciation, heroic feeling, and group scenes all use Jiāngyáng |
| Yánqián | broad | 7 | Sending out scouts, “The wind is sharp,” coded messages, singing the enemy's deployment | **The rhyme for “explaining a plan”**—four passages all set out concrete arrangements |
| Rénchén | broad | 5 | Rendezvous, transition, interrogation, instructions, entrance | **The rhyme for short arias and transition arias**; four of the five passages do not exceed 8 lines |

| Huáilái | **risky** | 1 | Diao Deyi's attempt to persuade her to surrender | **The only risky rhyme is given to the villain's persuasion scene** (开 kāi, 白 bái, 迈 mài, 排 pái, 派 pài, 台 tái, 耐 nài, 台 tái, 米 mǐ) |

**Four criteria**: ① **one rhyme category per scene is the rule; a change of rhyme means a change of scene or a change of plot line**; ② **asides and duets must share one rhyme** (the ten lines of “Battle of Wits,” alternating among three characters, all rhyme on Jiāngyáng); ③ **a risky rhyme is used only once, and given to a supporting character's independent aria**; the protagonist's long passages always use broad rhymes; ④ **ensemble song chooses the most resonant broad rhyme**, and its 14 lines do not change rhyme.

### 6.6 List of Rule-Breaking Line Patterns (only 6 in 36 arias)

- **Consecutive five-character lines**: the eight lines of “Build the seven-star stove... afterward think no more” in Scene 4.
- **Three-character duoju**: “Once a person leaves, the tea goes cold” in Scene 4; “But alas, hard to escape, with running dogs and hawks” in Scene 6; “My heart is at ease, my body is plump, I cannot walk the road, I cannot climb the mountain” in Scene 2.
- **Three-character repeated lines**: “What to do, what to do, what to do?” in Scene 6.
- **Jiaotou (叫头, jiàotóu — an emotional cry cue)**: “The Party!” in Scene 6 stands on its own line and is not counted as a line.
- **Overlong line**: “Enduring every hardship, marching east to the south of the Yangtze, penetrating deep behind enemy lines, liberating cities and villages” in Scene 7 (a four-part line).
- **A numerical phrase as the opening line**: “August 13, the Japanese invaders fought in Shanghai” in Scene 7.

→ **Rule-breaking has a price; leave at most one instance in a single aria**; four of this play's six rule-breakings occur in the two famous arias “Build the Seven-Star Stove” and “The Wind Is Sharp.”

### 6.7 The Use of Conventions in This Play (Reforming Conventions for a Modern-Dress Play)

- **Self-introduction**: **Hu Chuankui is the only character in the play** who has one (he enters in Scene 3 and sings four lines of 〔西皮散板〕 giving his background / armed strength / position / relationships); **he does not give his name** (Diao Deyi introduces it in the next spoken line), **he does not face the audience**, and he uses sanban rather than dry recitation. Positive characters never introduce themselves—Chen Tianmin introduces Sister Aqing in speech. **Rule: a modern-dress play keeps the “information density” of self-introduction but cancels its “speaking to the audience.”**
- **Entrance poem / entrance verse**: none in the play. The only remnant is the four lines that Diao and Hu **recite in alternation** in Scene 7 (rhyming on Jiāngyáng, with lines of uneven length); it comes after the curtain rises and tells “what happened between the previous scene and this one” as fast as possible.
- **Exit poem**: zero in the play. Three substitutes—**a sung line to close** (Scene 8), **speech to close** (Scenes 3 and 7), and **lights/curtain to close** (Scenes 2 and 5).
- **Asides**: three instances—a three-person aside (Scene 4, **asides and direct speech alternate within the same passage**); a solo aside (Scene 6, speech first and then song, **speech lists the facts and song releases the feeling**); and a residue of bodily grammar (“look at the back—turn around” followed by direct speech).
- **Entrances and exits**: **seven of the ten scenes begin with the characters already onstage** (2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 9, and 10); only Scenes 1, 6, and 8 use an entrance. **But two of the oldest conventions are kept: entering to an offstage daoban** (Grandma Sha in Scene 7, Guo Jianguang in Scene 8).
- **Transitions, offstage speech, and fights**: transition scenes convey the larger situation without dialogue; **there are more than ten instances of offstage speech (内白, nèibái — calls from behind the curtain)**; in Scene 7 the shooting of Wang Fugen (王福根) and Old Liu (刘老头, Liú Lǎotóu) **is carried out entirely by offstage shouts + gunshots; the men do not appear**; **the fight is given in a single line**: “The fight begins; the assault platoon wipes out Hu Chuankui's troops.”
- **Script format**: the character's name begins a new line at the left margin, and the dialogue begins another new line; metrical-pattern markings are written in parentheses after the name (“(sings ‘xipi yaoban’)”, “(continues singing ‘fan xipi yaoban’)”); **a mid-passage change of metrical pattern is marked at the end of the line where the change occurs**, and jiàosàn is marked in the middle of the last line; stage business (科介, kējiè — action and business cues written into the script) either occupies a line of its own in 〔 〕 or is inserted in （ ）; **role types, percussion patterns, and costumes are not marked**, and the vocal score is appended at the end of the book.

**Three conclusions**: ① **the revision does not touch structure, only words**—the ten scene titles, the order of events, and the placement and metrical patterns of every scene's arias remain unchanged for five years; what changes are names, single characters, and details of plausibility—**“ten years to polish one play” polishes sentences**; ② **the direction of every change is “reduce coincidence and increase plausibility”**; ③ **two places where the 1965 text is “older” than the definitive text can serve as comparative specimens** (Grandma Sha's folk tone in “seven children,” Sister Aqing's personal tone in “all eighteen people are flesh and blood to me”), which the definitive text smooths away.

**If the full definitive text is obtained later, what remains to be filled in is**: whether the “Battle of Wits” duet is word-for-word identical, the number of lines in the “Green Pine” ensemble song in Scene 5, and Township Head Zhao's actual share of the action in Scene 6. The “preface” to the separate edition of the Peking opera 《芦荡火种》 (Fire Seeds in the Reed Marshes), which records the first round of adaptation, is not included in this book and is a known gap.

### 7.2 The Afterword: 11 Points (p0126–0128, May 1965, signed “the adapter”)

1. **Original title and prior history**: “The Peking opera Shajiabang was originally called Fire Seeds in the Reed Marshes... the circumstances of the adaptation of Fire Seeds in the Reed Marshes have already been explained in the preface to the previous separate edition.”
2. **Method of work**: “It was carried out throughout under the direct care and instructions of the Party leadership, and is the result of the **three-in-one combination of leadership, professional workers, and the masses**.”
3. **The most important change**: “**The role of armed struggle is emphasized**; the final destruction of the enemy relies on the guns of the New Fourth Army... an assault platoon was formed to cooperate with the main force, drive into Shajiabang, and strike straight at the enemy headquarters.” (This corresponds to Scenes 8, 9, and 10. According to the Wikipedia entry, this change arose from an opinion expressed by Mao Zedong (毛泽东) after seeing the play in July 1964.)
4. **Supporting changes**: “**Scenes 2 and 5 were substantially changed, and some lyrics were added and revised.**”
5. **Sister Aqing's repositioning**: “She has been placed in a **more appropriate position**... more attention has been paid to **strategy and flexibility**... she has more skillfully mastered the methods of **legal struggle**.”
6. **How the villains are written**: “**The contradiction between Hu Chuankui and Diao Deyi is sketched more clearly; Hu Chuankui's attitude toward Sister Aqing is more trust than suspicion.**”—the technical precondition that makes the three-way duet in Scene 4 work.
7. **The masses**: “Some masses were added, to bring out the fish-and-water relationship between the New Fourth Army wounded and the people.”
8. **The villains' crimes**: “A small amount of space was used to write the crimes of Hu Chuankui and Diao Deyi.” (In Scene 7, the shooting of the two men is carried out with offstage speech + gunshots; they do not appear.)
9. **The change in scene format (the most directly methodological point)**: “In the scene structure, **the idle expository text of characters' entrances and exits was reduced**. In most scenes, when the curtain rises the characters are already present and the action begins at once; at the end, the lights usually cut to black and the curtain falls. This was done to save time, and even more to **avoid the necessity of playing characters on and off with gongs and drums, the invisible frame of ‘generals out, ministers in’ (出将入相) on the stage, and the invisible court boots and painted shoes (朝靴彩鞋, cháoxué cǎixié) under the actors' feet**, and to reduce the old formal routines and the sense of obsolescence.”
10. **“Ten years to polish one play”**: “The present script is still far from perfect... **the leadership has taught us to spend ten years polishing one play, and we can now better appreciate what that means. Creating a revolutionary modern-dress play is no simple matter.**”
11. Signature: “The adapter, May 1965.”

> Point 9 is the most methodologically useful statement in the whole book: it treats “cutting the idle expository text of entrances and exits” and “abolishing gongs and drums that play characters on and off, and the ‘generals out, ministers in’ stage doors” as one and the same thing—**the abolition of conventions was carried out together with the abolition of the scene format**, not as a separate deletion of a few movements.

### 7.3 Cross-References to Weng Ouhong and Fan Junhong

- **Fan Junhong**: judges Diao Deyi to be “a character who breaks through role type... a character figure,” and reasons that his role-type base “cannot be a small painted face (小花脸 — a chou role), nor a great painted face (大花脸 — a jing [净] role), still less a young male; what remains is only the older male, but the older-male role type has its own limitations and must be broken through”; on this basis he wrote the two passages “This woman is no ordinary woman” and “Mother Sha, do not take it so hard”; he cites “The wind is sharp, the rain heavy, the sky low and the clouds dark” as an example of **a first line that opens an aria well**; at the same time he **criticizes later writers for abusing the aside**—“there are too many aside arias in which two or three people clash psychologically... improper imitation looks like renewing the old tradition, but in fact it falls into the rut of a new tradition.” **The model and the negative example are set side by side.**
- **Weng Ouhong** records the performance business of “spilling the tea” and “wiping the table” from the Fire Seeds in the Reed Marshes period (“it looks like a casual wave of the hand, but underneath it holds the heavy mood of contempt for the enemy and vigilance against him”), while the 1965 script writes only “throws the teapot into the water and hurriedly enters the room”—**the action is left to the actor and not written in the script**, a piece of evidence for the format of the modern-drama script.
- **Written by Wang Zengqi**: the lyrics of the three-way duet and the marketplace idiom of “Build the Seven-Star Stove” were written by Wang Zengqi (according to “Wang Zengqi and the Writing of Shajiabang,” 《汪曾祺与〈沙家浜〉的写作》).
