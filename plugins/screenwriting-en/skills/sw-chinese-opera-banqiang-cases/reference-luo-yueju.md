## §1 Yueju 《李清照》 (*Li Qingzhao*, 1999): Seven-Scene Structure and Aria Table

**Main line**: seven cross-sections of Li Qingzhao's life—as a girl she composes a new ci; the marital pleasures of Qingzhou; reunion after the move south; Mingcheng's death from illness; the deception in Wuzhou; the tavern where she reclaims herself; and, in old age, mounting the swing once more. **The structural method is "one ci poem per scene"**: each scene takes a ci by Li Qingzhao as its title, opens with that ci sung offstage, and closes by singing that ci again (or another one). **The ci is the frame of the scene; inside the frame the play shows how that poem came to be written.** **The first and last scenes both use 《如梦令》 (*Rumeng Ling* — "Like a Dream"); the swing is mounted at the start and mounted again at the end, making the whole play a closed circle.** At the end of the play the author notes: "This play draws on the 1991 Yueju television screenplay 《人比黄花瘦》 (*Thinner Than the Yellow Flowers*), written with Xu Jin (徐进)" (p0114).

**Reading**: ① **The longest aria is in scene 5 (the swindle and collapse), the shortest in scene 7 (the reunion)**—arias are placed in the scene with the greatest value shift; ② **the protagonist has not one line of song in scene 7**; the ending rests entirely on speech and the ci sung offstage—**a grand close can do without singing**; ③ the aria counts of the seven scenes are 12/13/3/9/3/2/1, with the weight on scenes 1, 2, and 4 (meeting, keeping each other, final parting), and **scene 5 uses one super-long passage to offset its small number of passages—"many passages" and "one long passage" are two ways of writing a heavy scene (重场戏, zhòngchǎngxì)**.

### 1.3 Convention Inventory

- **Self-introduction (自报家门, zìbào jiāmén)**: **none**. No one introduces himself or herself in the whole play; identity is established by 【字幕】 (surtitles) plus dialogue ("This is a master of the Jiangnan lyric world").
- **Entrance poem (定场诗, dìngchǎngshī)**: **one variant** (p0094)—the four young gentlemen enter and each recites one seven-character line: "In the tenth month the chrysanthemums of Jiangnan turn yellow / the poets are busy enjoying the autumn / who says the nation's fortunes are already strained? / both banks of the Qinhuai are fine scenery." **The entrance poem is split among four speakers and simultaneously accomplishes four things: the entrance, the season, the place, and the temperament of the characters.**
- **Exit poem (下场诗, xiàchǎngshī)**: **none in the whole play**; in its place each scene ends with **an offstage song of a Li Qingzhao ci**—the same functional position (to close the scene and leave a lingering aftertaste), with the vehicle changed from "a recited poem" to "a sung ci".
- **Aside (背供, bèigōng)**: none for Li Qingzhao in the whole play; in the entire volume only 《梨园天子》 (*The Emperor of the Pear Garden*) has two instances marked "(sings aside)" (暗唱, ànchàng).
- **Offstage song / offstage chorus (幕内唱, mùnèichàng / 幕后合唱, mùhòu héchàng)**: 15 instances, the play's heaviest convention. Four uses: ① setting the keynote at the opening (《如梦令》); ② insertion mid-scene (in scene 3 the two stanzas of 《一剪梅》 are placed before and after the reunion); ③ closing and stating the theme (the second stanza of 《孤雁儿》); ④ **hétóu** (in scene 5 "is a friend and also a friend" repeats the four lines the protagonist has just sung).
- **Entrances and exits**: written only as 【…上】 (…enters) and 【…下】 (…exits), with no percussion, no yǐnzi (引子 — entrance prelude), and no entrance poem.
- **Prop through-lines**: **the swing** (appears in scene 1 and again in scene 7, absent from the five scenes between) + **the manuscript of 《金石录》 (*Jinshi Lu* — *Record of Metal and Stone*)** (jointly compiled in scene 4 → scattered in scene 5 → completed in scene 7). The two through-line chains align the beginning and the end.

### 1.4 Aria Excerpts (and What They Demonstrate)

**(1) Two groups of four seven-character lines with the same head—the volume's main device for expanding a passage** (p0086, scene 1):

```
手捧着《新词集》墨香阵阵，
Holding the New Lyrics Collection, the scent of ink rises,
又是喜又是惊心绪不宁。
I am happy and startled, my feelings unsettled.
早听闻赵明诚精深学问，
I had long heard of Zhao Mingcheng's deep learning,
未料及竟是我词中知音。
never expecting he would be the one who truly knows my lyrics.
觑着他心儿怦怦跳；
I steal a look at him—my heart goes pit-a-pat;
觑着他脸儿泛红云；
I steal a look at him—his face is flushed with red cloud;
觑着他胆儿忽然大；
I steal a look at him—his courage suddenly grows;
觑着他语儿变得轻。
I steal a look at him—his voice turns soft.
赵公子呀——
Young Master Zhao—
赋新词随心遣兴，
writing new lyrics for your own amusement,
小使俩何劳上心。
why take such trouble over a little trick?
不避讳花园聚会，
You do not mind meeting in the garden,
嘱丫头引进后门？
yet you tell the maid to bring me through the back gate?
```
—**Four ten-character lines for narration → four seven-character lines with the same head for the act of "looking" → four seven-character lines with the same head for the "decision"**. The same head ("I steal a look at him") replaces Peking opera's duǒ lines (垛句, duǒjù — inserted short lines): **the character count is not shortened; only the line head is repeated. Yueju parallelism is even-character, not duǒ.**

**(2) The layering of the play's longest passage** (p0104, scene 5):

```
明诚啊，
Mingcheng, ah—
谁怜你一副傲骨葬异乡，
who pities you, with your proud bone buried in a foreign land;
谁怜你空怀壮志和抱负；
who pities you, with your grand ambitions all in vain;
谁怜你清白名声被玷辱；
who pities you, your clean name dragged through the mud;
谁怜你书生中年一命休！
who pities you, a scholar dead in middle age!
莫名冤案错、错、错，
for an unexplained injustice—wrong, wrong, wrong;
高节蒙污羞、羞、羞！
for a noble character stained—shame, shame, shame!
……
罢了！
Enough!
献什么收藏，
Why offer up the collection?
追什么行舟，
Why chase the boat?
澄什么疑案，
Why clear the doubtful case?
辩什么冤由，
Why argue the cause of the wrong?
且把珍宝来看护，
Better to guard the treasures,
切莫使战乱年月一件丢。
and never, in these years of war, let one piece be lost.
```
—Within a single passage, **three changes of line pattern without a change of rhyme** (ten-character lines with the same head → seven-character lines with reduplication → five-character lines with the same head → a ten-character close). "Wrong, wrong, wrong / shame, shame, shame" directly adapts the kind of reduplication found in Li Qingzhao's own 《钗头凤》 (*Chaitou Feng* — "Hairpin Phoenix")—**the character is a poet, so her aria text can be more literary than anyone else's.**

**(3) Two-character pauses, the shortest line pattern in the volume** (p0107, scene 5):
```
国破！家毁！夫丧！物尽！
The country is broken! The home destroyed! My husband dead! The collection gone!
一连串的变故，
One change after another,
一回回的伤心，
one grief after another,
一重重的劫难，
one calamity after another,
我要呼喊一声不公平！
I want to cry out: it is unfair!
```
—**Four two-character words are set side by side and used as one line**, followed by three seven-character parallel lines of "one ×, one ×". This is the form closest to duǒ in Yueju aria text, but **it is still not the great duǒ of Peking opera, where three-character pauses are strung together**.

**(4) The seam where speech turns into song: the form of address** (the rule throughout the volume; here p0091):
```
李清照　明诚，国家用人之际，你就领命上任吧。
Li Qingzhao: Mingcheng, the country needs men now; take up the appointment.
……
赵明诚　谢夫人。
Zhao Mingcheng: Thank you, my lady.
　　　　（唱）一纸官文不由己，
          (sings) A single official dispatch leaves me no choice;
　　　　　　　无奈上任去莱州。
                  reluctantly I go to take up office in Laizhou.
```
—**The line before almost every aria passage is a form of address or a short line of speech** ("Mingcheng, ah—", "Young Master Zhao—", "My lady!"). Set beside the general rule Wu Mei (吴梅) records for 《惊梦》 (*Jingmeng* — "The Interrupted Dream") and for all of 《朝阳沟》 (*Chaoyang Village*), **this can be upgraded from a suggestion to a hard rule that both systems write by**. The self-check is exactly the same: **is the line before each aria passage a form of address?**

**(5) Drawing existing ci into the song: the play's distinctive technique** (p0083, p0114): of the play's 15 offstage songs, **13 sing the original text of a ci by Li Qingzhao herself** (《如梦令》 ×2, 《浣溪纱》, 《点绛唇》, 《凤凰台上忆吹箫》, both stanzas of 《一剪梅》, 《夏日绝句》, 《添字采桑子》, 《孤雁儿》, both stanzas of 《行香子》, both stanzas of 《声声慢》, 《鹧鸪天》), and 1 sings Ouyang Xiu's 《玉楼春》 (*Yulou Chun* — "Spring in the Jade Tower") (sung onstage by Zhao Mingcheng). **The method: the original ci is used only for offstage song and scene titles; everything a character sings onstage is newly written Yueju aria text; the two are never mixed in the same passage.** When writing a play about a poet or ci writer, this is a ready-made template—**the original work is the frame, the new lyrics are the flesh, and the original is never allowed to take the onstage character's voice away from her. It is not medium-specific.**

## §2 Yueju 《真假驸马》 (*The Real and Fake Prince Consort*, created 1986 / revised 2004): Six-Scene Structure and Aria Table

Luo Huaizhen's **first play to reach the stage**, written in 1986 on commission for the Shanghai Yueju Theatre and premiered by that theatre the same year; the following year the theatre recruited him as a talent, and he moved from Huaiyin, Jiangsu, to Shanghai. ⚠️ **What this volume collects is the revision made eighteen years later, not the premiere script; the book does not include the old draft, so the differences between the two versions cannot be compared.**

**The main line, in one sentence**: Dong Wenbo (董文伯), the top scholar (状元, zhuàngyuan) and prince consort (驸马, fùmǎ), falls from a cliff and is believed dead; to preserve the Dong family's wealth and honor, the Princess orders his twin brother Wenzhong (董文仲) to take his name and impersonate the prince consort. Three years later Wenbo returns alive, and to protect the false arrangement that has grown up in his absence, the whole family **joins forces in the imperial hall to denounce him as a madman**; he is forced to admit "I am a madman" in order to live, and is still condemned to death. His mother snatches the poisoned wine and dies in his place; Wenzhong bites off his own tongue; Kuang Zheng (匡正) throws down his cap and leaves.

**A one-line note under the character list is the play's technical premise** (p0006): "(Note) Dong Wenbo and Dong Wenzhong are twin brothers, **played by the same actor in this play**."—**this is a structure tailor-made for the Yueju female xiaosheng** (女小生, nǚ xiǎoshēng — a woman playing young male roles; 度身, dùshēn — made to measure for a performer): one actor plays two people and two temperaments, and the two are never onstage together before scene 6. **The very subject of "twins + substitution" grew out of the role-type (行当, hángdang) condition of one actor playing two roles (一赶二, yī gǎn èr).**

### 2.3 Convention Inventory

- **Self-introduction**: none. **Entrance poem**: none (Kuang Zheng's entrance is replaced by a (sings) passage; supporting characters are replaced by soliloquy). **Exit poem**: none; in its place, **exit lines that are sung** (in scene 2, the two five-character lines "hold back a thousand tears / swallow the bitterness and grief"—the singer exits as soon as they are sung).
- **Aside**: not marked anywhere in the play. The "double meaning" in scene 6 (the Princess to Wenbo: "If you were not a madman, how would you dare talk nonsense in the imperial hall?", with the script explicitly noting "(staring at him, with a double meaning)") is **the substitute for an aside**: it is not spoken to the audience, but **says two things at once in front of everyone**.
- **Entrance on an offstage song**: in scene 6, Wenbo is summoned, sings one line offstage, and then enters (p0033)—the only time the volume preserves the "daoban entrance" form.
- **Offstage song**: 14 instances, four functions: theme song (opening and closing), scene exposition ("light the lanterns and search everywhere"), **psychological aside** (singing the thought the Princess cannot conveniently sing), and **interlocking with onstage speech** ("The imperial word—" / the Emperor speaks: "Summon him!" / 【the offstage voice takes up the song: "Summon, summon, summon"】, p0033). **The fourth is the only instance in the volume.**
- **One actor playing two roles**: the twin brothers are played by the same actor; in scene 6, while the gongs sound for Wenbo's execution, Wenzhong rushes on "pointing at his severed tongue, gesturing at the Emperor"—**the two characters appear in the same scene but never show their faces at the same time**, avoiding each other through "muteness" and "being carried off".

### 2.4 Aria Excerpts

**(1) Eight seven-character single lines exchanged—the basic shape of a Yueju duet** (p0010, scene 1):
```
董文仲　（唱）寸寸儿移步慢、慢、慢，
Dong Wenzhong: (sings) Inch by inch he moves, slow, slow, slow,
公　主　（唱）泪珠儿滴落二弟肩。
The Princess: (sings) tears fall one by one on the second brother's shoulder.
董文仲　（唱）兄长也曾背嫂嫂，
Dong Wenzhong: (sings) My elder brother once carried his sister-in-law too,
公　主　（唱）那是调皮和喜欢。
The Princess: (sings) that was mischief and affection.
董文仲　（唱）兄长背的是团聚，
Dong Wenzhong: (sings) what my elder brother carried was reunion,
公　主　（唱）兄弟背的是离散。
The Princess: (sings) what the brothers carry is separation.
董文仲　（唱）从此兄嫂两不见，
Dong Wenzhong: (sings) from now on brother and sister-in-law will never meet again,
公　主　（唱）想到离散情何堪？
The Princess: (sings) when I think of that separation, how can I bear it?
```
—**One line per singer, one rhyme to the end, each line picking up the words of the line before** (carrying his sister-in-law → what he carried was reunion → what they carry is separation → never meet again). It is the same technique as Yuju's 二八连弹 (èrbā liántán — a linked duet pattern), **but Yueju has no special name for it and no banshi markings, so the playwright controls only two things: "a change of singer every line" and "every line taking the last syllable in relay"**. Self-check: cover the character names—do the lines still connect into one complete speech?

**(2) A three-character head + four twelve-character long lines—the high point of the volume's aria text** (p0023, scene 3):
```
盗我者，是我一胞同生的亲兄弟；
The one who robbed me is my own brother, born of the same mother;
欺我者，是我牵肠挂肚的老母亲；
the one who deceived me is my mother, the one I worry about day and night;
负我者，是我日思夜想的结发妻；
the one who betrayed me is my wife, the one I think of day and night;
伤我者，都是我相敬相爱的自家人。
the ones who wounded me are all my own family, the people I respect and love.
结发妻子叫嫂嫂，
My wedded wife I must call sister-in-law,
孪生兄弟称兄尊。
my twin brother I must call elder brother.
我挣扎回来图什么，
What did I struggle back here for?
如今是死不算死，生不算生。
Now death does not count as death, and life does not count as life.
```
—① **A three-character lead-in + a long line** is Yueju's main exit from seven- and ten-character lines (each line here has 12–13 characters); ② of the four lines, only the first and fourth rhyme in rénchén—**in a long parallel group, the rule "upper line need not rhyme, lower line must" is relaxed to "rhyme at the head and tail, anything in between"**; ③ two seven-character lines immediately follow to pull the long lines back to even-character verse—**long and short lines must return to even-character lines to close**.

**(3) Six linked three-character lines (anadiplosis, liánzhū)—the only instance in the volume** (p0026, scene 4):
```
翘首望，思亲人，
I raise my head and gaze, thinking of my dear one,
思亲人，更伤情，
thinking of my dear one, my grief grows deeper,
更伤情，泪纷纷，
my grief grows deeper, tears fall thick and fast,
泪纷纷，暗呻吟，
tears fall thick and fast, I groan in the dark,
暗呻吟，一声声，
I groan in the dark, one sound after another,
一声声，唤娘亲！
one sound after another, calling my mother!
```
—Three-character lines are paired two to a line, **and the head of the next line = the tail of the line before**. This is a form Yueju brought in from táncí (弹词 — plucked-string storytelling) and changshu (唱书 — sung-story books); it needs no banshi support and advances purely on the surface of the words. It can be taken directly for a passage about "a person alone at night, thinking and thinking until the bitterness deepens". **Once is enough; it appears only once in this volume.**

**(4) At the grand close, kneeling to two people and changing rhyme once** (p0039–0040, scene 6): to his mother he uses rénchén (心 xīn, 情 qíng, 亲 qīn, 恨 hèn, 平 píng); to his wife he uses yánqián (涟 lián, 恋 liàn, 冤 yuān, 田 tián, 面 miàn, 甘 gān). **In one long aria, a change of addressee means a change of rhyme**—parallel to "set the rhyme by the character's personality", this is **setting the rhyme by the listener**: the same singer, a different person knelt to, a different rhyme category, and the audience's ear knows at once that the address has turned. **This is a rule this volume provides that the earlier materials do not.**

**(5) The climax scene hardly sings** (scene 6 has only 6 aria markers, 2 of them offstage songs): the confrontation in the imperial hall—the "loyalty and trust" proposition, the position of the mole on the palm, the Empress's reversal, the four people crying "madman" together—**is all in speech**. Song appears in only four places: the offstage entrance song, the Emperor's self-persuasion, Kuang Zheng's defection, and Wenbo's "waking from the dream" and his death. **Yueju's "not singing when a song is expected" is not necessarily a fault**: argument over facts, proof of identity, and collective oppression move faster in speech; **song is reserved for "one person opening the heart to another".**

**Reading 1: subject matter determines the song-to-speech ratio.** The two plays with the lowest marker density (*Liu Rushi* with 1.29, *Li Qingzhao* with 1.35) are both **argumentative / historical-biography subjects**: Liu Rushi and Qian Qianyi (钱谦益) spend whole acts arguing "to come out of retirement or not" and "to die or not", while *Li Qingzhao* moves forward entirely through the tea contest, the book-guessing game, medical diagnosis, and buying wine on credit. The two with the highest (*Meilong Town* with 2.63, *Blue Robe · Red Robe* with 2.54) are **a romantic comedy and a string of excerpt scenes (折子串本, zhézi chuànběn)**, carried from scene to scene and chapter to chapter by duet. **Before writing Yueju, settle the type of subject first, then the density of arias.**

**Reading 2: arias are concentrated in heavy scenes, not spread evenly.** In both the seven-scene and six-scene plays the aria counts are front-heavy and back-light, and **the climax scene actually sings the least** (*The Real and Fake Prince Consort* scene 6 has only 6 markers; *Li Qingzhao* scene 7 has only 1). **Use song for lyricism and preparation; use speech for the showdown and the close.**

The samples total **496 aria markers**.

### 3.6 E. Script Conventions (Compared with Peking-Opera Scripts)

| Element | Yueju script | For comparison: Peking-opera script (*Shajiabang* / *The White Snake* / *The Jewelry Purse*) |
|---|---|---|
| Metrical pattern (bǎnshì) | **Not marked** (0 instances in the volume) | Marked for every passage |
| Song | `（唱）` (sings) after the character's name; continued song uses `（接唱）` (continues singing); also `（合唱）` (chorus), `（齐唱）` (unison), `（伴唱）` (accompanying chorus), `（暗唱）` (sings aside), `（内唱）` (sings offstage), `（齐诵）` (unison recitation) | The banshi marker is itself the marker for song |
| Speech | **Carries no marker at all** ("what is marked is song; what is unmarked is speech") | Often marked "speech", or distinguished by the presence or absence of a banshi |
| Stage directions | **Begin with a one-sided square bracket "【" that is never closed**, occupying a line of its own | Parentheses or square brackets in pairs |
| Unit of scene division | scene / act / chapter (回, huí) (all three occur in this volume) | scene |
| Scene title | Two kinds: titled (*The Real and Fake Prince Consort*; all seven scenes of *Li Qingzhao* use **Li Qingzhao's own ci tune titles**), untitled (*The Emperor of the Pear Garden*, *Snake Love*, *Meilong Town* are numbered only) | Scene title or number |
| Time and place | **【Caption: …】** (8 instances in the volume, 6 of them in *Li Qingzhao*) | Self-introduction, opening prose |
| Character list | Names + a one-line note on identity; *The Real and Fake Prince Consort* adds a performance note at the end | Role types are usually marked |
| Role types | **Never marked** | Marked in the character list or at the head of the play |
| Percussion, costumes, stage-keeping (检场, jiǎnchǎng) | **Never written** | Weng Ouhong's scripts (翁偶虹) include them (master script — 总讲, zǒngjiǎng) |
| Signature | At the end of the play: year of writing (+ year of revision), note on the source of adaptation | Preface / afterword |

**In one sentence**: a Yueju script = **scene (or act / chapter) + 【one-sided-bracket stage directions】 + character name + (sings) / unmarked speech + 【Caption】 to establish time and place + offstage song framing the edges**, with no banshi, no role types, and no percussion. **What the librettist hands over is words and emotion, not tune and beat**—set beside the Peking-opera librettist, who "hands over words, banshi, and percussion", **the two must be written side by side; otherwise readers will take "the script conventions of the banqiang system (板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ)" to have only one form.**

### 3.7 F. Convention Choices (the Practice of Contemporary Newly Written Yueju)

- **Cut**: self-introduction (0 in the volume), exit poem (0), percussion / costume / stage-keeping directions (0), banshi markings (0), role-type markings (0). The entrance poem survives only as 1 variant (p0094, the four young gentlemen each recite one seven-character line) + 1 mixed recitation-and-intonation passage (p0122, Qian Qianyi: "Sixteen years confined beneath the trees… (intoned) the great affairs of the world trouble my mind less").
- **Kept**: entrances and exits (【…enters】【…exits】, without percussion), **the circular walk (圆场, yuánchǎng)** (2 instances, and in *The Emperor of the Pear Garden* p0047 "circular walk" is put in quotation marks, showing that the author knows it is a convention term), dance and stage business (身段, shěnduàn) (8 instances), **entrance on an offstage song** (1 instance, p0033), **the aside** (2 instances, both in scene 5 of *The Emperor of the Pear Garden*, marked "(sings aside)", and **used only for suspicion**).
- **Added**: 【Caption】 (8 instances), **offstage song / offstage chorus / accompanying chorus** (present in all 7 plays and already part of the house style, with four functions: framing with the theme line / scene exposition / psychological aside / hétóu), **theme song** (*Snake Love*: ten long and short lines repeated verbatim four times), **storyteller** (评书人, píngshūrén; in *Blue Robe · Red Robe*, he enters and exits before and after each of the four chapters; the author appears in person), **multiple performance areas and a revolving stage** (p0025, "multiple performance areas"; all five acts of *Liu Rushi* use "the stage revolves, the scene migrates").

### 3.8 G. Six Things This Volume Cannot Support (the reference must leave them blank)

1. **A Yueju banshi table**—not one of 四工调 (sìgōng diào), 尺调 (chǐdiào), 弦下调 (xiándiào), 二凡 (èrfán), 流水 (liúshuǐ), or 嚣板 (xiāobǎn) appears; this volume provides no correspondence between banshi and emotion or line pattern. **This is the biggest blank in the Yueju appendix**, and must be filled from a Yueju music book or a performance script with the score.
2. **A character table dividing the rhyme categories**—one can induce only "which rhyme categories are common and which are mixed together"; there is **no division-of-characters table**. **Do not apply Peking opera's thirteen rhyme categories or Yuju's Zhongzhou rhymes.**
3. **The relation between schools and line pattern**—the Yuan, Fan, Fu, and Yin schools are not mentioned once.
4. **How traditional core repertory is written**—all 7 plays in this volume are newly written after 1986, so **what is induced is the writing practice of contemporary newly written Yueju**; the line patterns and rhyme use of traditional scripts such as 《梁祝》 (*Butterfly Lovers*), 《红楼梦》 (*Dream of the Red Chamber*), and 《碧玉簪》 (*The Jade Hairpin*) cannot be corroborated from this volume.
5. **The differences between premiere and revised scripts**—*The Real and Fake Prince Consort* (1986→2004) and *Snake Love* (1993→2003) are marked with revision years, but **the book does not provide the old drafts**. If a premiere script is obtained later, the most worthwhile comparisons are: whether scenes were added or cut, whether arias were moved, and **whether the words or the structure were changed** (compare the reading of the two versions of *Shajiabang*: "the revision changes words, not structure").
6. **Vocal style, instrumental bridges (guòmén), accompaniment, and key**—under the general rule these never enter the skill, and this volume says not one word about them in any case.

### 3.9 The Structure of the Other Five Yueju Plays and One Unique Device Each

| **蛇恋** *Snake Love* (1993/2003) | Prologue + 8 scenes, **100 aria markers** (tied with *Meilong Town* for the most) | The White Snake does not want divine immortality, only to be human; she saves people with her blood and is surrounded and beaten, saves her husband with her life and is feared by him, until the pagoda falls, her child is born, and she puts on the plainest clothes | **The theme song serves as the play's skeleton**: ten long and short lines **repeated verbatim four times** (prologue, end of scene 3, end of scene 7, end of scene 8), not one word changed, **belonging to no character, not rhyming in Yueju's usual rhyme categories, not written in upper-and-lower line pairs**—it is a song, not an aria passage. A structural component that 1990s newly written Yueju borrowed from opera and television drama |

**The original text of the decreasing line lengths in *Meilong Town*** (p0231–0232):
```
正　德（唱）我要孩儿常欢乐，
Zhengde: (sings) I want the child always happy,
皇太后（唱）我要孙儿读书忙。
Empress Dowager: (sings) I want my grandson busy with his books.
正　德（唱）要他知疾苦，
Zhengde: (sings) I want him to know suffering,
皇太后（唱）要他驭万方。
Empress Dowager: (sings) I want him to rule the realm.
正　德（唱）不失本真，
Zhengde: (sings) not losing his true nature,
皇太后（唱）先学端庄。
Empress Dowager: (sings) first learning propriety.
正　德（唱）懂得善恶，
Zhengde: (sings) knowing good and evil,
皇太后（唱）识得忠良。
Empress Dowager: (sings) recognizing the loyal and good.
正　德（唱）天性！
Zhengde: (sings) natural instinct!
皇太后（唱）国邦！
Empress Dowager: (sings) the nation!
```
—**Seven characters → five → four → two**, one line per singer, one rhyme (jiāngyáng) to the end, couplet by couplet. Set beside Yuju's 狗撕咬 (gǒusīyǎo — "dog bite", a quarrel-specific pattern with a banshi name): **in Yueju the decrease expresses "the two press closer and closer, neither willing to yield"; it need not be a quarrel. Yueju has no banshi names, so the playwright can produce this effect only through character count—character count is itself Yueju's banshi.**

**Four (five) ways of closing, for reference** (when writing a Yueju ending, choose one directly; do not mix them): ① the offstage song repeats the theme line verbatim (*The Real and Fake Prince Consort* p0041); ② the theme song returns verbatim for the fourth time (*Snake Love* p0196); ③ six lines of pure speech, not one line of song added (*Liu Rushi* p0152); ④ a tune running through the whole play is sung by a third person, and reconciliation follows (*Meilong Town* p0234); ⑤ return to the same action and the same ci with which the play opened (*Li Qingzhao* p0114: she mounts the swing again and sings 《如梦令》 again).

## §7 Afterword to the Yueju Volume (a First-Hand Record of Composition)

**The eight points of the afterword (p0264) are the only first-hand record of composition in the Yueju section:**

1. **《真假驸马》 (*The Real and Fake Prince Consort*)**: written in 1986 on commission for the Shanghai Yueju Theatre, which premiered it the same year. "**It is also the first play of mine to reach the stage.**" The following year the Shanghai Yueju Theatre recruited him as a talent, and he moved from Huaiyin, Jiangsu, to Shanghai. → Read together with the note at the end of the play, "written 1986 / revised 2004": **what this volume collects is the revision made eighteen years later, not the premiere script**.
2. **《梅龙镇》 (*Meilong Town*) and 《青衫·红袍》 (*Blue Robe · Red Robe*)**: "I tailor-made them especially for Zhang Ruihong, and the Shanghai Yueju Theatre also premiered them. Ruihong was once my life partner and is forever my artistic soulmate. Both scripts also condense her emotion, wisdom, and sweat." → **The only two plays in the volume explicitly described as "tailor-made for an actor"**; their shared features (male roles, one actor playing many, long duets) are the concrete content of "tailor-made".
3. **Yueju works not collected here**: he also wrote or co-wrote 《西施归越》 (*Xi Shi Returns to Yue*), 《风月秦淮》 (*Wind and Moon on the Qinhuai*), 《紫玉钗》 (*The Purple Jade Hairpin*), and the Yueju television dramas 《真假驸马》 (*The Real and Fake Prince Consort*), 《人比黄花瘦》 (*Thinner Than the Yellow Flowers*), 《秦淮烟云》 (*Misty Rain on the Qinhuai*), among others. → **Rewriting the same subject across different opera genres is his normal practice.**
4. **《梨园天子》 (*The Emperor of the Pear Garden*)**: premiered by the Nanjing Yueju Company (not the Shanghai Yueju Theatre).
5. **《柳如是》 (*Liu Rushi*)**: grew out of 《风月秦淮》 (*Wind and Moon on the Qinhuai*), written with Ji Naixian; "later it was re-created as the Hanju play 《柳如是》 (*Liu Rushi*), and is now collected in its Yueju form". → **One script in three forms**; what enters the collected works is "the Yueju form", not the latest Hanju script—**the author himself has performed a translation back across genres**.
6. **《蛇恋》 (*Snake Love*)**: originally written at the invitation of the Zhejiang Xiaobaihua Yueju Company and premiered in 1994 under the title 《白蛇与许仙》 (*The White Snake and Xu Xian*); later rewritten for the Ningbo Yueju Company of Zhejiang and retitled 《蛇恋》. → **A change of title is a change of theme**: from "The White Snake and Xu Xian" (a matter of two people) to "Snake Love" (the matter of a snake wanting to become human).
7. **《青衫·红袍》 (*Blue Robe · Red Robe*)**: "written in collaboration with Gong Xiong." (The note at the end of the play gives "Gong Xiaoxiong", p0263; **the two credits disagree, so the play-end credit is followed and the discrepancy noted**.)
8. *Li Qingzhao* is not mentioned in the afterword; the note at the end of the play says "**this play draws on the 1991 Yueju television screenplay 《人比黄花瘦》 (*Thinner Than the Yellow Flowers*), written with Xu Jin**" (p0114). Xu Jin is the librettist of the Yueju 《红楼梦》 (*Dream of the Red Chamber*); **a television script → stage script adaptation back**, which, together with the cross-genre adaptation back in item 5, forms a pair of "multiple transformations of the same material".
