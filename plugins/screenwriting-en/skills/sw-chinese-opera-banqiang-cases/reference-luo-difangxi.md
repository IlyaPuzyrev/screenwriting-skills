# The Eight Local-Opera Plays of Luo Huaizhen (罗怀臻): Full Script Records for *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* (《金龙与蜉蝣》) and *The Wife for Hire* (《典妻》), Structural Records for the Other Six, and a Single Table of Line Patterns Across Six Opera Genres

>
> **Source text and verification status (must be cited together with the data)**: *The Drama Writings of Luo Huaizhen*, Volume 2, Scripts · Local-Opera Volume, Shanghai People's Publishing House, first edition, February 2008, 301 scanned pages. **Printed page = scanned page − 5**; throughout this file pages are cited as scanned pages `pNNNN`. Contains 8 plays across six opera genres (3 Huaiju [淮剧, Huáijù — Huai opera], 1 Yongju [甬剧, Yǒngjù — Ningbo opera], 1 Huangmei opera [黄梅戏, Huángméixì], 1 Yuju [豫剧, Yùjù — Henan opera], 1 Chuanju [川剧, Chuānjù — Sichuan opera], 1 Qinqiang [秦腔, Qínqiāng — Shaanxi opera]). **Not one of the eight plays carries a single metrical-pattern (板式, bǎnshì) marking**; they mark only "(sings)" and "(recites)," plus 【stage directions (科介, kējiè)】, 【surtitles】, and 【offstage singing / chorus / bāngqiāng (帮腔 — offstage answering chorus)】. ⚠️ **The sample is one author, one to three plays per genre; only Huaiju spans three points in time—1981, 1992, and 1994—so its reliability is higher than that of the other five genres.** ⚠️ **Two adaptation scripts must be discounted**: *The Hegemon of Western Chu* (《西楚霸王》) from a Peking-opera script, *Yang Guifei* (《杨贵妃》) from a musical (see §6.5). ⚠️ **Rhyme categories (韵辙, yùnzhé) are everywhere summarized by Mandarin pronunciation; until they have been checked against each genre's dialect rhyme table they can serve only as examples of "where the rhyme falls"** (Yongju uses Ningbo dialect, Chuanju Sichuan dialect, Qinqiang the Guanzhong dialect).

## §4 Huaiju *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* (1992): Seven-Unit Table, 44-Passage Aria Table, and Conventions

Character list, p0054: 金龙 (Jinlong — a monarch), 蜉蝣 (Fuyou — Jinlong's son), 玉凤 (Yufeng — Jinlong's wife), 玉荞 (Yuqiao — Jinlong's daughter-in-law), 牛牯 (Niugu — Jinlong's follower), 子子 (Zizi — Jinlong's grandson), 老王 (the Old King — Jinlong's father), Actors A and B (优伶甲乙, yōulíng jiǎ yǐ), a young eunuch, the former kings, soldiers, and the consorts. **Time: "since the Western Zhou, in one of the feudal states of Huaxia, broadly a branch of the Chu line"—the deliberate refusal to name a dynasty; this line is the play's declaration of itself as fable.** The premiere was billed as "**urban new Huaiju**" (都市新淮剧, dūshì xīn Huáijù).

| One · Putting Out to Sea | p0056–0061 | Yufeng, Jinlong, the baby, the Old King (apparition) | Three years as a fishing family; a dragon boat passing on the sea stirs up the past; the Old King's ghost appears and forces him to restore the kingdom; Jinlong abandons his wife and child | Mainly song (8 passages) | The offstage solo repeats; the hook (扣子, kòuzi — a suspense device at the end of a segment) is "either a fisherman or a prince" |
| Two · Entering the Palace | p0061–0067 | Jinlong, Niugu, Fuyou, soldiers, Yufeng, Yuqiao | The kingdom is restored; Niugu is killed; Fuyou, searching for his father, is castrated; at the same moment Jinlong feels abdominal pain; three locations sing in alternation (palace / hometown) | Mainly song (nearly 20 passages) | A long road stretches as a single line + the offstage solo "Seeking the son's return, calling the husband home" |
| Three · Lingering | p0067–0075 | Jinlong, Fuyou, the young eunuch, Actors A and B, the palace beauties, Yuqiao, Yufeng, Zizi | Eight years without an heir; the actors are killed for satire; Fuyou and his wife meet again in the palace; Fuyou urges his wife to serve the king and take revenge; Yufeng forces her way into the palace | Song and speech alternate | Yufeng's "Go!" + Fuyou bares his blade |
| Four · Storming the Palace | p0075–0080 | Jinlong, Yufeng, Zizi, Fuyou | Yufeng denounces the king to his face; Zizi introduces himself as "a little bug"; the helmet appears; Fuyou attempts the assassination and recognizes his father | Long solo passages + speech | Fuyou cries "I am not your son" and runs off |
| Five · Sacrificing to the Ancestors | p0080–0084 | Jinlong, the former kings, Fuyou | At the royal tomb he confesses his guilt; father and son sing a duet of recognition; Fuyou asks to leave; Jinlong stabs his son | Mainly duet (including ten rounds of question and answer) | Fuyou dies holding the sword |
| Epilogue · Taking the Throne | p0084–0085 | Jinlong, Yufeng, Yuqiao, Zizi, soldiers | Yufeng dies; Yuqiao cuts her own throat; Jinlong sets Zizi on the throne and is run through by Zizi's sword | Speech + offstage accompaniment | The soldiers prostrate themselves and hail "the king"; Zizi holds up the helmet, his face showing bewilderment |

**Seven units (prologue + five acts + epilogue); the play never uses "scene" (场) but "act" (幕)**—this is its first trace of modernization, distinguishing it from *The Death of Han Xin* (《韩信之死》) in the same volume with its thirteen scenes.

In the table, seven passages—1, 9, 16, 28, 29, 35, 44—belong to the offstage solo / accompaniment layer (**they belong to no character**). **The rule: the accompaniment handles only two things—covering time jumps (16, 29) and translating inner life that cannot be spoken aloud (35); it never advances the plot.** The three heaviest turns in the play (patricide and usurpation, castration, father and son recognizing each other) are all sung by the characters themselves.

**Song–speech balance**: of the 44 passages, Jinlong has 12, Fuyou 11, Yufeng 8, Yuqiao 5, the accompaniment 7, and the rest 1. **Father and son, Jinlong and Fuyou, take more than half**, and in Act Five the two merge into a ten-round question-and-answer duet—**the distribution of arias itself draws the play's character structure: two generations of men sing against each other, three generations of women sing in turn.**

**The character of the spoken dialogue (宾白, bīnbái)**: the spoken lines are short and carry action cues; often one line is a turn ("You are the little bug"—"My king, my father's name was Niugu"—"I, the lone king, keep the son of a rebel minister to show the world my mercy"). **Long expository statements are always given to song; spoken lines only make hooks and ask questions**—the exact opposite of *The Death of Han Xin*, where long stretches of spoken dialogue carry argument (the court debates are almost entirely speech). This is the second change in eleven years.

### 4.3 Checklist of Conventions

- **Variants of self-introduction (自报家门, zìbào jiāmén)**: no traditional "this old man, so-and-so"; instead **someone else asks and the character answers**. Fuyou, p0062: "My name is Fuyou—a little bug, a tiny bit, this big, floating in the water, swimming and swimming!"—name + image + fate in one breath; **on p0078 Zizi repeats this speech word for word to Jinlong**—self-introduction has been turned into **a structural device that recurs a generation later**.
- **Ghosts onstage**: the Old King (p0058, p0060) and "the former kings" (p0080) appear in midair and speak with the living. **The traditional ghost convention is used, but it is filled with externalized psychology.**
- **Entrance poem / exit poem (定场诗, dìngchǎngshī / 下场诗, xiàchǎngshī)**: none in the play. **The contemporary new play replaces the exit poem entirely with offstage accompaniment** (most typically #28, "Seeking the son's return, calling the husband home," whose position and function are exactly those of an exit poem).
- **Aside (背供, bèigōng)**: Fuyou, p0069, "(sarcastically) Come now! (sings) This one is lovely of face…"—an aside-style appraisal sung to Jinlong's face.
- **Fighting and dance**: the soldiers "dance, their steps trampling around the corpse" (p0062); the scream of the castration and Jinlong on the throne "struggling and tossing… rigid on the throne, like a corpse" (p0065)—**tumbling and falling (翻扑, fānpū) are applied to psychological content**.
- **An independent throne in the manner of one table and two chairs**: the throne is the play's only fixed point (in the prologue the Old King hangs above it → Niugu leaps onto it → Jinlong takes the seat → at the end Zizi is forced into it)—**the whole space is organized around a single stage prop (砌末, qìmò)**.

### 4.4 Six Aria Excerpts

**① Nursery-rhyme self-introduction (p0063, Fuyou)—line pattern as character**
> Mayfly, mayfly, a little thing. ／ A hard life from the start— ／ a mother, but no father. ／ My king, your enemy meant no harm; ／ there is no reason to kill me. ／ Why not do one good deed ／ and let me go find my father.
> (Original: 蜉蝣蜉蝣小东西。／从小命儿苦，／有娘没有爹。／大王啊，对头本无意，／杀我没道理。／何不积一德，／放我去寻爹。)

Beyond the seven-character lines everything is three- and five-character short lines—jumpy, with no long breath. The same speech is repeated word for word by Zizi on p0078 (changed to spoken dialogue)—**"same words, different situation": the first time it is a plea for life, the second a recurrence of fate.**

**② Anadiplosis across three locations (p0066)**
> Yufeng (sings): At this end of the river, a woman; ／ at that end of the river, a boy. ／ Fuyou (sings): The boy kneels below the steps— ／ Yuqiao (sings): his wife watches from the doorway— ／ Yufeng (sings): his mother thinks of him at home— ／ (sings): one thought, one broken heart.
> (Original: 玉凤（唱）江水这头是女子，／江水那头是儿郎。／蜉蝣（唱）郎在阶下跪——／玉荞（唱）妻在倚门望——／玉凤（唱）娘在家中想——／（唱）一想一断肠。)

The last character of one line becomes the first of the next; three people in three places sing the same rhyme and the same line pattern; the final line shrinks to six characters to close. **This is the ready-made way the banqiang system (板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ — the system that organizes arias by metrical pattern rather than fixed tune patterns) handles simultaneity: no scene change, three spaces juxtaposed by round singing.**

**③ Broken line patterns (p0076, Yufeng storms the palace)—the hardest evidence for "urban new Huaiju"**
> My husband dead, my own son maimed, ／ my daughter-in-law forced inside the palace walls, ／ it is like a thunderbolt bursting open my breast, ／ cutting off my ten thousand hopes, my thousand dreams, my hundred cares, my whole life's hope; ／ all that is left is a heart full of grievance, anger that reaches the sky. ／ I no longer care for this aged body—I will storm in, come what may, and face the king to argue right and wrong!
> (Original: 丈夫死，亲儿伤，／儿媳被逼入宫墙，／好一似霹雳炸开我胸膛，／绝了我万种期盼、千回梦想、百般牵挂、一生希望，／唯剩下，满腔怨愤、冲天怒火，／我顾不得年迈之身、横冲直闯、面见君王论短长！)

Three-character opening → seven → nine → **four four-character chains (ten thousand / a thousand / a hundred / a lifetime)** → three-character lead + four-character chain → a fourteen-character long line. **Within one passage the character count rises from 3 to 14. The frame of the traditional seven- and ten-character couplet is still there (the rhyme runs jiangyang throughout), but the "waist" of every line has been stretched open—what urban new Huaiju changed is not the rhyme but the variance of line length.**

**④ Ten rounds of question and answer (p0083, Jinlong and Fuyou)—duet as negotiation**
> Jinlong (sings): I ask you, Fuyou—does your heart hate your father? ／ Fuyou (sings): Half of it is tears, half is resentment. ／ Jinlong (sings): I ask you, Fuyou—why did your mother go blind? ／ Fuyou (sings): She married the wrong man, and her tears have never stopped. ／ … ／ Jinlong (sings): To whom shall I entrust the realm? ／ Fuyou (sings): I am sick of conquest long ago. ／ Jinlong (sings): A fisherman is no emperor's heir, ／ Fuyou (sings): The emperor and I are enemies!
> (Original: 金龙（唱）问蜉蝣，心中可把爹爹恨？／蜉蝣（唱）一半是泪水，一半是怨仇。／金龙（唱）问蜉蝣，你娘因何瞎了眼？／蜉蝣（唱）错嫁了人儿泪长流。／……／金龙（唱）江山托付谁？／蜉蝣（唱）霸业早厌透。／金龙（唱）渔夫不是帝王后，／蜉蝣（唱）帝王与我是对头！)

Ten rounds; **the questions shrink from seven characters to five, while the answers stay seven characters and hold the final rhyme**. **The close is achieved by shortening the questions and not the answers—the tempo accelerates while the rhyme does not move.**

**⑤ Four-fold refrain in the confession (p0081, Jinlong)**
> … With my own hands I crippled my son Fuyou; ／ with my own hands I took my son Fuyou's wife; ／ with my own hands I destroyed Fuyou's wife; ／ with my own hands I tormented Fuyou's kin.
> (Original: ……亲手将，蜉蝣儿身残；／亲手将，蜉蝣儿媳占；／亲手将，蜉蝣妻子摧；／亲手将，蜉蝣亲人煎。)

**The three-character lead "with my own hands" heads four repetitions, with the verb landing at the end of each line (crippled / took / destroyed / tormented)—the crimes are compressed into four verbs. This is the contemporary use of duǒjù (垛句 — inserted short lines): not narrative acceleration but a list of guilt.**

**⑥ Theme song as frame (p0056 / p0060–0061 / p0085, offstage solo)**
> Big Brother's heart is too black— ／ what he can think of, he can do; ／ Little Sister's heart is too soft— ／ she has a way, and yet no way. ／ From now on I ask you only one thing: ／ never leave your sister's side for a lifetime—
> (Original: 大哥哥心太黑，／想得出就做得出；／小妹妹心太软，／有办法也没办法。／从今只求你一件事，／一辈子不离妹半尺——)

Three appearances: after the rape, at the abandonment, and at the end of the play. **Not one word changes; the situation changes three times. The contemporary new play replaces the exit poem with offstage solo precisely for this capacity of "same words, different situation."**

### 4.5 How Modern Structure and Traditional Convention Coexist

1. **Fable**: no dynasty, no name of the state, characters without surnames. Names are symbols—Jinlong (golden dragon = royal power), Fuyou (mayfly = born in the morning, dead by evening), Niugu (a head of cattle = the follower who is like livestock), Zizi (son of a son). **Because it does not anchor itself in historical fact, the conventions (the throne, the ghost, the ceremonial guard in the manner of qǐbà [起霸 — opening martial display]) can be carried over unchanged without colliding with historical research.**
2. **Time and space**: three big jumps marked by surtitles (three years / twenty years / another eight years) compress three generations into seven units; every jump is bridged by offstage accompaniment. **The traditional rule of one scene, one time, one place is not broken; what is broken is the distance between scenes.**
3. **The symbolic and realist layers run in parallel**: Fuyou is both the castrated son (realist) and the little bug "floating in the water, swimming and swimming" (symbolic); when Jinlong is punished, his abdomen hurts at the same moment—**the punisher and the punished share one body**. This is an operation on the symbolic layer, but it uses the device Chinese opera knows best: "separate singing in different places on the same stage."
4. **A chain of props**: the helmet = the cooking pot (p0057, "once it is blurred it is no longer a helmet but a pot") → the name is boiled illegible → mother and son take "Niugu" as the father's name → Fuyou searches for his father by it → Zizi carries it to beg → on p0079 Jinlong picks it up and his soul leaves his body → at the end Zizi holds it up and "his face gradually shows bewilderment." **One prop carries the entire causal chain of the play's misrecognition.**
5. **Circular structure**: the prologue has the Old King murdered and hung above the throne ↔ the epilogue has Jinlong run through by his grandson and falling at the throne; the soldiers hail "the king" twice. **The fable closes not by a character arc but by structural recurrence**—this conflicts with the general layer's character arc, and is one row to be added to the medium-boundary table.
6. **The place of traditional conventions in a modern structure**: the conventions are not deleted; **their bearer is swapped**—fighting is used for massacre rather than martial display; ghosts for inner interrogation; tumbling for physical pain; ceremonial ("drums and cannon, pray to the ancestors" p0062) for satire. *The Hegemon of Western Chu* (《西楚霸王》) in the same volume is even more direct: p0091 explicitly writes 【Xiang Yu leads the singing and the dance, the whole company performs "qǐbà"】, p0104 explicitly writes 【leading the company in a "tàngmǎ" (趟马 — horse-riding mime) dance】—**the convention's name is written straight into the stage directions; the playwright assigns the convention at the textual level.**
7. **A counterexample to keep beside it**: this play **has no "one person, one event," and no "concentration of the main line."** Each of the three generations has its own line (Jinlong's restoration—Fuyou's search for his father—Yufeng and Yuqiao's search for their kin), and the three lines collide in Acts Three and Four; the second half of Act Two juxtaposes the palace and the hometown in round singing, which is precisely the "horizontal concentration" of spoken drama. → **"Concentration of the main line and of scenes" must be annotated: contemporary new plays (fable form) may be exceptions, on condition that a single prop runs through and sustains cause and effect for the main line** (in this play, the helmet). **This directly conflicts with Fan Junhong's "vertical concentration"; set the two side by side.**

## §5 Yongju *The Wife for Hire* (2000): Five-Scene Structure, 39-Passage Aria Table, and Four Principles of Adaptation

**Adapted from Rou Shi's (柔石) novella *A Slave Mother* (《为奴隶的母亲》)** (the author's note under the title, p0120); premiered by the Ningbo Municipal Yongju Troupe, Zhejiang Province.

### 5.1 Characters and Format (p0120)

Character list: **Wife, Husband, Xiucai, Senior Mistress, Liu Ma, Huang Ma, Sedan-chair Bearer A, Sedan-chair Bearer B**, plus **春宝 (Chunbao) and 秋宝 (Qiubao)**, who never appear onstage and exist only as children's voices off.

- **No names**: the protagonist is called "Wife," the others "Husband," "Xiucai," "Senior Mistress." **Only the two children have names—the children are named, the adults are not**; this is the play's most direct modern move.
- Every scene opens with 【Surtitle: …】 establishing time and place (p0121, the Wife's home in a village in eastern Zhejiang in the early Republic; p0127, the day the Wife is hired into the Xiucai's house; p0134, one year later; p0142, the three-year term expires; p0146, on the road home; epilogue p0149, the Wife's former home). **All five scenes are timed by surtitles; the play's three years are divided by four surtitles.**
- Between scenes, always 【Offstage singing (幕内唱, mùnèichàng): "…"】, **one poem of four seven-character lines, six poems in all**. Only "(sings)" is marked; there is no metrical pattern, no qupai (曲牌, qǔpái — a fixed tune pattern), and no "(recites)."

### 5.2 Scene-by-Scene Structural Table

**The one structural fact to remember above all: the question-and-answer exchange from Scene One is repeated word for word in the Epilogue** (p0122 / p0149):
> Wife: Chunbao's father, why haven't you lit the lamp? ／ Husband: There is no oil. ／ Wife: Why haven't you lit the stove? ／ Husband: There is no firewood. ／ Wife: Why haven't you cooked? ／ Husband: There is no rice. ／ Wife: And Chunbao—why can't I hear him crying?
> (Original: 妻：春宝爹，为何不上灯？／夫：没有油了。／妻：为何不生灶？／夫：没有柴了。／妻：为何不做饭？／夫：没有米了。／妻：春宝呢，为何也听不到他的哭声？)

**In Scene One the answer is "I just coaxed him to sleep"; in the Epilogue it is "he is very ill, there is no curing him now, he was waiting for you to come back and look at him one last time." Three years for a hundred silver dollars, and she is back at the starting point, worse off—this same-words-different-answer frame is the play's main structure.**

### 5.4 Checklist of Conventions

- **Self-introduction**: none. Replaced by **the "six there-is-no" aria of circumstance** (p0121) and **the Senior Mistress's interrogating question-and-answer** (p0128–0129, making the Wife hold out her hands, laugh, cry, testing her item by item).
- **Entrance poem / exit poem**: none. **The whole function is replaced by six 【offstage songs】 of four seven-character lines**—each at the head or tail of a scene, functioning as time-setting ("a night of wind and rain, the road is hard; the sedan chair enters the gate at dawn"), commentary ("this bargain is too absurd"), and thesis ("one knows she is a slave, one knows she is a mother"). **This is the most complete sample of the contemporary new play's replacement of the exit poem.**
- **The sedan-chair journey (行轿, xíngjiào)**: Scene Five 【The suona wails. Bearers A and B carry the Wife on, she still holds the old umbrella; the chair travels】【the chair stops】【the chair travels】【the chair travels as if flying】—**the traditional sedan-chair convention is used for a whole scene**, all the arias are sung in the chair, and three stops make rhythmic breaks.
- **The virtual and the offstage**: Chunbao and Qiubao have only voices throughout; Scene Five 【children's laughter】→ Chunbao's call → Qiubao's call, alternating and receding, while the Wife runs after them onstage. **Using voices to make virtual "people" is cheaper and crueler than bringing children onstage.**
- **One prop runs through**: **the old umbrella**. In Scene One the Wife finds the broken umbrella to take to her husband → it is sent back; when she enters the sedan chair, "Chunbao's mother holds up an umbrella—it does not keep out wind or rain, it hides her face"; in Scene Four "she still carries the umbrella she came with under her arm"; in Scene Five "the Wife still holds the old umbrella." **Three years, one umbrella: she goes in as she came out.**
- **Same words, different answer** (the four questions and answers at head and tail)—the play's most important structural convention.
- **Aside**: the Senior Mistress, p0136, sees the Husband enter, 【hides】, then sings "Who is that—he looks like my little woman's husband"; in Scene Three she 【peeks out】 three times—**three peeks are a foreshadowing counter onstage**; immediately after the third, she has him seized.

### 5.5 Five Aria Excerpts

**① "There is no" replacing self-introduction (p0121, Scene One)**
> Chunbao cries pitifully in his illness, ／ and my husband has gone out and does not come back. ／ There is no rice in the rice tub, ／ there is no firewood in the firewood stove, ／ there is no oil in the oil lamp, ／ there is no water in the water vat. ／ Three whole days without a meal— ／ my heart burns as I wait for my husband's return.
> (Original: 春宝病中哭声哀，／丈夫出门不回来。／米桶里面没有米，／柴灶里面没有柴，／油灯里面没有油，／水缸里面没有水，／整整三天未吃饭，／心急火燎盼夫归。)

Four "there is no × in the ×," the line pattern identical, only the noun changed. **The parallelism is not rhetoric; it is an inventory of poverty.** In the Epilogue these four lines become the Husband's four spoken answers—**song turns to speech, same content, different carrier.**

**② The contract written as aria lyrics (p0124, Scene One)**
> There is a household rich in means, ／ but alas the Senior Mistress bears no child, ／ and they want to borrow a wife to carry on the line. ／ The term of the loan is three years; ／ the one who lends is the husband. ／ The contract is signed on the spot, ／ and the silver dollars paid in a lump. ／ The sedan chair is already at our door— ／ I will come back in it, and you will set out.
> (Original: 有户人家家道富，／可惜大娘不生养，／要借一个传代妇。／借期共三年，／借主是丈夫。／现场就画押，／大洋一笔付。／轿子已在家门口，／我乘轿回来你上路。)

The first three lines are seven characters of exposition; the middle four shrink to five; **the five-character line here is the rhythm of a contract clause**—short, hard, not open to negotiation. The last two return to seven and nine characters and compress the time to the present moment. **The change in character count substitutes for a change of metrical pattern: seven characters = narrative, five = clause, nine = pressure.**

**③ Speech inside song, the closing line given to the other (p0137–0138)**
> Husband (sings): I have taken him everywhere to seek a doctor, ／ I have taken him to the temple to pray to Guanyin. ／ But no medicine can cure him…
> Wife: How is he?
> Husband (sings): A bundle of bones, thin and frail.
> (Original: 夫（唱）我也曾带他四处去求医，／我也曾带他庙里拜观音。／无奈百药治不好，……／妻：他怎么样？／夫（唱）他一把骨头瘦伶仃。)

The upper line deliberately breaks after the third line, and the Wife's one spoken question asks out the final line. **The spoken line is inserted at the waist of an aria; its job is to turn the heaviest seven characters from narration into answer.** This is the typical way a life-like genre like Yongju divides labor between song and speech.

**④ Three questions, three answers (p0144, Scene Four)**
> Xiucai (sings): Do you not think of Qiubao, still so young? ／ Do you not think of him calling for his mother, tears streaming?
> Wife (sings): I do think of Chunbao, young as he is; ／ I think of him calling for his mother from morning till night.
> Xiucai (sings): Are you not afraid of the hardship of a poor home? ／ Are you not afraid of worrying over the next meal?
> Wife (sings): I have no choice—I was born to a poor and lowly fate; ／ I ask only that poor folk may stay together.
> Xiucai (sings): Are you not afraid your husband will never mend his ways? ／ Are you not afraid that in his desperation he will hire you out to a brothel?
> Wife (sings): My husband is good at heart; ／ I do not think he will fail me again. ／ And if he truly has lost his conscience like a beast, ／ I would rather hold Chunbao and throw myself in the lake.
> (Original: 秀才（唱）你不念秋宝孩儿年尚幼，／你不念他叫唤娘泪长流？／妻（唱）我也念春宝孩儿年也小，／我也念他日里叫亲娘叫到夜里头。／秀才（唱）你不怕回到穷家苦难受，／你不怕吃了上顿下顿愁？／妻（唱）无奈我生来就是贫贱命，／我只求穷苦人家长聚头。／秀才（唱）你不怕亲夫恶习总难改，／你不怕他穷极之时把你典卖到青楼？／妻（唱）我亲夫原本心地也善良，／我料他不会再将我辜负。／倘若他果然丧心似禽兽，／我情愿怀抱春宝把湖投。)

**The format is fixed**: questions always begin with the pair "do you not think of / are you not afraid"; answers always begin "I also think of / I have no choice / my husband." **After three rounds the answering side adds two extra lines to close**; the questions grow longer each round (7+7→9+9→10+13), and the answering side breaks form only in the third round with two extra lines—**the outcome of the duel is written in the number of lines.** This is the opposite closing method from *Golden Dragon and Mayfly*'s "questions grow shorter"; **keep the two side by side.**

**⑤ The longest passage: three years compressed into twelve verbs (p0147–0148, Scene Five)**
> Married one man, ／ bore one boy; ／ was a wife once, ／ was a mother once; ／ broke one home, ／ had one heart broken; ／ stepped over one old threshold— ／ floating, drifting, dazed, stumbling, ／ senseless, half alive and half dead, one foot fell into a new door wall. ／ A new door wall, where the soul cannot settle; ／ the heart is always in a panic. ／ A stranger, an old Xiucai, ／ drags me this way and that onto the bed. ／ The next year I bore a son, Qiubao, ／ and from then on Qiubao held my heart. ／ Leaving the old home, the child cries for its mother; ／ parting from the new home, the mother cries for her boy; ／ one child I cast behind me; ／ the other I set aside!
> (Original: 嫁了一个人，／生了一个郎；／做了一回妻，／当了一回娘；／破了一个家，／断了一回肠；／迈出了一道旧门槛——／飘飘忽忽，悠悠荡荡，浑浑噩噩，跌跌撞撞，／无知无觉，若生若死，一脚又跌进了新门墙。／新门墙，魂难放，／心头总是一个慌。／陌生一个老秀才，／横拉竖拖就上床。／来年生下了儿秋宝，／这秋宝儿从此牵住了我心房。／离旧家，儿哭娘；／别新家，娘哭郎；／我把一个儿抛下；／又把一个撇一旁！)

Five characters, six lines (three pairs of "× one / × one time") → long seven-character lines → **eight pairs of reduplicated words joined into two lines** → three-character anadiplosis ("new door wall") → seven-character narrative → three-character antithetical close (leave the old home / part from the new). Jiangyang rhyme presses down from beginning to end. **Three years, two homes, two sons—all packed into one passage, by changing gear four times from 5 to 7 to 4 to 3 characters. The banqiang system can produce the effect of sanban (散板 — free rhythm), yuanban (原板 — original pattern), kuaiban (快板 — fast pattern), and duoban (垛板 — duǒjù pattern) without marking metrical patterns; the switch is character count.**

### 5.6 Four Principles to Remember in Adapting Rou Shi's *A Slave Mother*

1. **The protagonist renamed "Wife"**—in the novella she is also nameless; the script makes namelessness a formal entry in the character list, and makes the Husband, the Xiucai, and the Senior Mistress nameless too.
2. **Added "three conditions"** (p0125–0126: give up drink and gambling / raise Chunbao well / at the end of three years I want to see a healthy son and a husband who has made something of himself), **each answered by thunder**. **The passive hiring is turned into a negotiation, and the character thereby has action. In the Epilogue all three conditions fail.**
3. **Added Sedan-chair Bearers A and B** (Scene Five): by the rules they may carry her only half the way, but the two volunteer to carry her home. **The only disinterested kindness in the play is placed in the last scene**, against the calculations of the Xiucai, the Senior Mistress, and the Husband.
4. **Qiubao and Chunbao never appear; only their voices remain.**

### 5.7 Three Notes on Dialogue and Characterization

1. **Spoken dialogue carries all the "transaction"; song carries all the "heart."** In Scene One the husband and wife's question-and-answer about the "hire" (p0122–0123) is entirely speech: "What is a hire? / It is a loan. / Marry? I have already married you—what is there to marry? / Not the 'marry' of taking a husband—the 'borrow' of borrowing a thing. / Borrow a thing—what thing?"—**a single word misheard (典 diǎn, "hire"; 嫁 jià, "marry"; 借 jiè, "borrow") makes the cruelty of the system plain, with not one line of song.** Song begins only after she has decided. **Information and negotiation belong to speech; decision and feeling belong to song.**
2. **The Xiucai's two faces are entirely in code-switching**: when flirting he quotes the *Classic of Poetry* (p0132); when he turns nasty he says, "I paid a hundred silver dollars for her; I am her husband. Never mind scolding—I will beat her" (p0141); at parting he sings, "I know your heart has already flown home" (p0144). **One character, three registers, marking his readiness to retreat at any moment into the identity of "buyer."** He says it himself: "Actually I do not know how many lines of what I say are true and how many false."
3. **The Senior Mistress is a three-stage pattern—first assert authority, then feign kinship, then turn nasty**: p0128 finds fault with her tears and her smile; p0129 sings "I call you little sister" and takes her hand as a sister; p0136 sings "gnashing my teeth, anger in my heart"; p0141 searches her, hires a canopy-less chair, and says "you may never come to see Qiubao as long as you live." **Each stage gets one aria; the distribution of arias is the division of the character.**

### 5.8 The Division of Labor Between *The Wife for Hire* and *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* in "the Modern Use of Convention"

| Traditional convention | *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* | *The Wife for Hire* |
|---|---|---|
| Self-introduction | Changed to others ask and the character answers, and it recurs a generation later (Fuyou / Zizi) | Changed to the "six there-is-no" aria of circumstance, supplemented by the Senior Mistress's interrogation |
| Entrance poem / exit poem | Offstage solo, accompaniment (7 passages) | Offstage songs of four seven-character lines (6) |
| Ghost / apparition | The Old King and the former kings speak with the living | In Scene Five she hears both children calling |
| Sedan-chair journey / tàngmǎ | None (replaced by dance and fighting) | Scene Five is a whole scene of sedan-chair travel, with three stops as breaks |
| Prop running through | The helmet (the master device of misrecognition) | The old umbrella (the unchanging circumstance of three years) |
| Same stage, different places | Three-place round singing (palace / hometown / road) | Not used (strictly one scene, one place) |
| Tumbling | During the castration Jinlong struggles and tosses on the throne | Not used (the most violent moment is only "she embraces her head and faints") |

**How to read it: the fable form calls on many and strange conventions; the realist form calls on few and steady ones. "How a modern play uses convention" must be advised in these two separate tracks; one set of advice will not do.**

**Huaiju *The Death of Han Xin* (1981, "my first real play," written before the age of 25, 13 scenes)**: 1 Changle Palace: the merits of the three heroes debated / 2 The Prince of Chu's mansion: sword dance · Zhongli Mei storms the mansion / 3 The rear hall: a duel in single seven-character lines / 4 Luan Zhong comes with a portrait to probe · Hu Tu recites the long "crawling between the legs" passage / 5 Weiyang Palace: Empress Lü and Shen Yiji conspire / 6 Changle Palace: court debate · the ruse of "a fake tour of Yunmeng" is proposed / 7 Qiulan brings word · Xiao He urges execution · Zhongli Mei sings the Song of Gaixia (《垓下歌》) and cuts his throat / 8 After the demotion to Marquis of Huaiyin, husband and wife talk at night · Chen Xi rebels and Han Xin asks to take command / 9 The reviewing stand: "the more the better" · the coded phrase "fiery horse" is left behind / 10 Weiyang Palace: the trap is set · the Luan brothers bring false charges · Xiao He's six-fold "no wonder" / 11 Xiao He lures Han Xin into the palace · Yin Taoniang's eight-line foreboding "you are not going… you are going to" / 12 Confronting Empress Lü · a long declaration of his wrongs / 13 The bell chamber: he refuses to escape through the water hole · three dying wishes · the bell tolls and he dies laughing. **Main line**: loyalty itself becomes a way to die. **Representative lyric** (p0042, Yin Taoniang), "symmetrical duǒjù lines of four negations + four affirmations": the first four lines—"you are not going… to the battlefield / to a distant place / to kill the enemy / to the frontier"—rule out every normal possibility; the last four—"you are going… not knowing the depth / not knowing good or ill / not knowing whether you will return"—pile on one layer after another and land on a specific person. **In 1981 this play is neat seven- and ten-character couplets throughout**; set beside *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* eleven years later, it measures exactly how much "urban new Huaiju" changed at the level of line pattern. Also: in Scene Thirteen the reason for refusing escape is locked in by the "crawling between the legs" humiliation already fully laid in Scene Four—**setup and close echo each other across nine scenes.**

**Huaiju *The Hegemon of Western Chu* (1994 Peking-opera original / 1998 adapted to Huaiju, prologue + 5 acts)**: Prologue, a monologue outside the curtain, "more than seventy battles, never defeated" / 1 Joining the army and crossing the river (Yu Ji, disguised as a man, presents the black steed Wuzhui; 【the whole company performs "qǐbà"】) / 2 Burying the troops alive and burning the palace ("Bury!"—one word sets the scene; Fan Zeng wants Han Xin killed; Xiang Yu lets him go) / 3 The siege battle ("I order a retreat of three arrow-flights—not for the hegemony, but for the beauty"; 【he leads the company in a "tàngmǎ" dance】) / 4 Visiting Han Xin and parting from Yu Ji (Yu Ji rides alone to seek out Han Xin and obtains the troop deployment map; she asks for fire, dances, cuts her throat, and falls into the flames) / 5 The sorrowful song at the Wu River (Wuzhui leaps into the river; Xiang Yu cuts his throat and his body does not fall; **before the curtain falls all the dead revive and form a "flying golden roc"**). **Main line**: four refusals form a line of action that destroys the self. **Representative lyric** (p0112): "six three-character lines in a row (cross the Wu River / resist the Qin army / enter Xianyang / destroy Ying Zheng / fight the King of Han / battle Han Xin) compress eight years of war into a single breath," while seven- and ten-character couplets hold it up before and after, and the renchen rhyme does not move—**what is stacked is a list of events, not adjectives.** Also: **this is the only script in the volume that writes the names of conventions straight into the stage directions**; the original words of the *Song of Gaixia* are given to three people in three places (Xiang Yu's rewritten version as lead song / Yu Ji hums it as she soothes a child to sleep / Xiang Yu sings the original words when he mourns Yu Ji)—**one ancient song in three variations, same words, different situations.**

**Huangmei opera *Southeast Flies the Peacock* (2002, with Wang Chang'an, 5 scenes + epilogue)**: 1 A Pact of the Heart (they play the konghou and blow the xun across the water, and recognize each other riding the old water buffalo; Mother Jiao suddenly bursts into tears) / 2 The Vow at the Peacock Terrace (with the old water buffalo as witness they bow to heaven and earth) / 3 The Wedding Night (Mother Jiao sleeps with a pillow in the middle of the bridal chamber; the three sit up and lie down in turn as they sing; the three aunts cry in unison, "Divorce! Divorce! Divorce!") / 4 Joy in the Weaving Room (Lan Zhi's six "why"s; Mother Jiao answers "she has committed no fault," "the food is not good," "she is not lazy"; the konghou is smashed and the signed divorce paper handed over) / 5 Sorrow by the Water (snow and wind at the Peacock Terrace; Lan Zhi mistakes the old water buffalo for Zhongqing; she coughs blood and red peacocks fill the sky) / Epilogue A Pact of Heaven and Earth (three years later two wedding processions meet, silently play a tune together, bow to each other, and part; the old water buffalo cannot bellow and falls dead). **Main line**: the Han yuefu tragedy is rewritten as "malice without a cause" (Mother Jiao: "I cannot explain it either… I just find you an eyesore"). **Representative passage** (p0177–0178): **two sets of "one to ten" numeric fùzibǎn** ("first, not greedy for food, second, not greedy for sleep… tenth, not harsh to my husband—I entered the Jiao house with no second thought… every part of me spent"): **not marked "(sings)" but spoken, yet the rhythm is the rhythm of song—the speech of the fùzibǎn style is a third register between song and speech.** Also: **the chorus sings the original Han yuefu poem directly**, six times; **the original poem serves as chorus text and the new lyrics as the characters' song; the two registers are never mixed**; **the old water buffalo is the play's only witness and its "silent opponent"** (nodding, shaking its head, bellowing, dying)—**a mute role written as the play's dramatic focus (戏眼, xìyǎn — the one-line core attraction).**

**Yuju *Journey to Fengshan* (2002, with Yao Jincheng, prologue + 7 scenes)**: Prologue, the edict is proclaimed and he goes to take up his post · soldiers seize Rang Hao / 1 Hai Mei's home · Yilu Bo presents the water-diversion map and then dies / 2 Wei Shitai and Dong Liang conspire; Lady Cao arrives with his mentor's one-word maxim "caution" (谨) / 3 Cuizhu Ridge · he drinks the mountain folk's last bowl of millet wine · reads out the proclamation pacifying the people on the spot · asks Rang Hao to get into Dong Liang's sedan chair / 4 The worksite · the relief grain is diverted to the outer islands / 5 The Taiwan prefectural yamen · he kneels before the steps for days and nights · a thunderclap and he collapses / 6 The worksite · Lady Cao offers a plan · "open the granary; I alone will bear it" · the granary is opened and grain distributed / 7 Longhou Ridge · the imperial commissioner reads the edict: Cao Jin is dismissed and sent home · a wedding is held at the worksite, the water is released, and farewells are said. **Main line**: trading his office for a canal. **Representative lyric** (p0213–0214): **four "asks" + six "do not asks" in two sets of duǒjù lines** (ask the great house / ask the gold plaque / ask your excellency / ask the magistrate; do not ask about disaster / do not ask about hardship / do not ask about mulberry and hemp / do not ask about the fields / do not ask about the common people / do not ask about the locality)—**ten-character lines to open, duǒjù lines to close, the more he curses the shorter and more urgent it gets**; one solo runs about 40 lines. **Structure to remember**: **the character 谨 (jǐn — caution) is used as the through-keyword** (his mentor renames him "for you I changed the 'jade' 瑾 in your name to the 'caution' 谨"; Lady Cao urges caution; Cao Jin sings "I must avoid the edge, grasp the key… walk cautiously out through this crack of sky"; at the end he breaks the rule for the people)—**one word is the play's opponent**; the ending changes Du Fu's "though lowly, I dare not forget my country" from "country" (国) to "the people" (民) (p0221), **one character changed is the play's whole argument.**

**Chuanju *Li Yaxian* (2007, after the Ming chuanqi *The Embroidered Coat* [《绣襦记》] and the Chuanju play of the same name, 5 scenes, every scene name containing the character 目 "eye")**: 1 Eyes Meeting, Love Passing / 2 Paired Eyes Parted / 3 Weeping Eyes, Listening to Song / 4 Piercing Her Eyes to Urge Him On / 5 Embroidering Eyes to Keep Her Image. **Main line**: fulfillment is separation. **Representative lyric** (p0236–0237): **the duet's "diminishing close"**: the madam and Li Yaxian go from five characters down to three, two, one, and finally exchange single characters ("I want wealth / I want the man / wealth / the man / wealth! / the man!")—**the rhythm is controlled entirely by character count, with no metrical marking needed**. **Two things unique to this play in the volume**: ① **bāngqiāng (帮腔 — offstage answering chorus)** (【bāngqiāng: "A heavenly immortal descends to the dusty world!"】【bāngqiāng: "One look and the soul is startled; the silk whip falls in the dust."】【bāngqiāng: "Change, change, change—" "Quick, quick, quick—"】), with three functions—voicing the heart, naming the action, prompting a change of breath; ② **the liánhuālào (莲花落 — lotus-fall narrative song) chorus** ("the Eight Immortals" = a gang of beggars parodying the Eight Immortals), each scene opening and closing with "Beggars, wild and mad / sing liánhuālào, mimic the Eight Immortals… ha ha, liánhuālào ye," **serving at once as announcer, commentator, time caption ("one season of spring goes, one season of summer comes"), and characters within the play who save people—one chorus does four jobs**. There is also **the liánhuālào style with piles of er-suffixed padding syllables** ("the little miser clutches a broken gourd ladle…"): **the padding syllables carry identity (beggar child) and rhythm, not rhyme.**

**Qinqiang *Yang Guifei* (2000 musical *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* [《长恨歌》] original → Huangmei opera → 2007 Qinqiang, overture + 4 acts, act names are places)**: Overture (offstage chorus sings *Song of Everlasting Sorrow*, "The Han emperor, loving beauty, longed for a beauty that could topple a kingdom") / 1 The Hunt at the Foot of Mount Li (Yang Yuhuan, consort of the Prince of Shou, bursts onto the scene and is "hunted" as an assassin) / 2 Summoned to the Hot Spring Palace (granted a bath and made Imperial Consort; the Prince of Shou, Consort Mei, and An Lushan sing their separate thoughts on the same stage; the *Rainbow Skirt and Feathered Robe* tune; the war drums of Yuyang) / 3 Death at Mawei Slope (An Lushan sings the "wolf and sheep" song; the two armies "hunt" a second time; Yang Yuhuan gets the emperor drunk and goes calmly to her death; the Prince of Shou cuts his throat) / 4 Dreaming Back to the Palace of Eternal Life (years later the aged Li Guinian performs on the street, sings the whole plot and breaks a string; Consort Mei on her deathbed praises her as "the first woman of past and present"; on the seventh night of the seventh month they meet in a dream at the Palace of Eternal Life; the retired emperor dies, and the funeral becomes a wedding). **Main line**: the whole play runs on the single metaphor of the hunt—**the same staging appears twice, and the second time it kills the person the first hunt caught.** **Representative lyric** (p0270, An Lushan): **the villain's worldview written as the permutations of two nouns**: "If you are strong, you are a wolf; / if you are weak, you are a sheep. / A wolf must come to eat the sheep… When the wolf comes, you must pretend to be a sheep; / when the sheep comes, you must turn back into a wolf. / … A wolf is not a sheep, / a sheep is not a wolf. / A wolf is a sheep, / a sheep is a wolf." Also: **Bai Juyi's original *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* is divided into nine segments and used as offstage chorus** to string the play's timeline together (the same method as Huangmei opera's use of the Han yuefu); at the opening of Act Four Li Guinian's street performance recaps the whole plot, the string breaks, and the spectators clap and scatter—**a character retells the play in public and the audience's indifference becomes part of the plot; this is the volume's only metatheatrical structure.**

### 6.3 A Single Table of Line Patterns Across Six Genres (Induced from This Volume)

> ⚠️ **Everything in this table is induced from the aria texts of eight scripts, not copied from any book of operatic music theory.** It cannot replace a "metrical pattern—line pattern—emotion" correspondence table of the kind found in Liu Jidian's *Introduction to Peking Opera Music* or Fan Shanglin's *A Collection of Yuju Metrical Patterns*; what it can do is **answer a question those tables cannot: when a playwright does not mark metrical patterns, what does he use instead? Answer: character count.** "Colloquialism level" is a ranking internal to this volume, not an absolute property of a genre.

| Opera genre (play) | Dominant line pattern | Colloquialism level | Main rhyme (summarized by Mandarin) | Signature format | Chorus form |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Huaiju (The Death of Han Xin / Golden Dragon and Mayfly / The Hegemon of Western Chu) | seven characters as the base; after 1992 the variance is very large (3–14 characters) | medium (spoken dialogue extremely colloquial) | jiangyang (江阳), renchen (人辰) | long exchanges of one seven-character line per person; event-list duǒjù lines | offstage solo / accompaniment (none in the 1981 play) |
| Yongju (The Wife for Hire) | mainly seven characters, ten-character lines second | **highest** (no allusions, no four-six parallel prose) | jiangyang, youqiu (由求) | parallelism and refrain; **speech inserted into song** | offstage singing, one poem of four seven-character lines |
| Huangmei opera (Southeast Flies the Peacock) | five and seven characters equal, three-character fast cuts | fairly low (elegant) | yanqian (言前), renchen | fùzibǎn-style speech (two sets, one to ten); three-person round singing | chorus sings the original Han yuefu poem |
| Yuju (Journey to Fengshan) | **ten characters (3-3-4) overwhelmingly dominant** | stratified: official classical / folk colloquial | yanqian, jiangyang | "ask / do not ask" paired duǒjù lines; one person can carry 40 lines in a passage | offstage singing (mostly for entrances) |
| Chuanju (Li Yaxian) | most miscellaneous (bāngqiāng short lines + liánhuālào er-suffixation) | medium | renchen, zhongdong (中东), youqiu | duet diminishing to one character; piles of padding syllables | **bāngqiāng (can be embedded inside a sung line) + liánhuālào chorus** |
| Qinqiang (Yang Guifei) | **four- and five-character lines, the shortest** | lowest (elegant, written) | jiangyang, renchen | lead line repeated fourfold / sevenfold; anadiplosis duet | offstage chorus, nine segments of *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* |

**Four commonalities across genres**: ① **all mark only "(sings)"; no metrical pattern, no qupai, no gongs and drums** (not one of the eight is an exception)—**what the playwright hands over is character count and line pattern; the metrical pattern is fitted by the composer**; ② **all seven plays after 1992 are equipped with a "chorus" layer (歌队, gēduì)** (the only exception is *The Death of Han Xin*, 1981), **a layer belonging to no character**, handling three things: establishing time and place, translating inner life, commenting on events, **and it entirely replaces the traditional entrance poem and exit poem**—in the eight plays of this volume the exit poem does not appear even once, and the entrance poem appears only once each in *The Death of Han Xin* and *Li Yaxian*; **the appearance of the chorus and the disappearance of the "(recites)" marker and the entrance poem are two sides of the same thing**; ③ **all use short "one line per person" duets for confrontation, and all close by changing character count**—four closing methods **stand side by side and must not be merged**: Huaiju shortens the question and keeps the answer (p0083), Yongju adds two lines to the answer in the last round (p0144), Chuanju diminishes both sides to one character (p0236), Qinqiang uses anadiplosis and closes on three characters (p0283); ④ **paired upper and lower lines (上下句, shàngxiàjù), one rhyme to the end of a passage** remain the foundation of every genre; jiangyang is the volume's universal broad rhyme, and four of the six genres' longest passages rhyme in jiangyang.

**A paragraph on differences**: in character count, Yuju favors ten characters (3-3-4), Qinqiang four and five, Yongju and Huangmei opera seven, Huaiju takes seven as its base but *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* has the greatest variance, and Chuanju is the most miscellaneous because of bāngqiāng and liánhuālào; colloquialism from high to low runs roughly **Yongju > Yuju (folk scenes) > Huaiju > Chuanju > Huangmei opera > Qinqiang**, while Yuju itself is stratified by class into a classical and a colloquial layer; in chorus, **only Chuanju's bāngqiāng is embedded inside the sung line (it can interrupt a line of song); the choruses of the other genres stay at the head or tail of a scene and never enter the interior of an aria passage—this is the hardest textual dividing line between gāoqiāng (高腔 — high-pitched vocal system) and the other banqiang genres.**

**Two cross-cutting conclusions**: ① ***The Death of Han Xin* (1981) is the only play in the volume that keeps "(recites)," the entrance poem, and no chorus**—the three disappear together, which shows that for Luo Huaizhen they are one and the same apparatus: **the traditional "exposition device" is replaced wholesale by chorus + surtitles**; when writing the skill, these three should be taught as one item, not split apart; ② **the "poetic sublimation" ending appears in fable / legend subjects (*The Hegemon of Western Chu*, *Southeast Flies the Peacock*, *Yang Guifei*, *Golden Dragon and Mayfly*), while realist subjects (*The Wife for Hire*, *Journey to Fengshan*, *Li Yaxian*, *The Death of Han Xin*) all end realistically**—**this is a clear dividing line, not a stylistic preference.** Self-check: if a realist play wants a sublimated ending, **first ask whether any of its first four scenes laid a non-realist scene as preparation**—in the four realist plays of this volume, not one does.

### 6.5 Traces in the Two Adaptation Scripts and a Gap in This Volume

- ***The Hegemon of Western Chu***: the 1994 original was a Peking-opera script, adapted to Huaiju in 1998 (author's note at the end, p0118). The trace is that it is **the only play that explicitly writes "qǐbà" and "tàngmǎ"**—Peking-opera convention vocabulary left in a Huaiju script; together with *The Death of Han Xin* in the same volume it forms **two plays about the same group of characters** (Han Xin, Zhongli Mei, the halberd-bearer), **usable as a controlled sample of "the same material, thirteen years apart, two treatments."**
- ***Yang Guifei***: the 2000 original was the musical *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* (originally also titled *The Imperial Consort of Tang* [《大唐皇妃》]), later adapted into Huangmei opera, **and the Qinqiang script is yet another adaptation made on the basis of the musical book and the Huangmei script** (author's note at the end, p0284; afterword). The trace is its **density of four- and five-character short lines** and its **large stretches of "lead line + seven repetitions" song-lyric texture** (p0278, "Yuhuan has gone and left you—who will share your mornings and evenings / watch the morning star / drink wine / play the jade lute / bathe in the hot spring / comb your cloud-like hair / ask after your illness in detail")—**this is musical-theatre lyric writing left in the Qinqiang script; when using it as a sample of Qinqiang aria text, discount it—it did not grow out of Qinqiang tradition.**
- **One gap to be filled**: ***Cold Plum* (《寒梅》)** (Luo Huaizhen's only modern-dress Huaiju, adapted from the film *Daughter of the Party* [《党的女儿》] and the Huaiju play of the same name) **is not included in this volume** (afterword item 6). → **"How a modern play uses convention" has only *The Wife for Hire* (and that is a Republican-era play) to support it in the Luo Huaizhen material.**

## §7 Afterword to the Local-Opera Volume, Creative Chronology, and Cross-References to the Theory Volume

### Ten Items from the Afterword to the Local-Opera Volume (p0286)

1. The Huaiju *The Death of Han Xin* is "my first real play," **written before the age of 25**.
2. At the time scripts were hard to print, **so his first wife, Wang Jinjian (王谨坚), copied it out character by character.**
3. "To commemorate the callow years that have passed, **apart from weighing a few individual words, I have kept the original appearance entirely**." → ***The Death of Han Xin* included in this volume is the unrevised 1981 original, and is therefore a reliable sample for "the same author eleven years apart."**
4. Huaiju *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* and *The Hegemon of Western Chu* were written while he worked at the **Shanghai Huaiju Troupe**, and **both premieres were billed as "urban new Huaiju."**
5. *The Hegemon of Western Chu* **was originally a Peking-opera script**, was published and won a drama-literature award, **and was later adapted and staged as Huaiju.**
6. The Shanghai Huaiju Troupe also staged his modern-dress script *Cold Plum*, **which is not included this time.**
7. Yongju *The Wife for Hire* was premiered by the **Ningbo Municipal Yongju Troupe of Zhejiang Province.**
8. Huangmei opera *Southeast Flies the Peacock* **was written with Wang Chang'an**; Yuju *Journey to Fengshan* **was written with Yao Jincheng**; Chuanju *Li Yaxian* was premiered by the **Chongqing Municipal Chuanju Troupe.**
9. Qinqiang *Yang Guifei* **was born from his musical *Song of Everlasting Sorrow***, was adapted into Huangmei opera and performed by the Anhui Huangmei Opera Theatre; **the Qinqiang script is yet another adaptation made on the basis of the musical book and the Huangmei script.**
10. **"In principle, different opera genres and different versions of the same script are not included twice; Qinqiang *Yang Guifei* is the only exception."**—this is the volume's editorial rule; it means **every play in the volume represents one independent treatment by one opera genre, and cross-genre comparison is a reading the editor himself permits.**

### Entries Related to Composition in the Captions (Creative Chronology)

- **On 23 October 1986 *The Real and Fake Prince Consort* (《真假驸马》) was premiered by the Shanghai Yue Opera Theatre—the first time one of his scripts reached the stage.**
- In 1989 the Yue opera *Xi Shi Returns to Yue* (《西施归越》) premiered, "two changes of director, two changes of troupe, three changes of lead"; after the premiere the company dispersed and it was never performed again.
- **In the nine years from 1991**, *Golden Dragon and Mayfly*, *The Hegemon of Western Chu*, *Snake Love* (《蛇恋》), *Ban Zhao* (《班昭》), *The Lotus Lantern* (《宝莲灯》), *Meilong Town* (《梅龙镇》), *Li Qingzhao* (《李清照》), and *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* **were all written at the same desk, and all in longhand, without a computer** (he switched to a computer only in 2002).
- **In May 1993, at the Shanghai Majestic Theatre, the urban new Huaiju *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* premiered**; three months earlier the author had been hit by a car and badly injured internally, and was supported into the theatre to watch the performance.
- **On 30 September 1993 he went to Chengdu for the National Local-Opera Festival**, "the first time my work appeared on the national dramatic stage"; in October of the same year he performed in Beijing, and Chen Gongmin (陈恭敏) introduced him to **Zhang Geng (张庚) and Guo Hancheng (郭汉城)**.
- In 1994 he won the "First National Cao Yu Drama Literature Award" for *Golden Dragon and Mayfly*; afterwards *Ban Zhao*, *Xi Shi Returns to Yue*, and *The Wife for Hire* won awards in succession.
- **"After the whirlwind of *Golden Dragon and Mayfly*, I went through a full five years of stagnation in Shanghai"**; during this period he sought work elsewhere (Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Guangdong, Shanxi, and Taiwan all staged his plays), **and from then on began to advocate "the modernization of Chinese opera" and "the urbanization of Chinese opera."** → **Four of the seven plays in the Yue-opera volume (*Snake Love* 1993, *Li Qingzhao* 1999, *Liu Rushi* [《柳如是》] 1999, *Meilong Town* 2000) fall in this period of stagnation and after it, and all are either for out-of-town companies or rewrites of old drafts**, which matches his own account.
- The slogan of the Taiwanese actress Kuo Hsiao-chuang (郭小庄), "belonging to tradition, to modernity, to you and me," coincided with his "**walk into tradition, walk into modernity, walk into you and me**"—these twelve Chinese characters were his slogan of the period.
- In 1997 he lectured at Cheng Kung University on "Listening to the Echo of History"; in a 2001 interview the article was titled "The Opera Stage Must Consciously Resist the Craze for Glitter."

→ **"Urban new opera" has definite works, years, and performance occasions** (written 1992, premiered in Shanghai in May 1993, performed at the Chengdu festival in September 1993); **when writing the skill, do not turn it into a generality.** ***The Death of Han Xin* (1981) and *The Hegemon of Western Chu* (1994) are two plays about the same group of characters; with *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* (1992) added, the three Huaiju plays form the only time series in this batch of material from which the author's changes in technique can be measured.**

## §8 Addenda on Technique (Two Items Not Fully Covered in §6 and §7⑥ of SKILL.md)

1. **Repetition falls into two methods; do not mix them.** ① **Not one word changed, function unchanged** = the frame of the whole play (the theme song of *Snake Love* 4 times; the offstage singing at head and tail of *The Real and Fake Prince Consort* 2 times; the theme song of *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* 3 times); ② **not one word changed, function different each time** = a thermometer of relationships (*Meilong Town*'s children's song 5 times: humming → answering → declaring love → a child's voice as omen → reconciliation). *Failure condition*: if one play uses both methods, the audience cannot tell whether the repetition is a "frame" or a "keepsake." *Self-check*: list the scene and purpose of each appearance of the repeated item; **if the purpose column is all the same, it is method ①; if all different, method ②; half same and half different means it is badly written.**
2. **In adaptation, give a passive protagonist one negotiation.** In *The Wife for Hire*, before entering the sedan chair the Wife names three conditions (give up drink and gambling / raise Chunbao well / at the end of three years I want to see a healthy son and a husband who has made something of himself), **each answered by thunder; in the Epilogue all three fail.** **The passive hiring becomes a negotiation, and the character thereby has action.** *Failure condition*: if the added conditions are not settled one by one at the end, the negotiation is merely padding. *Self-check*: go through the conditions the protagonist names and check that each is accounted for by the final curtain.
