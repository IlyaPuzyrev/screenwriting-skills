## I. The Pilot Beat Sheet: Calvisi's Benchmark, the Full Beat Chain, and Miller's Landmark Model

### 1.1 Calvisi's Benchmark (Teaser plus five)

**Calvisi's Benchmark (Teaser plus five)**: Teaser 2–10 pages → Act One 12–15 pages → Act Two 6–10 pages (ending around p. 30) → Act Three 8–12 pages (ending on p. 40) → Act Four 6–12 pages (ending on p. 48) → Act Five 2–8 pages.

### 1.2 The Full Beat Chain

**The beat chain**: OPENING SEQUENCE → **CATALYST** (teaser act out) → establishment of the A/B/C/D stories + entrance of the New Arrival / Shadow / Dynamic Ally → **INCITING INCIDENT** → **TURN / Roadblock** (Act One act out) → Aftermath → **FIRST TRIAL / FIRST CASUALTY** → New Complications → **MIDPOINT** (Act Two act out, with a ticking time clock) → Aftermath → **SHADOW SHOWDOWN** → Partial Integration → **ASSUMPTION OF POWER** (Act Three act out) → Skirmishes / Integration / Stopping Points → **DECLARATION OF WAR + ALL IS LOST** (Act Four act out) → **CLIMAX (A, B)** → **EPILOGUE / THE NEW WORLD** (or BIG ENDING) → **HANGING QUESTION / CLIFFHANGER**.

### 1.3 Two Directly Verifiable Numbers and Four Television-Specific Beats

**Two directly verifiable numbers**: **AOP falls on pp. 37–40**; **DOW falls at roughly the 4/5 point of the whole script**—proportionally, the Shadow Showdown in *Scandal*, *True Detective*, and *The Walking Dead* all fall at around 4/5 of the way through (TWD's pilot runs 66 minutes because its premiere slot was extended). **Four television-specific beats** (use them as a dedicated checklist when writing a pilot): **Assumption of Power, Declaration of War, Shadow Showdown, First Trial / First Casualty**.

### 1.4 Calvisi's Own Limits

**Calvisi's own limits**: beats overlap and shift forward or backward; "this is not an exact science. It cannot perfectly fit every one-hour television pilot."; some shows will subvert it ("Game of Thrones with its multi-protagonist structure comes to mind"); with four-act shows (True Detective, Mad Men) you have to merge acts yourself. The qualifier repeated twice in the book: **"This is form, not formula."** Another hard requirement: **"Act One should be able to operate as its own closed story, with a beginning, middle and end**, albeit with a cliffhanger at the end." And the Act One act out must be "a shocking yet inevitable turn in direction."

### 1.5 Miller's Landmark Model (Chapter 3)

**Miller's landmark model** (Chapter 3)—it gives no page numbers, only anchors act breaks. Alignment: **point of attack falls inside the teaser** ("In television, the point of attack generally happens in the teaser.") / end of Act I = end of Act One / mid-point = end of Act Two / end of Act II = end of Act Three / climax in Act Four. Six landmarks: **point of attack** (the first hint of the problem) → **hero committed** (before this he could still turn back to his old life) → **hero succeeds—almost** (midpoint; **"It's not 'the hero fails'"**—the audience needs to see what success looks like before it is taken away) → **emotional squeeze** (a choice between two bad options, and the choice must show that the character has changed) → **hero succeeds** (often with an emotional twist) → **hero's new life**. The pilot's hero committed should kill two birds with one stone: in *The Knick*, Thackery accepts the post of chief of surgery while insisting that his deputy must be Gallinger rather than Edwards, whom the board wants—one action simultaneously commits the protagonist to the whole season and sets up this episode's obstacle. **Which landmarks work as act outs** (read off the grid for *The Walking Dead*'s "Days Gone Bye"): point of attack, succeeds—almost, and new life are all good act breaks, and so is Rick being shot at the end of Act One; **the squeeze usually has little suspense—"needs to play out and needs a solid resolution."** **The tragedy variant**: the squeeze flips positive and the climax flips negative (*It's Always Sunny*'s "The Gang Gets Racist")—"a tragedy is the inverse of a comedy."

### 1.6 Three Strategies for Ending a Pilot (Landau 1e, Tip 17)

**Three strategies for ending a pilot (Landau 1e, Tip 17)**: **Launching the Franchise** (the last two words of *The Killing* pilot script are "Find him.") / **The Final Montage** (*Boardwalk Empire*, *Friday Night Lights*) / **The Final Discovery** (in *The Shield*, Vic shoots his colleague Terry Crowley in the face). The bottom line: "give us something to discover by the end." The accompanying principle of withholding: "**don't tell us everything in the pilot**."

### 1.7 Structure Is Inherited: Act-and-Page Comparison of the *Breaking Bad* Pilot and #308

- **Structure is inherited.** Calvisi compares the *Breaking Bad* pilot (T 1-3 / A1 4-17 / A2 18-31 / A3 32-43 / A4 44-57) with the same show's #308 (T 1-4 / 5-15 / 16-31 / 32-42 / 43-53): the series follows the pilot's act structure and page ranges. **Writing a pilot means setting the structural mold for the entire series.**

## II. The Episode Container: Page Anchors, Scene-Count Ratios, Act-Count Conversion, and Pages/Running Time

### 2.1 Page Anchors for Four Acts (Douglas, Chinese trans. pp. 0090–0091)

**Page anchors** (Douglas, Chinese trans. pp. 0090–0091, a four-act first draft including the teaser): **Act One ends on pp. 17–18 / Act Two p. 30 / Act Three p. 45 / Act Four around p. 60 ("or anywhere between pages 55 and 65")**. The reason: commercials "break roughly every 13 to 15 minutes... after the commercials there is just under 50 minutes left, and each act is under 15 minutes—a little over 12 minutes."

### 2.2 Scene Counts and Three Proportional Schemes (Douglas / Oberg / Chase)

Douglas's arithmetic: "If an act is 14 minutes long and each scene is about 2 minutes, 14 divided by 2 equals 7." Flexibility: five solid scenes can fill an act, and "episodic" shows can run to 9–10 scenes. Totals: **a four-act one-hour show generally has around 28 scenes; a five- or six-act show only 20**. Two other cross-checking measures: **Oberg's ratio**—A about 50% (14–15 scenes), B about one-third (9–10 scenes), C the remainder (4–5 scenes), about 30 in total, though he warns that "modern scenes run about 1 minute, important scenes 3–4 minutes, and the old model of 'two-minute scenes' is out of date"; **Chase (foreword to *The Sopranos*)**—about 35 beats per episode, "thirteen or so in the A and B stories, usually five to six in the C story, and sometimes a D story with just a few beats."

### 2.3 Converting Four Acts into Five or Six

**Douglas (Chinese trans. pp. 0091–0092)**: start by conceiving a four-act show → **stretch the teaser to about 10 pages and make it a new act** ("you must bring the element of danger, controversy, or action directly into the story right at the start") → each subsequent act runs about 10 pages, and with six acts about 8 pages each. **The six-act tail design** (the most useful one): often all the arcs are completed by the end of Act Five, "then a very short Act Six is used to add an unexpected turn, and that turn... stirs up a new topic or a new challenge rather than putting an end to the existing storyline"—that is, **Act Six is actually the next episode's teaser** (*Nip/Tuck*). Other answers: **Peter Blake (*House*)** writes four acts and then adds two act breaks, **splitting in half whichever act is longest afterward**; **Miller**: six acts = four acts with **Acts Three and Four each split into two**, and the new act breaks must still "hit... hard with some holy shit! moments"; **Douglas's way of identifying a five-act show**: "look for which two acts are the shortest."

### 2.4 Page Counts and Running Times (Figures from Various Sources)

**A one-hour pilot runs 54–58 pages, with a ceiling of 60** (Calvisi); **one-hour shows run 48–63 pages**, and fast-paced shows (*Scandal*, *The West Wing*, *E.R.*) can exceed 85 pages (Landau); **drama pilots run 55–65 pages** (Miller); **a one-hour drama runs about 60 pages, teaser + four acts** (Blum, Chapter 14). Running times (Oberg, 2023): ad-supported networks 42 minutes / premium 56 / streaming 45–75 / Europe 50–52 / half-hour comedy 22. Landau 2e's trend judgment (*WandaVision*): "**Varying episode length is now a common trend along with shorter season orders. There is no standard, predictive pattern or formula.**"

> **Recency flag**: Blum (Chinese translation, early 2000s) and Goldberg & Rabkin (2003) belong to the **classic broadcast-network system** (act counts determined by ad slots); Landau 1e (2014) reflects the peak of five/six-act broadcast-network shows; Landau 2e (2021) and Oberg (2023) reflect the fact that "there is no single industry standard." When citing page counts, label them with the book's year.

### 2.5 Should ACT Be Written on the Page? Five Positions

- **Landau 2e**: **break story and write the outline around act breaks** (you can rename them **turning points**), **but delete the labels from the final draft**—"story over format."
- **The opposing view on the same page**: many showrunners still want to see act breaks on the page, "as evidence that the writer has a clear grasp of the structural twists and turns."
- **Miller, Chapter 9**: whether to include act breaks is **a market judgment**—if the material can go to a broadcast network or a non-premium cable channel, you must write them; "Leaving out act breaks can make you look like you don't understand television."
- **Mazzara (TWD)**: the pilot had no act breaks and was later changed to teaser + 5 acts (AMC wanted an extra ad slot); "It forces you to make choices. **It's about compression.**"
- **Alex Pina (*Money Heist*)** offered a solution for platforms where you don't know where the ad breaks fall: "We write in three acts, but permeating these with many **milestone moments**."

## III. Teaser / Cold Open: Ten Types, Length Figures, and a List of Shows Where It Is Not Essential

- **Ten types** (Douglas's seven paradigms + Landau's three): the world portrait (protagonist absent)｜the cinematic anomaly (protagonist doesn't know yet)｜arrival at the scene after the fact｜the case happens first｜the ensemble's day begins｜the thematic tie-in (unrelated to this episode's story)｜the environmental cue｜**bookend**｜**(seemingly) innocuous**｜**DPU (direct pickup)**.
- **Length**: Douglas (Chinese trans. p0097)—the teaser, or cold open, is the dramatic material before the title credits, and can be "a one-minute 'hook,' or as long as 10 minutes of assorted short scenes, making the teaser almost equal to a traditional act." Landau 1e Tip 15: about 5 pages on the page (**the teaser of *The Good Wife* pilot is 16 pages**). Oberg is tighter: **within 3–5 minutes, and usually ends on a cliffhanger or a surprise**.
- **The teaser is not essential**: Landau 2e—"not every series uses a teaser… and just starts in act 1" (*GoT*, *Sopranos*, *Homeland*, *Mad Men* go straight into Act One; *Grey's Anatomy* and *Once Upon a Time* are "six acts without a teaser"; *Scandal* and *Modern Family* use "a short first act as the teaser"). Oberg: a teaser is only wanted when it serves a dramatic purpose; otherwise it is "just pointless."

## IV. Invisible Acts: A Test of the Three Criteria in *Succession* and Season 4 Container Data

### 4.1 The Three Criteria, One by One

1. **Story-day / time-block markers**—`day one / day two / day three` (S3E9, S4E5, S4E10), on-screen clocks (S4E01's "LA 12 p.m."), timestamps (S4E08's 4:54 P.M.→3:40 A.M.). **A new day means a new act.**
2. **Changes of location and mode of transport**—S3E9 moves from the villa → boat → Pienza → parking lot → car → villa, six acts corresponding to six spaces; "a car = a moving locked room, one space for an entire act."
3. **The moment the core question turns**, often closing on a "loneliness-button" stage direction (at the end of Act Two of S3E9, Roman is on the lake scrolling through his phone—"No one to call."). The test: **at the end of every act there must be a transfer of information or power**; a suspense hook is not required.

### 4.2 Container Patterns and Scene Counts Across the Ten Episodes of *Succession* S4

A companion pattern (*Succession* S4, ten episodes): **8 of the 10 episodes compress the whole episode into a container with a fixed procedure** (a wedding / retreat / investor day / party / election night / funeral), and **the milestones of the fixed procedure replace act breaks**; when no ready-made procedure exists, they mark the breaks on the page—**which is to say, streaming writers are still writing acts, they just disguise them as a schedule.** Scene count is inversely proportional to container density: a single enclosed day runs 34–42 scenes; a multi-location, multi-strand episode runs 76–78. Landau 1e Tip 19 also offers a reverse-engineering demonstration of the six act positions in *Homeland*'s "The Vest."

## V. Act Out and Cliffhanger: A Library of Types and Examples

### 5.1 Ten Kinds of Cliffhanger (Seven from Landau 2e + Three from 1e)

2e's seven: **① character in peril｜② ticking bomb｜③ love is in the balance｜④ surprise twist / aha moment｜⑤ death of a character** ("figuratively re-setting chess pieces and altering relationships") **｜⑥ discovery of a secret｜⑦ life events**. 1e adds three: **⑧ lack of closure** (*Sopranos*' finale cuts to black—"if a cliffhanger feels too ambiguous or information is withheld, there can be viewer backlash"; the counterexample is *The Killing* S1, which does not reveal the killer, "Lessons learned.") **｜⑨ mini-cliffhangers = the act out itself｜⑩ comedy cliffhangers** (the break between Acts One and Two in a two-act sitcom: something upsets the balance, and Act Two is the effort to restore harmony).

Two of the most transferable moves: **complete a reversal with a single word** (*Succession* S2 finale: Kendall first recites the compliant version in full, then says "But."); **do not go out on the laugh—hold the revelation** (*Hacks* S1 finale—"going out with the anticipation of what will happen when Deborah finds out").

### 5.2 Where Cliffhangers Go and How Long They Can Wait

- **Placement and timing**: one-hour shows save the climactic cliffhanger for the end of the episode; **in multi-cam sitcoms the cliffhanger usually comes at the midpoint** (the ending must hit the reset button and return to the status quo). You can postpone revealing the whole truth, **but make sure it is revealed before the season ends**—stretching it past the first season "is perhaps testing your patient audience's good will."

### 5.3 Eight Types of Act Out Tested on *The West Wing*, the Wordless Act Out in "Two Cathedrals," and Four Types from *Downton*

Eight types tested across six episodes of *The West Wing*: **a suspenseful question / a reversal line / an emotional revelation / a wordless action / a freeze-frame on an object / a repeated line whose meaning reverses / a setup cut short / a gesture that pays off a plant**; "only the Pilot and A Proportional Response used a traditional hook; the last four episodes increasingly favored emotional act outs." Example: at the end of Act One of the Pilot, Josh looks at a photo and says, "That's a good picture of her."—**an act out can be a gentle whisper, as long as it opens a new strand.** Companion rules: **give each act out to a different strand**; **use a wordless action or an object for at least one act out—do not use dialogue for all four**—a wordless act out must first teach its grammar (in Act Three of "Two Cathedrals," Mrs. Landingham recites "hands in pockets, look away, smile" item by item in dialogue; at the end of Act Four, when Bartlet makes that gesture, the screen cuts to black and the answer has never been spoken). **Four types from *Downton*** (Fellowes's footnote): a request that crosses a boundary｜a hidden fact spoken in one line ("He is standing.")｜a Dowager-style one-line button ("Worse luck.")｜a physical fact (a corpse, handcuffs, a collapse into sobs).

### 5.4 An Act Out Can Be Purely an Information Event: A Two-Episode Comparison from Goldberg & Rabkin's Appendix

**An act out can be purely an information event.** Goldberg & Rabkin compare two episodes in their appendix: *Martial Law*'s act out is physical (someone is holding an armed bomb and cannot move a muscle), while *seaQuest 2032*'s "Depths of Deceit" act outs are entirely "who has what / who has lost what" (one act out stacks a dead man coming back to life + an ally arrested for treason + the antagonist suspended). The two episodes share three general laws: **① the end of Act One is not the biggest crisis but "the protagonist pushed into a situation with no way back"; ② the Act Two break must change the nature of the story, not merely raise the danger; ③ the end of Act Three must turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat.**

### 5.5 Acceleration: Rabkin's *24*/*Scandal* Comparison and the L+3 Logic

Rabkin (2017): **24 is the art of the act break**, with a big reversal before every commercial—but the new scene merely overturns what came before, "the illusion of movement when there really was none," a gimmick; **Scandal delivers "major changes not only in plot, but in character and story every seven or eight minutes."** His direct instruction for a spec pilot: **"That really cool thing that you have happening at the end of act four – move it to the end of the teaser. And then keep that pace up for the next sixty pages."** The industry logic is the L+3 ratings window—the only way is for every episode to have "the scene everyone is talking about the next day" (Ned Stark's beheading, Will Gardner shot in the courtroom, Sophia walking out of the barn), "deliberately planted … a requirement of contemporary television"; "A plot twist that comes after your reader has stopped reading is the same as no plot twist at all." **Streaming can drop three things**: the "previously on"; the implicit in-story recap (one of Netflix's notes on *Jessica Jones* was that she spent too much time rehashing previous episodes); and the scene reset after a commercial.

## VI. The Four Information-Management Tools, Four Red Lines, and the Rhythm Formula of Suspense

| Tool | Definition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Dramatic irony** | The audience knows something that at least one character does not (that character is the victim) | → suspense; or make the **antagonist the victim** (*The Night Manager*). In *Ted Lasso* S1, Rebecca hires Ted to destroy the team—by S2 this irony ends, and "takes away a significant part of the story engine." Companion **ironic question**: "How and when will the victim find out, and how will they react?" |
| **Surprise** | Information suddenly given to the audience that it did not expect | → raise the stakes; in comedy, "almost every gag is a surprise" |
| **Mystery** | Give the audience just enough information to "know that they don't know" | Slow burn (*Broadchurch*, *Mare of Easttown*). Risk: once a mystery is introduced, the intellectual process starts, and if it drags on too long it becomes tiresome. Recommendation: **one big mystery per season, resolved at the season finale and replaced with a new one** (*Lost* is the successful exception) |
| **Suspense** | The audience is aware of danger | **Does not necessarily require dramatic irony**—"As long as the audience knows where the threat comes from, there is suspense, even if all the characters are aware of the danger." (The Joyce strand in *Stranger Things*: "There is no mystery about the reality of the danger.") |

Four red lines:

1. **Do not let the protagonist know more than the audience for long stretches**, especially backstory needed to understand their actions and emotions. Counterexample: *Sharp Objects*—"we struggle to identify with her emotionally because we don't know what caused the trauma." Solution A: let the audience catch up quickly (in *Happy Valley* S1 they catch up within the first episode); Solution B: let the protagonist not know either—amnesia, coma (*The Walking Dead*: Rick is shot at minute 10, and when he wakes up he catches up alongside the audience, **avoiding a long stretch of exposition**), repressed memory. A mystery about the main character or the antagonist is fine (*Stranger Things*' Eleven).
2. **For a cliffhanger, prefer an "information bomb"; do not just cut a fight in half.** The end-of-episode cliffhanger in the *Transparent* pilot is achieved entirely through information management—"purely through managing information."
3. **closed mystery vs. open mystery**: closed = neither the protagonist nor the audience knows (*True Detective*, *Broadchurch*); **an open mystery is actually dramatic irony** (*Columbo* at the episode level, *Happy Valley* S1 at the season level)—the audience knows whodunit, and the mystery is when and how the protagonist will solve it.
4. **"you can have some mystery over the nature of the antagonist, but not over the reality of the danger it represents."**

### 6.1 The Rhythm Formula of Suspense (Douglas's Six-Beat Demonstration from *NYPD Blue*)

- **Douglas's rhythm formula** (Chinese trans. p0139; English edition: Anticipation — Expectation — Surprise): **Anticipation → Expectation → Surprise**—"the audience is first led to anticipate one thing... but the story suddenly turns, producing a feeling of astonishment. **Then a new suspense sequence begins.**" (She demonstrates it beat by beat in the six-beat cold open of *NYPD Blue*'s "Hearts and Souls"; her method is to write a mini-outline first for an important opening.)

## VII. Defining A/B/C, Weaving Examples One by One, and Douglas's Grid

### 7.1 Four Definitions of A/B/C/runner

- **Douglas (Chinese trans. pp. 0089–0090)**: the three strands "are not subsidiary plots but three independent stories," each with different supporting characters, the same locale, and the same protagonist. **A = the most important or most resonant story (not necessarily the one with the most pages); B comes next; C is sometimes comic relief in a serious drama, or a "runner."** Extremes: *ER* often has more than three; *Monk* and *The X-Files* have **only an A story**.
- **Calvisi**: **the letters are ordered by amount of screen time**, A the most and D the least; "**These letter labels should never appear in your pilot script; they are for your use, only.**" Each strand is a **story engine** and must contain **a character, a goal, and the conflict standing in the way**; ideally each strand has at least three beats. *True Detective* has only A and B; *Scandal* and *GoT* have four each.
- **Landau**: **A story = franchise** (the show's signature and sweet spot), with the most scenes, and the protagonist's active goal is almost always on the A story; **B story** is a private story, an internalized emotional stake, and "serves to make the main character relatable and vulnerable"; **runners = C/D** (semi-trivial conflicts, a supporting character's private story, or a running gag).
- **Oberg**: every episode has at least A+B, at most 4–5 strands; **only A and B need to be designed in three acts; the others can be runners of 3–5 beats**; a season-level B/C strand can become the A story of a given episode (*GoT* focuses on the Starks in one episode and the Targaryens in the next).

### 7.2 Weaving Hard Rules, Example by Example (*Terriers* / *Game of Thrones* / *Mad Men*)

8. **Strand crossings work best when there is a thematic link** (Landau 1e); family is the strongest link (*Sopranos*' mob family vs. real family). **A runner can grow into a main strand** (*Terriers*' three-step promotion: the thing lies inert → someone breaks in at night → his schizophrenic sister is living in the attic → she helps him solve the big mystery); **a serial need not service every character every episode**—"Game of Thrones rotated its emphasis from episode to episode."
9. **An example of how to write a runner**: in *Mad Men* S6E11, Peggy's rat—caught in a trap, bloodstains, begging Stan for help in the middle of the night and being refused; by the end the problem is solved, **but we never see her buy the cat; at the end the cat is simply sitting on the sofa beside a much calmer Peggy**. Landau calls this **elliptical storytelling** ("telling the story 'in the cut'").

### 7.3 The A/B Double Track of Procedurals (Ann Donahue, *CSI: Miami*, Douglas Chapter 3)

**A story = the investigation, B story = the emotional line**; **you must cut back and forth between A and B**—"If Act One is nothing but a clue takes you to this character… you're failing."; **"you have to have a warm body by the end of Act One, within the first 17 pages"**; **every person investigated need not be guilty, but must give up something else so the story builds, builds, builds**; "no one cares about plot… But story—story is not the same thing." The same book gives three writers'-room orders: **The Good Wife decides where the character arc lies first and then attaches the case; CSI starts from the case; House plans A (the medical puzzle) / B (House's own struggle) / C (the relationship issue among the doctors, which must connect to the case).**

### 7.4 The Extreme Case of Multi-Strand Ensemble Drama (*Downton*, Fellowes)

- **The extreme case of *Downton* (Fellowes)**: S2 runs 8–12 strands at once, and the ensemble specification is "**one scene advances six or seven strands, each often given only a single line**"; each episode has at least one strand "completely unrelated to the episode's main crisis" to give the audience breathing room (the Ethel strand against the war in Ep3–5, the Bryant strand against the flu in Ep8). **Do not open a separate scene for a strand that can fit into an ensemble scene.**

### 7.5 The Basic Four-Act Grid and the Three Steps of Card Breakdown

**The basic four-act grid**: horizontal axis = T (teaser) | Act One | Act Two | Act Three | Act Four; vertical axis = scene slots 1–7 in each act; each cell holds one letter. **The three steps of card breakdown**: ① watch the episode and name each strand; ② **attach every story to a protagonist and summarize its arc in one sentence** (= each strand's log line); ③ fill in the letters cell by cell—"when the grid is full, it looks like a chessboard." Attitude and diagnosis: "there are **no rules** for how to make this 'chessboard' look good"; a strand can end in Act Three, the C story can only begin in Act Two, Act Four can lay out A's ending—"**all of this is OK. And structure serves story. Borrow from other TV shows, but do not imitate them.**"

### 7.6 Reverse Engineering

**Reverse engineering**: first fill three key points into the grid—**the opening, the "worst-case" suspense at the three-quarter point (at the end of Act Three in a three-act show, in Act Four or Five in a show with more acts), and the ending**; you can do only the A strand or A+B, "leaving room for the C story you will conceive later." Its function is **to prevent you from writing beats that run counter to the suspense and double back on earlier beats**.

### 7.7 The Requirements for a Log Line

**The requirements for a log line** ("Hearts and Souls," Chinese trans. p0138; all three strands take the protagonist rather than a guest character as their subject, e.g. the B strand: "When Sipowicz is challenged by Katie's alcoholism and despair, he must hold back his own anger, guilt, and history of alcoholism in order to help her"): **"Create a truly dramatic log line... and you can be sure that the story you write is full of conflict—that these are real stories and not merely situations."**

### 7.8 Oberg's Three-Step Alternative Method

**Oberg's three-step alternative**: **Mapping the Strands** (proportion) → **Sequencing the Strands** (find each strand's main dramatic action → inciting incident and climax → the strand's three acts → divide Act 2 into dramatic sequences by **subgoal** → divide each sequence into scenes by sub-subgoal) → **Weaving the Strands** (marks: X = inciting incident, BA2/BA3 = the starting point of each strand's second/third act, CH = cliffhanger, MAC = mid-act climax). Two laws: **start the A strand first so the audience knows the main stakes**; **the more storylines, the fewer sequences each can have**—**a one-hour episode holds about 5 dramatic sequences** (a plot-led procedural main line can have 4–6; a theme-led serial with five equally weighted strands gives each strand only one per episode).

## VIII. Scene-Level Specifications and Tests: Escalation Counts, Five Additional Series-Scene Specifications, and the Structural Job of Stage Directions

- **Quantity**: at least three escalations, "but it's better to have more. More escalations give the scene time to build and breathe." **Prefer action**—"If all of your escalations are oral… **Your TV show turns into a radio show.**" Tested: the FBI men's-room scene in the *Hannibal* pilot (1 minute 57 seconds) has 14–15 escalations; the woods scene in the *How to Get Away with Murder* teaser (1 minute 40 seconds) has 11.

**Five additional specifications for series scenes**: ① **length target 2 pages**, "a one-minute beat is more welcome than a five-minute scene" (Douglas). ② **No need to describe appearance** (Douglas, Chinese trans. p0117): "**What you most need to write is the character's emotional activity in this subtle moment**"; **dialogue often starts on page 1**. ③ **"ANGLE" is a literary device, not an instruction to the director**: when it is the same time and place and there is no new slug line, use it to mark that this is a new dramatic beat. ④ **Multi-layer staging within a scene (layering)**—Douglas uses four layers of simultaneity in "Hearts and Souls" to "create a sense of depth on screen"; but for beginners the order is reversed: separate the strands first, and weave them only when writing the teleplay. ⑤ **Do not micromanage the actors** (Miller): the real cost is "**You're actually training readers to skip your description.**"

**Stage directions carry structural work** (*Succession* script book): **write subtext in the stage directions, not in the dialogue** ("Kendall sort of wants his dad to tell him what to do, but doesn't want to ask for direction."); **let a character lie successfully and put the stakes in the stage direction** ("If she looks away, he'll know. But she doesn't.").

## IX. Season Shape: Show-by-Show Templates and Tested Data

### 9.1 Tentpole (Landau 1e, Tip 13)

**7.2 Tentpole.** Landau 1e Tip 13 groups teaser / act break / tag under the name **tentpoles** (帐篷柱, zhàngpeng zhù — "tent poles"). Functional chain: **the teaser sets the hook → Act One establishes the problem the main characters will face → the middle acts complicate the problem, raise the stakes, and make the solution seem impossible → the final act climaxes and resolves → the tag is an epilogue, "puts a button on the episode," and can be the payoff of a runner**, or a cliffhanger serving the franchise or the B story.

### 9.2 The Logistical Layer and the Dramatic Layer (*Stranger Things* S1 and the Episode-Count Check)

**7.1 The logistical layer and the dramatic layer.** Oberg (*Stranger Things* S1): the Duffer brothers' pitch bible uses a three-act logistical structure of **3-3-2** (E1–3 / E4–6 / E7–8); the dramatic three acts are Act 1 of about 10 minutes, **Act 2 of 371 minutes total** (from minute 11 of E1, when Joyce starts looking for her son, to minute 38 of E8, when Will is rescued), and Act 3 of only 13 minutes; his conclusion is restrained: "Both conscious and unconscious approaches can work in harmony." **Season-level pacing discipline**: in a serial, **each strand usually gets at most one dramatic sequence per episode** (the Joyce strand has 10 across eight episodes), and each sequence closes on success/failure. **The episode-count check**: "**Are you starting the story too early?**"—if it only gets good at Episode 3, compress the first three episodes into one; **cutting one episode (10→8, 8→6) often speeds things up**. Landau 2e makes the same point: demote the pilot to backstory, "**begin your series pilot in medias res**."

### 9.3 Bottle Episode: *The West Wing* "17 People" Tested

**7.3 Bottle episode: the cause is budget.** In the preface Sorkin wrote for *The West Wing*'s "17 People," he states that Warner demanded the next episode have "no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras and no film," so "**I got to write a play**." Measured: 58 PDF pages, act lengths **6 / 14 / 10 / 10 / 18** (the reverse of the previous four episodes, where Act Four is the longest); the whole episode is one night (the on-screen caption at the start of ACT ONE reads `11:35 P.M.`); **one night + one building = bottle**; the stage direction repeatedly says "The place is empty except for the SECURITY GUARD" to legitimize having zero extras. Three rules: ① **a bottle episode allows act outs that are not turns but "increases in depth"** (this episode's three act outs are three deepening passes at the same thing: the diagnosis → the medical details → the out-of-control accusation); ② **when there is no exterior to shoot, the number of strands becomes the source of pace** (four strands normally, five in the bottle episode, seven in the season finale—add strands rather than lengthen scenes); ③ **turn the four restrictions into three choices**: one night, one building, a main-strand node where "it's time to tell the truth," and **use the occasion to advance the secret the season arc has never dared to say** (Bartlet tells Toby about the MS).

### 9.4 A Bottle Episode Is Not a Single Strand: *The Sopranos* "Pine Barrens"

**A bottle episode is not a single strand.** *The Sopranos*' "Pine Barrens" (S3E11, 64 pages, 71 scenes) traps two men in the woods for a night, **while the B/C/D strands proceed as usual** (Gloria, Meadow–Jackie Jr., couples therapy), and uses parallel editing for counterpoint (starving in the woods ↔ three meals at Tony's house). Another device in the same episode: **a chain of information decay**—three levels of retelling turn "sixteen Chechens / Interior Ministry" into "sixteen Czechs / interior decorators"; **the phones don't work, so everyone has only half the intelligence**.

### 9.5 Container Episodes and Ritual Episodes: Two Templates from *Succession*

**7.4 Container episodes and ritual episodes (two templates from *Succession*).** **The pressure-cooker episode** (lock everyone in one building: the hospital, the Thanksgiving apartment, the warehouse party, the castle in S1) **always comes with a "limited communications" setup** (corridors, phone-confiscation bags, a phone-check desk plus signal blocking). The rule: **restrict the flow of information first, then make information travel by body**—"retrieve the phone and make a call" becomes a playable action. **The ritual episode** (a day with a schedule: the charity gala with on-screen timestamps from 4 p.m. to 9:30; the Thanksgiving arrival order = the order of introductions; the wedding's rehearsal / toasts / first dance / fireworks)—**ritual provides a free beat sheet; the writer only has to insert one conflict into each established segment**; the notes on S3E9 explain why it works: a wedding forces everyone into the same room, forces etiquette, forces periods of silence, and conspiracy can only advance through corridor asides, the bathroom, and the parking lot—**the stronger the order of the ritual, the stronger the sense of violation in the shadow plot**. **The absent protagonist**: in S1 E6, during the vote of no confidence, Kendall is stuck in traffic and speaks via Polycom; in S3E9, Logan is in nine scenes and almost never shares one with his children—**absence is the strongest means of sustaining suspense**; move the initiator of the climax offstage, and suspense shifts from "how will it turn out" to "can he get there / will he be found out."

### 9.6 Two-Parter: *The West Wing* "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" I/II Tested

**7.5 Two-parter.** *The West Wing* "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" I/II measured: the first half has a 7-page teaser + 21/14/11/14 = 67 pages; the second half has **a teaser of only 2 pages** + 11/14/16/18 = 61 pages—**the second half of a season-opening two-parter needs only a single capture scene in the teaser**. Division-of-labor rule: **the first half alternates between two timelines to create "two worlds"; the second half puts every act out in the present tense; one prop stitches the two episodes together** (an ID badge, a necklace).

### 9.7 Season Finales and Series Finales: *Downton* / *Succession* / *Sopranos* / Sorkin

- ***Downton* (Fellowes)**: "You've always got to give the audience a reason for coming back next time."; the final act is pure pay-off + **"streamers"**—"we won't resolve this tonight, but if you keep watching, at some point we will."; **the final act does only pay-off and introduces no new information**; before the penultimate act ends, let the audience know clearly which accounts are still unsettled.
- ***Succession* S1**: **the season finale turns around symmetrically with the pilot**—in E1 Kendall buys Vaulter at $140 a share but loses the throne; in E10 he tries to buy the company at the same $140 a share and loses everything, "using the same number to complete the shape of a season"; **the finale both closes doors and opens them**—all ten strands land their pieces, but every landing creates a debt for the next season (Greg's photocopies, Tom's cruise, Roman's manslaughter, Shiv's double loyalty). **S4 does the reverse**: each episode has one core question, the answer is given by the end of that episode, and suspense is carried instead by "the new configuration of power."
- ***The Sopranos*' anti-climactic endings**: use a public announcement / a single line / a single action, not a cliffhanger ("You got mayonnaise on your chin."); **a season-arc antagonist can be accidentally resolved by a third party** (Richie is killed by Janice at the dinner table), but the setup must be planted in relationship detail before that episode (the gun-to-the-head fetish).
- **Sorkin's season-finale method ("Two Cathedrals")**: his preface is almost just **a list of images** ("The cigarette. The storm. The motorcade. The press conference. Mark Knoffler.")—**first fix seven images for the finale, then let the structure serve them**, and run one object through three acts (the cigarette: appears in Act One → is stubbed out in Act Three → is picked up by the janitor in Act Four). Measured act lengths **3 / 13 / 13 / 12 / 17**, **the teaser is only 3 pages and marked `CONTINUOUS`**: the season finale's teaser does not re-establish the world; it has only one job—set the deadline for that night.

### 9.8 The Six-Episode Season Shape (the *Fleabag* Template)

**7.7 The six-episode season shape (the *Fleabag* template).** 1 lays out all the characters + the season's suspense at once (**not a premise pilot**; S2 does it with one engagement dinner in a single location) → 2 swaps Episode 1's emotion for a desire the audience can follow → 3 **plays the first "card"** → 4 **bottle episode**: a two-hander deep dive away from the usual locations (the silent retreat in S1; the confessional in S2) → 5 **the false-victory peak** ("the best day") to store energy for the collapse → 6 **the finale = a closed social occasion** (Sexhibition / the wedding), everyone recalled to one space, every strand closed there. The single-episode shape is fixed: **cold open (1–3 minutes) → TITLES → 3–7 segments → button (≤1 page, often wordless)**; **the button of a finale is never plot**—the last image of a half-hour show resolves the theme, not the plot. Two transferable devices: **one object runs through the whole season, changing hands once per episode, and when it is returned in the finale it reveals the truth** (the statue: stolen → sold → given as a birthday present → confessed → returned → used as a trophy → a wedding gift → "She was based on your mother." → she takes it away again); **put the bottle episode in Episode 4 and use a prohibition as its engine**.

### 9.9 The Multi-Strand Season Shape (*Downton* S2)

**7.8 The multi-strand season shape (*Downton* S2).** The act count varies from episode to episode: Ep1 six acts, Ep2–5 four acts, Ep6 five acts, Ep7 five acts, Ep8 six acts, the Christmas special seven acts. Strand lengths are layered: **a cross-season main line + a single-season arc + guest strands lasting one to three episodes**. Two rules: **the retention mechanism**—popular regular characters do not leave; "bankruptcy" (Thomas), "changing his mind" (Carson), and "a reduced sentence" (Bates) pull back characters already pushed to the door, and **the ones who can actually be killed are outsiders** (Lang, William, Lavinia, Vera, Bryant); **before killing a character, first let the audience suspect someone else**—"It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time."

## X. Alternative-Structure Checklist and Multiple Timelines

**7.9 Alternative structures (Landau 2e).** **Linear** and **Flashback** (when the "story present" appears only at the beginning and end, it is called **bookend structure**, as in *Chernobyl*, which opens with Legasov's tapes and his suicide); also zigzag, hopscotch, three timelines (the *OITNB* pilot), **Rashomon structure** (*The Affair*), **Time Loop** (the earliest is *The Twilight Zone* Episode 62, "Shadow Play"; *Russian Doll*), **telling it backward** (*The Assassination of Gianni Versace* goes from the murder back to earlier victims—"**The trick here is that the writer didn't tell the origin story until the series finale.**"). **Five questions for choosing a structure** (premise: "Time is the container for your series"; if unsure, use the classic three acts): ① How much time does the story cover? ② Is there far-past information the audience must know for the story to work? ③ Can reordering increase tension by changing the audience's expectations? ④ Does the structure best fit the story's **underlying experience** (the narrative gaps in *I May Destroy You* correspond to the experience of "drugged, blacked out, large chunks of memory missing")? ⑤ Are you truly eager to try a new approach? **Warning**: "if you deviate too much from the linear basics, you might lose your audience."

**Multiple timelines as a tonal regulator (Alex Pina, *Money Heist*)**: when the adrenaline peaks and the audience's anxiety becomes unbearable, cut to flashback, "above all, we go to another 'flavor' or genre"; the reason—"**If you do not work in different time planes, the emotional continuity makes it impossible for you to change the anguished or sad or dramatic tone of some of the actions.**" **The opposing view (Mazzara)**: "I don't believe that flashbacks are an element of horror films. They're an element of science fiction films… I don't find flashbacks scary."

| grid | 格子 (gézi — grid) | **格栅** ("grid") (Chinese trans. p0035, body) / **框格** ("frame grid") (Chapter 3 title, "The Basic Grid of the Four-Act Play") | A table with the horizontal axis = T / Acts One–Four and the vertical axis = scene slots 1–7 in each act, each cell holding the letter of one strand; "when the grid is full, it looks like a chessboard" (Douglas, Chapter 3). **The same concept has two Chinese renderings in the translation.** |
| dramatic sequence | sequence / dramatic sequence | not recorded | A string of dramatic scenes whose goals all connect to **the same subgoal**; no fixed number and no fixed length, "as short as a few minutes, as long as sixty minutes or more"; a one-hour episode holds about 5 (Oberg glossary). |
| dramatic scene | dramatic scene | (SCENE is rendered "**场**", Chinese trans. p0284; the dramatic/logistical distinction is not recorded) | A scene with clear stakes (who wants what, why, and what stands in the way); **a change in protagonist or goal → a new dramatic scene** (Oberg glossary). |
| logistical scene | logistical scene | not recorded | A scene cut by physical time and space; **a change of location or time → a new logistical scene**; one dramatic scene can consist of several logistical scenes (kitchen→bathroom→bedroom = 3 logistical, 1 dramatic) (Oberg glossary). |
| dramatic question | dramatic question | not recorded | Act 2's main hook: "Will the protagonist reach their goal?" (in a character-led story, ask about need instead); a season, episode, act, strand, sequence, or scene with clear stakes has its own dramatic question (Oberg glossary). |
| midpoint | 中点 / midpoint (zhōngdiǎn — midpoint) | not recorded | Calvisi: the Act Two act out, which must reverse the A story and carry a ticking clock; **Oberg objects**—"the midpoint can be safely ignored if you find it confusing," and offers **mid-act climax** instead (Oberg glossary). This pack keeps both side by side (see the disagreement table in Section 8). |
| worst point | 最坏情况 (zuìhuài qíngkuàng — worst case) | not recorded | A plot point in which the protagonist is trapped in an apparent dead end, equivalent to *Save the Cat*'s "All is lost"; Oberg notes "not at all necessary in modern series writing" (Oberg glossary); Douglas puts it at the three-quarter point of the whole story, Calvisi at about the 4/5 point. |

### 12.3 The Four Information-Management Tools (All from the Oberg Glossary; Not One Appears in the Chinese Translation's Glossary)

| English term | Usage in this pack | Official Chinese rendering (Yan Min translation) | One-line definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| dramatic irony | dramatic irony | not recorded | The audience knows something that at least one character present does not; three steps **set-up → exploitation → resolution**; the deceived character is the **victim**, "one of the most powerful tools in the writer's toolbox." |
| mystery | mystery | not recorded | Give the audience just enough information to "know that they don't know"; the worst kind is **the protagonist knowing more than the audience for a long stretch** (especially backstory needed to understand their actions). |
| suspense | suspense | not recorded | The audience is aware of danger; **does not necessarily require dramatic irony**—"As long as the audience knows where the threat comes from, there is suspense, even if all the characters are aware of the danger." |
| surprise (twist) | surprise | not recorded | Information suddenly given to the audience that it did not expect; in comedy "almost every gag is a surprise" (Landau). |
| ironic question | ironic question | not recorded | "How and when will the victim find out, and how will they react?"—when a strong irony runs through the story, it can matter more than the dramatic question. |

| English term | Usage in this pack | Official Chinese rendering (Yan Min translation) | One-line definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| bottle episode | 瓶子集 (píngzi jí — bottle episode) | not recorded | An episode confined to existing sets because of budget limits; the origin is in the preface Sorkin wrote for *The West Wing*'s "17 People" (Warner demanded "no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras and no film"), measured at 58 pages, act lengths 6/14/10/10/18, the whole episode in one night. |

| two-parter | 双集 / two-parter (shuāngjí — two-parter) | not recorded | A unit that tells one story across two episodes; tested (*The West Wing* "In the Shadow of Two Gunmen" I/II): the first half has a 7-page teaser + 21/14/11/14 = 67 pages; the second half has **a teaser of only 2 pages** + 11/14/16/18 = 61 pages. |
| movement | movement (乐章, yuèzhāng — movement) | not recorded | Splitting a season into segments of **4–6 episodes**, each driven by one answerable question; once answered, a new question starts the next segment (Katims interview, in the Tip 13 chapter of Landau 1e, p0193–0210; the question of the first movement of *FNL* S1 is "Will the town of Dillon survive the loss of Jason Street?", answered at the end of Episode 5's rivalry game). |

### 12.6 Three Translation Traps That Must Be Flagged

1. **log line = "计程绳" (jìchéng shéng — literally a ship's log line) (Chinese trans. p0282).** The translator literalized log line (the nautical instrument for measuring a ship's speed and distance); the current standard rendering is "一句话故事／故事梗概句" ("one-sentence story / story-summary sentence"). The Chinese translation's definition is itself correct: "a single sentence that sums up a story, occasionally two or three; it can quickly convey the series' premise and dramatic arc or plot"; Douglas EN adds the etymology—**the FCC required networks to keep a one-line log for each program**. This pack's body always writes **log line**; when reading Chapter 4 and the "Hearts and Souls" demonstration in the Chinese translation (p0138), restore "计程绳" to log line.
2. **staff writer = "试用编剧" (shìyòng biānjù — "trial writer")** (Chinese trans. p0285, also "宝贝编剧" (bǎobèi biānjù — "baby writer")). The same page translates freelance writer as "自由编剧" (zìyóu biānjù — "freelance writer"); today the standard rendering of staff writer is "编剧组成员／初级编剧" ("writer's-room member / junior writer"). "Trial" can easily be misread as unpaid or informal—it is the **first rung on the staff** (for the rank chain see `sw-writers-room`).
3. **"晚进早出" (wǎnjìn zǎochū — "late in, early out") is a same-name term with the opposite meaning and cannot be directly translated as enter late, leave early.** In Appendix 2 of 姚扣根 (Yao Kougen)'s 《电视剧写作概论》 (*An Introduction to TV Drama Writing*) (**p0367–0368**), "晚进早出" means: **enter late = first lay the groundwork and build atmosphere, let the audience become familiar with the new scene and accumulate expectation, then light the fuse**; leave early = once the turn is complete and a new concrete goal and new suspense have arisen, cut immediately and move on ("this in-and-out keeps the scene tense at all times"). The American **enter late, leave early** of the McKee tradition is the exact opposite: **skip the setup and go straight into conflict**. The two books agree on "early out" but disagree on "late in"; when writing Chinese structural documents you must state which system you are using, or scene lengths will differ by a factor of two. (Yao Kougen's "exit criterion"—leave as soon as a new suspense arises—can be stacked with Oberg's cliffhanger "before we leave a strand.")
