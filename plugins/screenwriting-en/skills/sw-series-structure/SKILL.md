## I. The Fundamental Difference Between Series Structure and Film Structure

- **A tighter form, so it is harder, not easier.** Douglas, Chinese translation, chapter 3 (p0088): “A TV script requires all the same elements compressed into a tighter form.” A film scene can run 5–7 pages; in TV **the target for a scene is 2 pages** — “if you start out writing a meeting scene at 4 pages, that is a red flag.”
- **The script simulates the edited finished film, not raw footage**, and must give the reader the experience of “watching the show” (Calvisi).
- **The writer labels the acts by hand; there is nowhere to hide** (Calvisi lists this as the first format difference of TV scripts): “There is no place to hide.” An unpaginated pilot looks like cheating and, like shrinking the margins, immediately exposes the amateur.
- **Scene numbers are not scenes in the dramatic sense.** Douglas: an exterior of a building “is not a scene in the full dramatic sense”; **one dramatic beat can contain multiple slug lines; it is synchronized with the development of the story, not with locations**. Oberg's counting rule: **the protagonist or objective changes → a new dramatic scene; the place or time changes → a new logistical scene**; structural documents count dramatic scenes.
- **Finished length and script length are two different numbers** (Calvisi): a broadcast network's main program, minus commercials, is only 45–48 minutes, but a pilot script must be at least 50 pages — better long than short, “but try not to exceed 60 pages.”
- **The “act” in a series is not a dramatic principle; it is an advertising slot.** In chapter 1, Oberg calls any division that prescribes a fixed number of parts and pages a **story format (logistical)**, whose origins lie in theater's candle changes, cinema's film-reel changes, and television's commercial breaks — “But all this is gone.” The “string of sausages” metaphor: the logistical method only determines “how many sausages and how long.” **The most important judgment in this skill: the number of acts is a delivery format; three acts (before / during / after a main dramatic action or evolution) are a design tool.**
- **But structure is also the production schedule.** Miller, chapter 9: the first thing the production department does with a script is count days in and out; **write only DAY or NIGHT** — if you write dawn, production will schedule the shoot precisely at sunrise that day.
- **Structure is inherited.** A series carries over the pilot's act structure and page ranges (see section 1 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the act-page comparison between the Breaking Bad pilot and the same show's #308) — **writing the pilot means setting the structural mold for the whole series.**

## II. The Episode Container: Teaser, Act Count, and Page Anchors

### 2.1 The Functional Chain of the Broadcast Four-Act (Goldberg & Rabkin, 2003)

**Act One** introduces the characters, conflict, and stakes → **the end of Act Two must push the story in a “startlingly new and unexpected direction”** (Law & Order: the police arrest an unexpected person) → **at the end of Act Three the protagonist thinks they are in control but discovers they are wrong**, “They are all going to die. There is no hope.” → **Act Four** finds a way, achieves the goal.

> **Expiration flag (2003)**: the book claims that “all one-hour shows are four acts,” **which is out of date** — from 2006 broadcast networks generally moved to five acts, and new ABC shows to six (Douglas, Chinese translation, p0091), while streaming removed act markings. But **the four-act functional chain is still valid**: both Miller and Landau (2021) still require breaking in four acts first, then subdividing.

See section 2 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the four anchor page numbers (Douglas, Chinese translation, p0090–0091, a four-act first draft with a teaser) and the reason for “a commercial every 13 to 15 minutes.”

> ⚠️ **These page numbers are valid only within a 48–63-page American one-hour episode and cannot be scaled proportionally to a Chinese domestic 45-minute episode.** The two systems divide acts on different bases: the American system divides by **pages and commercial slots**, while the Chinese system divides by **timeslots and segments** (about 41.5 minutes of main program: a 3-minute opening + a small 扣子 (kòuzi — a suspense hook at the end of a segment) every 15 minutes + a big end-of-episode 关子 (guānzi — a suspense device at the end of a segment)). When writing Chinese domestic drama, take only the medium-agnostic parts of this section (act-out typology, “the end of Act One pushes the protagonist into a corner with no way back,” the Act Two break changing the nature of the story, the five-part scene structure, the stakes test, the four information-management tools), and switch to `sw-chinese-series-practice` for timeslots and scene counts.
> **The same does not apply to half-hour shows** (a 30-minute script is about 30 pages, and both act count and scene count are a different system): see section 4 of `sw-sitcom-comedy` for act lengths, page numbers, and scene counts, and **do not scale this section's one-hour numbers down to them**. Diagnostics 4 and 5 below apply only to American one-hour episodes.

### 2.3 Converting Four Acts → Five / Six Acts

See section 2 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the three conversion methods of Douglas, Peter Blake (House), and Miller, and for the six-act tail design in which “Act Six is actually the next episode's teaser.”

### 2.4 Teaser / Cold Open

- **Length**: see section 3 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the teaser lengths given by Douglas, Landau, and Oberg.
- **Ten types** (Douglas's seven paradigms + Landau's three types): see section 3 of [reference.md](reference.md) for names and examples.
- **A hard rule for beginners** (Douglas, Chinese translation, p0098): “Insist on opening with a story rather than information… many students… mistakenly believe that opening with a grand theme or abstract philosophy will make a strong impression. **They are actually shrinking from the challenge of advancing a dramatic arc. The best teasers tend toward the best dramatization.**”
- **The taboo of the teaser flashback** (Oberg): you may start in the middle or even at the end of the dramatic action (The Walking Dead, Breaking Bad), **but the teaser must never answer the dramatic question**; if you draw from the ending, choose **before or during** the climax (Run All Night), not after it (John Wick).
- **A teaser is not mandatory**: see section 3 of [reference.md](reference.md) for Landau 2e's and Oberg's arguments and the list of shows with no teaser or with “a short first act serving as teaser.”
- **Chinese domestic 45-minute episodes**: the teaser and the “3-minute opening hook” (`sw-chinese-series-practice` 5.1) occupy the same position; **choose one**, and by default keep the hook.
- **cold open ≠ teaser** (Miller): the cold open of the Brooklyn Nine-Nine pilot does not affect the main story and only launches the episode's theme, so that episode's point of attack falls in Act One.

### 2.5 Invisible Acts: Three Tests

Premium cable / streaming scripts often mark neither acts nor pagination (Calvisi names **True Detective, House of Cards, and Mad Men as all unmarked, yet Mad Men places a FADE OUT at the conventional act break**), yet “a pilot script is almost always going to be structured in the conventional 4-6 act template.” Three tests measured act by act from Succession scripts:

Three tests: ① **story-day / time-block markers** — **a new day is a new act**; ② **changes of location and mode of transport**; ③ **the moment the core question turns**. The criterion: **every act must end with a transfer of information or power**; a suspense hook is not required. See section 4 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the Succession measurements test by test (S3E9, S4E01, S4E05, S4E08, S4E10).

An accompanying pattern (Succession S4, ten episodes): **8/10 episodes compress the whole episode into a container with a fixed procedure**, and **procedural milestones replace act breaks** — **that is, streaming writers are still writing acts, only disguising them as a schedule**. See section 4 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the container and scene count of each episode.

### 2.6 Should ACT Be Written on the Page? A Disagreement That Must Be Kept Side by Side

## III. Act-Out and Cliffhanger Typologies, and the Four Information-Management Tools

### 3.1 Definitions, Counterexamples, and Criteria

- **Calvisi**: every act (ideally every scene) should make people want to know what happens next; the end of an act is often called the **“Act-Out.”**
- **Landau 2e, chapter 12**: “A cliffhanger is a plot device that leaves one or more characters facing either a dangerous situation or a shocking revelation.”
- **Oberg**: a cliffhanger is “simply an unresolved conflict” — **its position is not only the end of an episode or season but also “before leaving a strand.”**
- **Counterexample: schmuck bait** (Landau 2e): the heart monitor flatlines, a nurse shouts “Code blue!” (commercial break) → “I'm getting a pulse.”
- **Today's rule (Landau 2e's most central one)**: “**forcing a cliffhanger at the end of each act break is deadly**… Instead, act breaks can land on a **turning point that emerges from characters, not merely from plot**.”
- **Criterion (repeated in 1e Tip 19 and 2e chapter 12)**: a cliffhanger must **“grow out of character jeopardy, risk, or fear”**; a bad cliffhanger is just a plot point that exists for shock value; “even a ‘surprise’ cliffhanger needs to be (subtly) set up so that the audience feels they **should have seen it coming—but didn't**.”
- **Placement and timing**: one-hour shows save the climactic cliffhanger for the end of the episode; multi-camera sitcoms usually place it at the midpoint (the ending must press the reset button and return to normal); you may postpone revealing the whole truth, **but be sure to reveal it before the season ends** (see section 5 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the original wording).

### 3.2 Ten Kinds of Cliffhanger (Landau)

See section 5 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the definitions and examples of the seven types in 2e (character in peril | ticking bomb | love is in the balance | surprise twist / aha moment | death of a character | discovery of a secret | life events) and the three additional types in 1e (lack of closure | mini-cliffhangers | comedy cliffhangers), plus the two most transferable practices (“turn the reversal with a single word” and “do not go out on the joke”).

### 3.3 Eight Forms of Act Out and Three General Laws

Eight forms measured across six West Wing episodes: **a suspenseful question / a reversal line / an emotional revelation / dialogue-free action / a freeze-frame on an object / a repeated line whose meaning reverses / a setup that is cut off / a gesture that pays off a plant**. Accompanying rules: **hand each act out to a different story line**; **use dialogue-free action or an object for at least one act out; do not use dialogue for all four acts** — a dialogue-free act out must first teach its grammar. See section 5 of [reference.md](reference.md) for examples of the eight forms, the teaching method in “Two Cathedrals,” and Downton's four forms.

**An act out can be entirely an information event.** Goldberg & Rabkin's appendix on two episodes (Martial Law and seaQuest 2032's “Depths of Deceit”) yields three general laws: **① the end of an act is not the greatest crisis but “the protagonist pushed into a situation with no way back”; ② the Act Two break must always change the nature of the story, not merely raise the danger; ③ the end of Act Three must turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat.** See section 5 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the act-by-act comparison of the two episodes.

### 3.4 The Components of Suspense and the Stakes Test

- **Douglas's rhythm formula** (Chinese translation p0139; English edition: Anticipation — Expectation — Surprise): **anticipation → expectation → surprise**, “then begin a new suspense segment”; see section 6 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the beat-by-beat demonstration of the six-beat cold open of NYPD Blue's “Hearts and Souls.”
- **Landau 2e**: Suspense = **Anticipation + Surprise**, ideally unexpected, inevitable but not predictable.
- **The stakes test (the most practical one)**: ask yourself whether “doing nothing in the face of the crisis” is a viable option for this character — “If your character is not compelled to solve a problem by the potential of losing something of value, then your stakes are insufficient.”
- **The polarization model**: find the positive and negative (+/−) charge in every episode, every scene, and every act; the difference from the old approach is that **the direction of an act break can be positive or negative** — “some acts will end on a win.”

### 3.5 The Four Information-Management Tools (Oberg, chapters 1–2)

Three-dimensional model: a character is a point; add the main dramatic action (conscious want) → a line; add the main dramatic evolution (unconscious need) → a plane; **add managing information → three dimensions**. Managing conflict answers “Who wants or needs what and why?”; managing information answers “**Who knows what and when?**”

See section 6 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the definitions and usage table of the four tools (**dramatic irony** / **surprise** / **mystery** / **suspense**).

See section 6 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the point-by-point expansion of the four red lines (① do not let the protagonist know more than the audience for long; ② for cliffhangers, prefer the “information bomb”; ③ closed mystery vs. open mystery; ④ “you can have some mystery over the nature of the antagonist, but not over the reality of the danger it represents.”).

Landau 2e's stitching sentence: “it's not only Who Does What When, but also **Who Knows What When**.”

### 3.6 Speed-Up: Act Out Is Not a Fake Reversal Every Seven or Eight Minutes

See section 5 of [reference.md](reference.md) for Rabkin's comparison of 24 and Scandal, his original wording about “moving the coolest thing at the end of Act Four to the end of the teaser,” the L+3 ratings logic, and “streaming can leave out three things.”

## IV. Weaving and Ratio of A/B/C/Runner

### 4.1 Definitions and Ordering

See section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the four definitions and orderings (Douglas's “three independent stories” and runner / Calvisi's “letters ordered by amount of story” and the three elements of the story engine / Landau's A = franchise, B = personal story, runners = C/D / Oberg's “every episode has at least A+B, at most 4–5 strands, and only A and B need to be designed as three acts”).

### 4.2 Hard Rules of Weaving

1. **The franchise (A story) runs through every act; the B story and runners are inserted as needed**, and each act generally holds **4–5 beats, ideally ending on the A story and landing on the protagonist's predicament** (Landau).
2. **Very rarely end an act on the C story** — “If the story is worthy of an act break, then it's not your C story – it's probably more your A or B story.” (Landau 1e/2e, identical wording; Oberg says the same.)
3. **But the end-of-act suspense need not be on the A story** (Douglas, Chinese translation p0095: “it may be a turning point in the B story”). Measured in the West Wing pilot: both the teaser and the Act Three act out are carried by the B story, Sam–Laurie.
4. **You do not have to alternate like a checkerboard; follow the “line of interest.”** Douglas uses NYPD Blue's “Simone Says,” scene 8 (an old woman downstairs is killed by a stray bullet), to demonstrate: “It is not necessary in terms of the plot order on the ‘checkerboard,’ but it is crucial for following the audience's line of interest.”
5. **Cutting to another line can create “ellipsis”** — the impression that time has passed, allowing the same characters to reappear in a way they “could not actually have experienced that quickly.”
6. **No scene is written solely for exposition. “If you need to convey a fact, put the fact in an emotionally charged context.”** (Douglas, Chinese translation, p0119)
7. **Keep the lines separate when planning; weave only when writing the teleplay** (Douglas's order for beginners, the reverse of her own order when analyzing the four-layer simultaneity of “Hearts and Souls”).
8. **Lines cross most effectively when there is a thematic link** (Landau 1e); family is the strongest link; **a runner can grow into a main story**; **a serial need not write every character in every episode**; see section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md) for examples of all three (the three-stage promotion in Terriers, the rotation in Game of Thrones, and Peggy's rat and elliptical storytelling in Mad Men S6E11).

### 4.3 The A/B Twin Track of the Procedural (Ann Donahue, CSI: Miami, Douglas chapter 3)

See section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md) for Ann Donahue's twin track of A = investigation / B = emotion, “warm body by the end of Act One, within the first 17 pages,” and the three writers'-room orders of The Good Wife / CSI / House.

### 4.4 Two Alternative Ways of Breaking Story (Must Sit Alongside A/B/C)

- **Wendy West (Dexter)**: the broadcast version has no acts, and the series effectively falls back to three acts; “**We break the beats by character and then do a weave.**” On the whiteboard every character has a beginning, middle, and end; **break by character, not by act**, breaking the protagonist's line first — “The reversals tend to come where you'd expect — page 40 to 45 out of a 55-page script.”
- **Glen Mazzara (The Walking Dead)**: **explicitly rejects A/B/C** — treating Rick as A and another person as B and then interweaving them, “That doesn't always equal a theme. It just pushes the ball further on each one.” Instead, ask **“What is this episode about?”** (the episode where the girl comes out of the barn, the episode where the herd breaks through the corral: every character has a place in that larger story). **He names the cost himself: a minor character may remain a minor character forever.**
- **Oberg's compromise**: multiple lines do not equal theme-led — if the characters in each line **share the same external problem and the same objective**, it is a plot-led co-protagonist structure (in Stranger Things, A1 Joyce / A2 Hopper / A3 the boys / A4 Jonathan are actually the same A story).
- **Downton's extreme (Fellowes)**: see section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the ensemble scale of 8–12 lines running simultaneously in S2 and “at least one line per episode unrelated to the episode's main crisis.”

See section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the basic four-act grid, the three steps of breaking, reverse-engineering, the requirements of a log line, and Oberg's three-step alternative (Mapping → Sequencing → Weaving the Strands).

## VI. The Five-Part Scene Structure and the Scene-Level Checklist

**The five parts** (Miller, chapter 9, adapted from Lance Gentile, writer on ER / Third Watch and an emergency physician): **hero's want / obstacle / escalations / decision / resolution**.

- **want and obstacle are established before the scene begins**; **the first escalation opens the scene**; **decision = the climax of the scene**; **resolution = the result of the decision**, and carries the character into the next scene.
- **Number**: at least three escalations, “but it's better to have more. More escalations give the scene time to build and breathe.” **Prefer action** — “If all of your escalations are oral… **Your TV show turns into a radio show.**” (See section 8 of [reference.md](reference.md) for the measured escalations in two scenes from Hannibal and HTGAWM.)
- **A scene has only one hero, and the hero can change** (the men's room scene is Will's; the next scene, Jack–Alana, is Jack's). **A one-person scene**: the opposing want comes from the world or the environment — in OITNB's “The Chickening,” Piper's want is to enjoy her Sunday-morning ritual, and **the obstacle is the prison**.
- **moment / image ≠ scene**: “If there isn't a strong character want, it's not a scene. **A moment without a character want is simply a moment, an image.**” And a moment cannot repeat a known emotion; it must show an evolving emotion.
- **Responsibility for ending the scene**: “**You the writer need to decide how to end the scene.** And you the writer need to decide what emotions your characters are feeling.”
- **exposition through conflict**: in the HTGAWM woods scene, everyone's objection is reasonable, and the difficulty and stakes are conveyed through the argument.

See section 8 of [reference.md](reference.md) for **the five additional specifications at the series scene level** (the 2-page length target, no need to describe appearance, “ANGLE” as a literary device, layering multiple levels within the same scene, and not micromanaging actors) and **the two ways stage directions carry structural work** (the Succession script collection).

## VIII. Disagreement Table: Which Position to Use When

| Point of dispute | Position A | Position B | Criterion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Are acts structure or format?** | Douglas / Calvisi / Goldberg & Rabkin: four/five/six acts are the structural skeleton, and page anchors can be tested | Oberg: acts are logistical, “just the size of the sausage”; use dramatic three acts for design, then divide for delivery | Delivering to a platform with commercials, or needing to prove structural competence → A; diagnosing “a saggy middle” or “formulaic structure” → B (divide into sequences by subgoal). They can be stacked: A as the working unit, B as the design tool |
| **Do you write ACT on the page?** | Landau: break the story at act breaks, remove the labels in the final draft (story over format) | The opposite view in the same book: many showrunners want to see the labels as evidence of structural command; Miller: if the material can go to a broadcast network, you must write them | Look at the target market (Miller's criterion): for a streaming crew you can remove them; for broadcast/basic cable you must keep them |
| **Does the midpoint mean anything?** | Calvisi: the Midpoint is the Act Two act out and must reverse the A story and carry a ticking clock | Oberg: “the midpoint can be safely ignored if you find it confusing”; the substitute is the **mid-act climax** | If the A-story goal can cover all of Act 2 → midpoint; if the goal changes midstream (heist films, the Occupied pilot) → mid-act climax |
| **Is the midpoint a false victory or must it succeed?** | The Snyder camp / Calvisi: choose either the illusion of victory or the illusion of failure | Miller: it must be “hero succeeds—almost” — “**It's not ‘the hero fails’!**” | When writing tragedy / an antihero episode, use Miller's own variant: **squeeze turns positive, climax turns negative** |
| **Where does the “worst thing” go?** | Douglas: at the three-quarter point; Calvisi's DOW + All Is Lost at the 4/5 point | Oberg: “a series will be successful **despite** these formulaic crutches, not because of them.” | First series → use the anchors as a self-check; already-solid sequence design → no need to place them |
| **A/B/C lines vs. other ways of breaking story** | Douglas / Calvisi / Landau: A/B/C(/D) + runner, letters ordered by amount of story, act breaks land on A or B | Mazzara: rejects A/B/C, asks instead “What is this episode about?”; Wendy West: break by character, not by act, then weave | Ensemble show where each episode should be “one complete story” → Mazzara; single-protagonist serial with reversals concentrated in the protagonist's line → West; procedural and broadcast drama → A/B/C. Mazzara himself names the cost: a minor character may remain a minor character forever |
| **Must every act have a cliffhanger?** | Calvisi: every act (ideally every scene) should make you want to turn the page; Kelley (Revenge, six acts): “five or six cliffhangers an episode” | Landau 2e: “forcing a cliffhanger at the end of each act break is deadly”; Sorkin, in the measured episodes: the Act One and Act Two act outs are both light, and Act Four is not a cliffhanger but a “reset” | Six-act broadcast drama (five commercial slots to fill) → Kelley's quota; no commercials or a strong auteur show → Landau/Sorkin. **The criterion does not change**: it must grow out of character jeopardy/risk/fear |
| **Thematic unity of multiple lines vs. theme as an emergent property** | Landau (Tip 14 / ch11): theme is the glue that holds divergent story lines together (ER's “Stuck on You”: all four lines are “stuck to each other”) | Johannessen (Homeland/Dexter/24): 24's room motto “**We don't do themes.**”; “If you want something that looks and feels real, **throw out the writerly devices**”; the substitute is the **operational question**. Mazzara is on the same side: focus on story first, and only see the theme when you step back | Strong realism / thriller first → B (switch to “this episode's operational question”); ensemble multi-line, needing to stitch several unrelated lines together → A. Note that Johannessen himself admits that Dexter S5's “Atonement” was **emergent**; Landau also lists shows that do not use a unifying theme (Breaking Bad; Parenthood and FNL “arranged asymmetrically”) |
| **Do you need an outline?** | Landau 1e: “**No outline, no paycheck.**”; Calvisi: “the Story Map is the ultimate outline template.”; Miller: grid first, then outline, then write | Mazzara: “I don't use outlines… None of their notes are applicable to script.” — the substitute is a two- or three-page story document + a beat sheet of two or three words per beat (“Rick kills Shane.”); Sorkin: “When I finish one script I have no idea what's going to happen in the next.” | Writing an episode in someone else's room / needing a development fee → an outline is mandatory (a contract milestone); if you are the showrunner and can hold structure while writing → Mazzara/Sorkin is viable. Mazzara drew the boundary himself: “All script problems… come down to structure problems.” See `sw-writers-room` for details |
| **Is a teaser mandatory?** | Calvisi / Douglas / Goldberg & Rabkin: the teaser is the first gate that grabs the audience | Oberg: only if it serves a dramatic purpose, otherwise “just pointless”; Landau: many shows start directly in Act One | With commercials and a viewer holding the remote → a teaser is mandatory; strong serial / binge platform → “a short first act + late title card” can replace it (Scandal, Modern Family) |

## IX. Diagnostic Checklist (Ask Yourself Item by Item After Finishing an Episode)

1. Can you state this episode's **core question** in one sentence? (Mazzara: “What is this episode about?”)
2. Does every line have a **log line attached to the protagonist (not a guest character)**? Is it a real story, or merely a situation?
3. Does the A story run through every act? Do the act breaks all land on the A or B story? Is any act ending on the C story — if so, that line may actually be A or B.
4. Does each act have 4–5 beats and 5–7 scenes? Is the episode's total scene count between 20 (five or six acts) and 28–30 (four acts)? **(Scene counts are valid only for American one-hour episodes: for half-hour comedy, 15–20 scenes [multi-camera] or 40–50 scenes [single-camera], see section 4 of `sw-sitcom-comedy`; for a Chinese domestic 45-minute episode, 25–35 scenes, see `sw-chinese-series-practice`. The three numbers differ by a factor of two to three; choose by “shooting method + running time,” do not scale.)**
5. **(This item is valid only for American one-hour episodes; skip it for half-hour and Chinese domestic drama.)** Do the page anchors line up? (Four acts with teaser: 17–18 / 30 / 45 / 60; Calvisi's five acts: teaser ≤10, Act Two ends at 30, Act Three at 40, Act Four at 48; AOP at 37–40; worst thing at the 4/5 point) **(Same as above: only for American one-hour episodes; do not run this item for half-hour or Chinese domestic drama.)**
6. Is there any scene longer than 3 pages that is not a complete beat? A four-page meeting scene is a red flag.
7. Is any scene there only for exposition? Is any “moment without a character want” being used as a scene?
8. Does every scene have the five parts (want / obstacle / at least three escalations, action preferred / decision / resolution)? At the end of the scene, does the audience know where the hero stands emotionally?
9. Can you state clearly what event each act out is? Does it grow out of the character's jeopardy/risk/fear? Is a “surprise” act out set up so that the audience “should have seen it coming—but didn't”?
10. How many of the eight act-out forms did you use? **Does at least one act use dialogue-free action or a freeze-frame on an object** (and has its grammar been taught earlier)?
11. Does the Act Two break change the nature of the story, or merely raise the danger? Does the end of Act Three turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat?
12. Stakes test: for this character, is “doing nothing in the face of the crisis” a viable option? Have you marked the +/− polarity of every act? Is there an act that ends on a “win”?
13. Where is each of the four information tools used? Does the protagonist know more than the audience for long stretches (especially backstory necessary to understand their motivation)? Will this season's big mystery be resolved by the season finale?
14. Is the teaser a story or information? If you used a teaser flashback, did it accidentally answer the episode's dramatic question?
15. Does the final act only do pay-off and introduce no new information? (**Not applicable to long Chinese domestic series**: the Chinese system requires selling another big 关子 (guānzi — an end-of-segment suspense device) at the end of every episode, which directly conflicts with this item. When writing Chinese domestic drama, use this item only for the “season finale,” and follow the end-of-episode suspense rules in `sw-chinese-series-practice` for individual episodes.) Before the second-to-last act ends, does the audience clearly know which accounts remain unsettled? Does the ending have something to “discover” while **not** cashing out everything?
16. Streaming script: after removing the ACT labels, can the invisible acts still be drawn with the three tests (story day / location change / core question turning)? Does every act end with a transfer of information or power?
17. Season shape: is this episode in the right place in the season (six-episode season: unfolding / desire / exposure / bottle episode / false victory / closed occasion settled)? Do the season finale and the pilot have a symmetrical reversal? Does each line's move also generate debt for the next season?
18. Speed-up check: can the coolest thing at the end of Act Four be moved to the end of the teaser? Does this episode have “the scene everyone will be talking about the next day”?
19. Production check: have you calculated the ratio of interiors/exteriors and day/night scenes? Did you write only DAY / NIGHT? Have you turned production limitations into the three choices of a bottle episode?

## X. Workflow: From an Episode Idea to a Delivery Draft

1. **Settle three things before writing** (Oberg): **series type** (procedural / serial / hybrid / limited / anthology / sitcom), **story-type** (plot-led / character-led / theme-led / hybrid / exception), **format** (episode length + broad category). If any is vague, do not write the outline yet. (For story-type judgment, see `sw-series-engine-bible`.)
2. **Choose a structural comp** (same item in Calvisi / Landau): find a **successful** show similar to yours in tone / genre / subject matter / audience and use it as the structural template; if you cannot get the script, watch the show and write a scene list. Ask four things: **Is there a teaser? How many act breaks? How many scenes per act? Is there a tag / epilogue?** It is also your comp when pitching. **A ready-made sample table of 47 shows** (show name | year | format | network/platform | act structure | premise/non-premise/hybrid pilot | showrunner; Landau 1e appendix, 2014 data) is in section 11 of [reference.md](reference.md), so you do not have to count them yourself.
3. **Break two or three episodes of the comp and fill in the grid** (Douglas): name A/B/C, write one log line per line attached to the protagonist, and fill in the letters cell by cell.
4. **Define this episode's core question**, and each line's **entry scene and exit scene** (Downton's line-table method: 8–12 lines per episode is the ceiling, and 1–2 of them must be unrelated to the main crisis).
5. **Reverse-engineer** (Douglas): first fill in three places in the grid — **the opening, the “worst thing” at the three-quarter point, and the ending**; do only the A story or A+B, leaving room for C; then work backward from the cliffhanger to the preceding beats.
6. **Build a landmark or dramatic sequence for each line separately**: use Miller's six landmarks (in a pilot, the hero committed must serve two purposes), or Oberg's inciting incident → subgoal → sequence → scene. **At this point write the lines separately; do not weave.**
7. **Compress into a one-page grid** (Miller, chapter 5): a row of act-label cards across the top, a column of cards under each act, one card per scene, with the scene heading + **character want** written on the card. For a show without commercials, break first as **teaser + four acts** and remove the labels in the final draft; for six acts, split Acts Three and Four in half. Check at a glance: are the landmarks in place, are the act breaks hard enough, is the protagonist proactive, does this episode fit the season?
8. **Lay out the act outs**: hand each act out to a different line; choose from the eight forms, and make sure at least one act is dialogue-free or object-based; keep the squeeze within the act (it needs to be played out).
9. **Give each episode a “procedural line” as a metronome** (a common feature of the six West Wing episodes): an external crisis (a military strike, a besieged embassy, the length of a surgery, vote counting) provides legitimate interruptions and a “one hour left” deadline, and finally hands its numbers to the protagonist as a moral argument; it does not carry an act out.
10. **Write the outline / beat sheet**: for one-hour drama, use a treatment or step outline; **Blum gives series treatments 5–10 pages, “the shorter the better,” and they must include act breaks** (film treatments are 10–20 pages); write each “beat” in the outline as that scene's log line. For the room process, see `sw-writers-room`.
11. **Weave only when you write the teleplay**: do multi-level staging within the same scene, ellipsis, parallel editing; at this point add the five parts to every scene.
12. **Write long and cut, or hold to the page count**: Succession's approach is 70+ pages per episode (only then is there room to refocus in editing); Calvisi's opposing warning is that beginners should not exceed 60 pages. Which one to take depends on whether you are the showrunner and whether the show has started shooting.
13. **Make one decision about format before delivery**: label ACT or remove ACT (per the criteria in 2.6); for the teaser act out write `SMASH CUT TO: MAIN TITLES`, and at the end of each act `FADE OUT.` + `END OF ACT N` (100% consistent across the six West Wing episodes); **pilot scripts do not carry scene numbers** (Miller: “Pilot scripts do NOT have scene numbers.”).
14. **Run the diagnostic checklist in section IX**, then use Grace Paley's method for endings (as retold by Landau) — **“I look for the lies.”**

## XI. Exercises

1. **Break one episode and fill in the grid** (Douglas's classroom assignment): name the A/B/C lines, write one log line for each, fill in the letters cell by cell; mark which line each end-of-act suspense belongs to, which line only starts in which act, and which line ends in Act Three.
2. **Goldberg & Rabkin's double exercise** (p0033): ① watch an episode, stopping at each act, **write the narrative arc of that act in one sentence, then write that act's final scene in one or two lines**; ② watch the **Act Two break** of several episodes in a row and analyze how that moment changes the direction of the story.
3. **Act-out count**: take one episode, write out which of the eight types each of the five act outs belongs to, and judge which one is schmuck bait.
4. **Calvisi's Story Map**: use the Basic 17 columns + Full map to break down a pilot, mark which page the AOP falls on and whether the DOW is at the 4/5 point; then beat out your own pilot using the same table.
5. **Miller's landmark sheet**: write six lines for your own pilot; then break down a pilot that is similar to yours in **structure** (not subject matter); the breakdown must include scene headings, story beats, character wants, scene ins and outs, act breaks, and landmarks.
6. **Escalation breakdown**: pick two 1.5–2-minute scenes, count the escalations one by one (target: 10 or more), mark the decision and resolution, and count how many are action and how many are dialogue.
7. **Reverse-engineering invisible acts**: take an episode of a show with no ACT labels (Succession, Homeland, Sopranos), draw the act positions using the three tests, and write “the act-out event + the basis for the division” for each act; compare with the Succession measurements in section 4 of [reference.md](reference.md).
8. **Information-management rewrite**: rewrite an act out that “cuts away halfway through the fight” into three versions — one surprise, one newly established dramatic irony, and one mystery; judge which makes you want to watch the next episode more.
9. **Act-count conversion**: convert a four-act episode outline into five acts (split Act Four in half), six acts (split Acts Three and Four in half), and no acts (replace with story-day markers), finding a defensible event for each new act break.
10. **Speed-up exercise** (Rabkin): move the coolest thing at the end of the pilot's Act Four to the end of the teaser, rewrite the whole episode, and see whether the next sixty pages can sustain the same speed.
11. **Budget-driven**: give yourself four prohibitions (no guest stars, no exteriors, no new sets, no extras), write a bottle episode — one night, one building — and use it to advance a secret in the season arc that you have not dared to tell, and increase the number of lines from 4 to 5.
12. **Six-episode season shape**: write one-sentence outlines for six episodes following the Fleabag skeleton, and designate an object that **changes hands once per episode**; the final image of the season finale resolves only the theme.
13. **Symmetry exercise** (Succession S1): find a number / object / line in the first episode, and have the season finale use the same thing to complete the reversal.
14. **Container episode exercise**: write an episode in which “everyone is locked in one building + communication is limited,” then write an episode of “a ritual day with a schedule” (list the fixed segments and insert one conflict into each).
