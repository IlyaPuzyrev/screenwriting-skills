# The Half-Hour Comedy: Format Specifications, a Catalog of Joke Techniques, and Structural Templates

> Use in conjunction with SKILL.md.

## 1. The Three Formats, Clause by Clause (Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*, Appendix A)

| | film / single-cam | tape / multi-cam | animation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page count | **27–30** (one page ≈ one minute) | **45–50** | **40–50** |
| Stage directions | Mixed case | **ALL CAPS**; on entrances and exits, **underline the character name and the verb** (`JAKE ENTERS`) | Mixed case, single-spaced, stopping at character position 70; a few camera directions allowed |
| Dialogue blocks | Single-spaced | **Double-spaced** | Double-spaced |
| dialogue cue | Lowercase, in parentheses | **ALL CAPS, in parentheses, set as another line of dialogue** | Same as tape |
| Scene numbers | None | **Lettered or numbered in sequence except for the cold open/tag** (`SCENE A`); most producers require each scene to start on a new page | None |
| First appearance of a new character | Name in full caps | Name underlined | Full caps |
| End-of-scene transitions | Mostly omitted | **Usually ends with `CUT TO:` / `DISSOLVE TO:`**; transitions in full caps **and underlined** | Titles and transitions in bold |
| Margins (character positions, 12pt Courier New, whole page shifted six characters right for binding) | Body **16→75** / dialogue **28→63** / cue **34→57** / character names **from 40** / transitions **from 60** | Body **16→70** / dialogue (including cue) **26→60** / character names 40 / transitions 60 | Same as film; stage directions stop at 70 |

1. **Markup is fixed**: `FADE OUT.` → two blank lines → centered, underlined, all-caps `END OF ACT ONE` → **the next act begins on a new page** `ACT TWO` → two blank lines `FADE IN:` → one blank line, then the opening; the tag follows the same rule as a new act (new page, then `TAG`); the ending is `END OF EPISODE`. If the first scene is a cold open, the scene after the first commercial is Act One, and you write `END OF COLD OPEN` first.
2. **Deliberately omitted from a spec**: scene numbers, a page header showing the draft, the cast list under the scene heading, separate slugs for sound effects/visual effects, camera instructions, the words "First Draft," a completion date—adding any of them slows the read and makes people think this is a production draft that has been through a punch-up room.
3. **Never write a dialogue cue if you can avoid it**: professional writers almost never use one; trust the director and the actors.
4. **Set economics**: aim for **3 sets** per episode (1 main + 2 secondary), with a ceiling of 5 (Blum p0143). ⚠️ **This is the economics of standing sets on a multi-camera stage; it does not apply to single-camera location shows**—single-camera counts the number of shooting locations and the number of moves (several spaces in one building count as one location). Multi-camera shows usually keep 3–5 **standing sets** plus one or two **swing sets**; in a spec, add at most one or two new sets. A Hawaii or Italy episode is a sweeps-period staff stunt; putting one in a spec tells people you are "out of the loop or just not very bright."
5. **Two rules for animation**: the number of acts varies (*The Simpsons* has 2, *King of the Hill* and *South Park* have 3), so verify against the target show; the format is closer to the single-camera film format, and only the double-spaced dialogue preserves a sitcom trace. World-view rule (David X. Cohen): **an absurd situation + a realistic human reaction = the engine; the absurdity must not spread into the layer of character reaction**. Groening's warning: don't assume you can be sloppy just because it's animation.

## 2. The Nine Classic Predicaments and the Six Character Mixes

**The nine classic predicaments**: ① The Big Lie; ② The Big Secret; ③ The Misunderstanding; ④ A Rock and a Hard Place; ⑤ The Surefire Scheme; ⑥ Something That Rocks the Boat (an outsider threatens the status quo → showdown → the outsider leaves); ⑦ Time Bomb; ⑧ Trapped in a Space ("It's a pressure cooker."); ⑨ Sex; and a tenth, The Dead Body. Listing them **is not an instruction to repeat them but a demonstration of how to weave comedy into the premise**—"as long as it is used freshly and true to character, it can be used again" (p0051).

**The six classic character mixes**: Role Reversal / Fish Out of Water / Odd Coupling / Sex (two people urgently need to be together yet are always prevented—after *Cheers* actually put Sam and Diane together the show weakened, so the producers split them up and cloned the original tension with Rebecca) / **Pretense** (pretending to be someone you are not → inevitable exposure and humiliation; Smith calls it "this is a big one") / plus one category he left open as an opportunity. One principle governs them all: **characters should complement each other, not duplicate each other; each must contribute something new**. Jim on his own is not funny; the laughs come from his role as a foil—sometimes he goads, sometimes he comments, and sometimes he simply "reacts as we would" (p0052).

## 3. Landau's Seven-Step Formula (Complete)

① Establish the everyday world, then break it with a problem (the A-story catalyst, usually the cold open/tease); the stakes are a **"tremendous trifle"**—a small thing that matters enormously to the character; ② Act One begins after the titles as the search for a solution; the B/C story problems appear for the supporting characters, and the protagonist settles on a game plan; ③ a single unifying theme threads through the A/B/C stories; ④ **a midpoint reversal plus an escalation of stakes—placed at the end of Act One in a two-act sitcom and at the end of Act Two in a three-act structure**: "trying to fix a small problem (accidentally) creates a bigger one"; the image is **the protagonist chased up a tree by a vicious dog in Act One, while in later acts the bystanders throw stones at him up there**; ⑤ after the midpoint, move toward all-is-lost (near the end of the second half); ⑥ at the end of Act Two (or Act Three), the problem is overcome through **honest communication and/or making amends** ("In movies, the climax = the truth"; the same holds for sitcom); ⑦ after the commercials there is sometimes a very short tag that shows the protagonist safe and content in the everyday world.

## 4. The Six-Episode Season Skeleton of *Fleabag* and Two Ending Rules

*Fleabag* has two seasons of 6 episodes × about 25 minutes; every episode is strictly `cold open (1–3 minutes) → TITLES → 3–7 segments → button (≤1 page, often wordless)`. The season skeleton can be copied: ① lay out every character and the suspense (a typical episode), giving each character a defining line at first appearance; ② turn the emotion of episode 1 into a desire that can be pursued; ③ put the first "reveal" at a family/social occasion; ④ **the bottle episode**—an enclosed space plus a single prohibition; the protagonist keeps violating it; each violation is a laugh, and the last is an emotional blow; ⑤ **the false-victory high point** ("the best day ever"); ⑥ bring the whole ensemble back to **one enclosed social occasion** (an exhibition/wedding) where every storyline closes.

Two ending rules: **the season finale's button does not resolve the plot; it resolves the theme** (S1: "People make mistakes." plus the pencil eraser); **one object runs through the whole season, changing hands in every episode, and its final move must change the protagonist's understanding of her past** (the statue: stolen → sold → birthday present → confession → returned → trophy → wedding gift → "She was based on your mother." → she takes it away again).

## 5. Freelance Stage-by-Stage Schedule (2009 Data)

Schedule (freelance, 2009 data): oral pitch preparation 2–5 days | outline 2 days–1 week | outline revisions 2–5 days | first draft 1–2 weeks | second draft 2 days–1 week.

## 6. The Nine Predicaments, One by One (Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*, 2009, ch. 4, p0045–0051)

Each entry gives four things: **mechanism / the book's own example (only the shows the book names) / conditions of failure**. Three general rules come first because they govern all nine:

- **General rule A (p0046, precondition)**: the audience must believe the characters will not really be hurt, and must have been given the signal that "this is comedy." Break either and the predicament immediately becomes drama.
- **General rule B (p0046, shelf life)**: a predicament's productivity depends on being "**unresolved**"—"a continuing predicament woven into the premise keeps producing laughs until it is resolved," and while it is unresolved even unrelated jokes seem funnier. **So the first moment each predicament fails is the moment it is resolved.**
- **General rule C (p0051, paraphrase)**: "These old predicaments keep coming back for two reasons: they reflect human behavior, and they are funny. As long as it is used freshly and true to character, it can be used (again)." Listing the nine is not to make you repeat them but to show how to weave comedy into the premise.
- Entries marked "**the notes give no special limitation**" are governed only by A/B/C; no limits are invented on Smith's behalf.

**① The Big Lie** (p0046–47)
- Mechanism: a moment of weakness → a lie → the lie grows → collapse.
- Original example: *South Park*, Cartman pretends to have Tourette's so he can swear at will, is invited onto *Dateline*, then begins uncontrollably saying what he really thinks; at the end Kyle tries to expose him but instead creates chaos that saves him.
- The three variants the book lists: the liar is intoxicated by his own good fortune (*The Simpsons*, Bart pretends to be a hero and then believes his own press); a moral dilemma; some endings let the character "escape with dignity and a prize."
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation. The correct addition at the sequence level is to stack Big Lie → Bigger Lies (p0064), not to repeat the same lie at the same level.

**② The Big Secret** (p0047)
- Mechanism: you know a secret but cannot tell it → it leaks everywhere → you tell the person involved at the worst possible moment.
- Original example: *The Office*, "The Secret"—Michael promises to keep Jim's crush on Pam secret and then leaks it twice.
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation. But the same example demonstrates the predicament's **compounding direction** (p0063): Jim asks Michael to keep the secret → Michael assumes the two are now close friends → Jim is forced to hang out with Michael. The secret alone cannot fill an episode; what fills it is "the new obligations that keeping the secret creates."

**③ The Misunderstanding** (p0047)
- Mechanism: connect what you see into a false chain of cause and effect, then act on the false conclusion.
- Original example: *30 Rock*, Liz sees a wall of maps in her new neighbor Raheem's apartment, suspects he is a terrorist, and reports him to Homeland Security; he is actually making an audition tape for *The Amazing Race*; after being interrogated he threatens to "create something spectacular."
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation.

**④ A Rock and a Hard Place** (p0048)
- Mechanism: forced to choose between two good options or two bad ones.
- Original example: *My Name Is Earl*, Earl must wear tassels and dance on stage to apologize to a stripper he injured; or sleep with an ex-con's elderly mother to avoid being killed.
- **Period limitation (2009 judgment)**: the old protagonist would eventually choose the noble side; Smith says equally common now is to be selfish to the end and lose everything, "**or not lose—the days when selfishness was always punished are long gone**." So the old rule that "he must choose right" no longer holds; do not end by it.
- Failure condition: it does not hold when the two options are not equally weighted (once the choice is obvious, the predicament disappears)—this follows from Smith's definition; the book does not list it separately.

**⑤ The Surefire Scheme** (p0048)
- Mechanism: "Trust me, what could go wrong?" → the plan proceeds by its own logic to disaster.
- Original example: *South Park*, the boys dye Cartman ginger so he can taste discrimination, and he incites the ginger kids to massacre all non-redheads.
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation. Adjacent rule (p0129): sitcom characters' strategies must be "stupidly outrageous"; "sitcom characters almost always ignore obvious solutions"; once the character adopts a reasonable plan, this predicament goes out on the spot.

**⑥ Something That Rocks the Boat** (p0048)
- Mechanism: an outsider threatens the status quo → showdown → the outsider leaves, the status quo is restored.
- Original examples: Devon Banks in *30 Rock*; in *Everybody Hates Chris*, Greg comes to stay for a week and the whole family dotes on Greg and forgets Chris.
- Failure conditions (the book gives two in ch. 10, Visiting Characters, p0143): **the visitor must not overshadow the regular characters** ("Not Too Fresh and Exciting"—a star's agent will call), and **the story the outsider brings must quickly become the regular characters' story**; his line count must be controlled. Fail these two and the outsider is not a predicament but scene-stealing.

**⑦ Time Bomb** (p0049)
- Mechanism: complete an impossible task by a deadline.
- Original example: throw a party while the parents are away, the vase breaks, the parents come home early. (The book uses a situational example here rather than naming a show—the notes have no show name for this one; none is supplied.)
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation.

**⑧ Trapped in a Space** (p0050)
- Mechanism: lock incompatible people in one space, each with a reason they must get out. "It's a pressure cooker."
- Original example: an elevator with a pregnant woman or a claustrophobe, one of whom has a wedding to get to.
- **Explicit failure condition (the book's own)**: the related variant "locked outside, naked" **usually sustains only one scene, not a whole episode**. Likewise, a spatial predicament ends the moment "someone gets out"—it belongs to the sequence level, "more finite, occur in real time" (p0064).
- Close relative: *Fleabag*'s retreat episode (S1E4) is how a half-hour show turns this into a whole episode—an enclosed space plus one prohibition (silence); the protagonist violates each in turn, every violation is a laugh, the last is an emotional blow (see §11).

**⑨ Sex** (p0050)
- Mechanism: pursuit and being pursued, both want it but are prevented, office romance.
- Original example: in the predicament section the book gives situational types; the named examples are in the character mix entry for Sex (Dwight–Angela, Pam–Jim, *The Office*).
- **Failure condition (same source as §7④, p0054)**: let the two actually be together and the machine stops—after *Cheers* put Sam and Diane together the show weakened, and the producers had to split them up and clone the original tension with Rebecca.

**⑩ The Dead Body (the tenth, which Smith lists outside the nine)** (p0050–51)
- Mechanism: hide a corpse, be suspected of murder, impersonate the deceased, be forced to care for the dead person's dog.
- Original example: MTM's *The Mary Tyler Moore Show*, "Chuckles Bites the Dust"—a clown dressed as a peanut is trampled to death by a parade elephant, and the whole episode is a group of people stifling laughter.
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation; this one is the most sensitive to General Rule A (the audience must be certain no one is really hurt).

## 7. The Six Character Mixes, One by One (Smith, ch. 4, p0051–0056)

Two principles govern all six (p0052): **characters should complement each other, not duplicate each other; each must contribute something new** (an opposing point of view / a knack for causing trouble / a bizarre way of life); **some characters are not funny on their own**—Dwight generates laughs anywhere, but Jim is not funny alone; his laughs come from being a foil, "sometimes he goads, other times he comments, and still other times he just reacts as we would." The corollary is the universal failure condition: **the moment two characters contribute the same thing, the mix is void** (diagnostic checklist item ③ asks exactly this).

**① Role Reversal** (p0053)
- Mechanism: forced into a role that goes against one's nature.
- Original examples: a single father with three small children; covering someone else's job; posing as an enemy's spouse. **Series-level example**: *Married... with Children* and *The Simpsons* used the "anti-family" to reverse the Cosby-style family and built FOX.
- Variant **Casting against type** (p0061): Christopher Walken playing a timid geek on *SNL*—Smith calls it "role reversal through casting."
- Failure condition: the reversal must **violate** that character's nature; once he adapts to the new role, or the role already suits him, the tension is zero. (This is the reverse of the definition; the book does not list it separately.)

**② Fish Out of Water** (p0053)
- Mechanism: put a character into a world that is not his own.
- Original examples: *The Beverly Hillbillies*; *Entourage* (four guys from Queens enter Hollywood); *Weeds* (a soccer mom deals drugs). Smith adds that the series-premise predicament of *Entourage* is fish out of water (p0075).
- Failure condition: the notes give no special limitation. Adjacent warning (p0089): **the real gold mine is cultural difference, not accent**—you may let foreign characters be foolish, but do not write them as fools/crooks/sleazebags; *South Park*'s Starvin' Marvin and *The Simpsons*' Apu are uses that turn the tables on xenophobia.

**③ Odd Coupling** (p0053)
- Mechanism: bind two incompatible ways of life together.
- Original examples: *The Odd Couple* (neat freak vs. slob, cultural snob vs. blue-collar); Charlie/Alan in *Two and a Half Men*; Liz/Jack in *30 Rock*. **At the episode level**, you can temporarily introduce a visiting relative/lover/business partner with an opposite personality.
- Failure condition: when a visitor is used for a single-episode odd coupling, the two Visiting Characters constraints from p0143 apply (the visitor must not overshadow the regulars, and the story must quickly become the regulars' story).

**④ Sex** (p0053–54)
- Mechanism: two leads "urgently need to consummate... but are somehow prevented."
- Original examples: Dwight–Angela and Pam–Jim in *The Office*. Another type: **a healthy sex life that is constantly interrupted** (*The King of Queens*).
- **Explicit failure condition (the book's own)**: after *Cheers* actually put Sam and Diane together the show weakened; the producers split them up and **cloned the original tension with Rebecca**. That is: consummation = failure; the fix is to swap in a new person and rebuild the same tension.

**⑤ Pretense** (p0054–56, Smith's own words: "This is a big one")
- Mechanism: pretend to be someone you are not → **inevitable exposure and humiliation**. Freud's phrase is "seized dignity and authority by a deception."
- Original examples: Michael Scott in *The Office*, Johnny Drama in *Entourage*, Larry David in *Curb Your Enthusiasm*; Bart cheats his way into the gifted class in *The Simpsons*; Michael brags that he has an improvisational gift and ends up a pariah in the acting class.
- **The book's own failure condition**: the worst version is the reunion episode (pretending to be rich/thin/engaged)—but *30 Rock* reverses it: Liz confronts the bully from her school days and discovers that the bully is herself (p0056). **That is: the standard pretense template is used up; the only rescue is to switch the person being exposed to the protagonist herself.**

**⑥ The sixth category: treating gay identity as a source of tension** (p0056)
- The book's shortest entry: after *Will & Grace* there were "few new shows," and Smith flags it as "**this is an opportunity for you**."
- **Period flag (2009)**: this is the 2009 revised edition's judgment about a gap in the market, not a judgment about how to write. To use it today you must reassess the current series landscape; the book gives it no mechanism and no original example—**so it is not an available option**: this pack treats character mix as **five usable + one questionable**; when assembling a mix, do not use it just to "make six." (The existing §2 records it as "the category he left as an opportunity"; here we state exactly which category it is, following the notes.)

**Dynamic use of the mix** (p0064, sequence level, parallel to the predicament compounding in §6): temporarily form an "unnatural alliance"—in *Seinfeld*, Jerry and Newman ask each other for help; the audience waits both for the story predicament to resolve **and** for the alliance to collapse; the ending restores the original configuration ("order (the character mix) was restored").

## 8. A Library of Joke Techniques (Smith, ch. 6, p0067–0092; ch. 12, Writing Dialogue, p0162–0183)

### 8.1 The Ten Comedic Nuances: Each Character's Own Way of Being Funny

Definition and use: a nuance is **that character's fixed way of being funny**, not the writer's toolbox—before writing dialogue, decide which one or two this character uses and give him no others. The notes transcribe **8 named items** from ch. 12; the book says "ten," and **the remaining two have no name in the notes, so none are invented here**.

| nuance | The book's original examples (named shows and lines) |
|---|---|
| **Understated Response** | Darnell in *My Name Is Earl*, Hank in *King of the Hill*—low-key even when the sky falls; Smith's judgment: "incongruous reactions almost guarantee a laugh" |
| **Exaggerated Response** | Joy in *My Name Is Earl*, Tracy Jordan in *30 Rock* |
| **Irony** | Ari in *Entourage*: "Let's hug it out, bitch!"; Johnny Drama: "I've been working steady the past twelve years, minus the last three." |
| **Nervous Babbling** | Kenneth in *30 Rock* (the short-burst type); Kelly in *The Office*—it has become the character's main trait |
| **Pulls No Punches** | Hank in *King of the Hill*: "I wouldn't have answered, but you coulda left a message, which I would've quickly erased."; Ari's mailroom line to Adam |
| **Space-Shot POV** | Creed in *The Office*—"Their words make sense, but only if you live in a different dimension" |
| **Wordplay** | *Seinfeld*: "How do they do it? How? Do they know people I don't know?..."—repetition, juxtaposition, multiple meanings |
| **Double Entendre** | Charlie in *Two and a Half Men*, Quagmire in *Family Guy*—hearing an innocent remark as a dirty one |

**Do not confuse this with the adjacent list**: the same section also has a list of "how emotion changes language" (Arguing/Depressed/Happy/Fearful/Anxious, plus seduce/placate/manipulate/impress/terrorize). That is **the same character deformed by different emotions**, not the character's fixed way of being funny.

### 8.2 A Library of Examples on the Setup Side

- **The two-part setup (the most important item in this chapter)**: *30 Rock* pilot—Jack asks Liz whether she knows about the GE Tri-vection oven → "I don't cook very much" (first part: she tries to shut him down) → Jack refuses to be shut down and delivers a long, precise dissection of her life ("New York third-wave feminist... Single and pretending to be happy about it. Over-scheduled, under-sexed...", the second part, or development, itself containing several small laughs) → Pete: "That's dead on." → Liz: "Are you gonna guess my weight now?" → Jack: "You don't want me to do that." **Smith's control experiment (p0071)**: cut Jack's speech down to the single line "New York third-wave feminist" and go straight to the weight joke—"Still funny but not as funny. Plus, it seems much more openly hostile, which isn't true to the characters." → the development section determines both **the strength of the laugh** and **the truth of the character**.
- **The setup must be ordinary and credible**: *My Name Is Earl*—Randy reads the invitation slowly: "You—are—cordially—invited—to Joy and Darnell's wedding"; Earl looks outside: "I wonder if Willie dropped my invitation in the parking lot"; Catalina: "I don't think so. This says 'Randy Hickey plus anybody but Earl.'" The audience guesses that he was not invited; it does not guess "anybody but Earl."
- **Split it up and plant it far ahead (three layers)**: *Two and a Half Men*—Charlie teaches Jake not to apologize sincerely, "just shut up and look sorry. You've got those big cute eyes. Use 'em!" (layer-one laugh: non-parental advice from a parent figure) → pages later Jake tries the doe-eyed look on the teacher and fails (layer two) → Charlie himself uses the same look on the teacher (layer three, the topping). Without the first setup, the other two laughs are "far less funny."
- **Never telegraph it (with a counter-example)**: *The Simpsons*—Homer: "Wow, Barney! You brought a whole beer keg." → Barney: "Yeah. Where can I fill it up?" **Counter-example**: Homer adds "Is that thing full?" → Barney: "No. Where can I fill it up?"—the joke is destroyed (p0072–73).
- **Life itself is half a setup**: *Seinfeld*, the car-rental counter—Lydia refuses to honor the reservation and goes off to "find the supervisor"; Jerry performs a fake conversation between clerk and supervisor for Elaine ("Hey Marge, these people think I'm talking to you so just pretend you're talking to me..."), Elaine plays along as the supervisor, then Lydia returns: "I'm sorry, my supervisor says there's nothing we can do." The whole routine runs on the audience's shared frustration that reservations mean nothing. Shared cultural experience (famous shows, celebrities, religious ritual, presidents, anything *Star Trek*) works the same way.
- **Tension can supply the setup and the punchline at once (the source of dramedy laughs, p0075–76)**: *Entourage*, the double date—Johnny brags that he has booked the goddess from years ago and promises Turtle will "get laid" (setup) → Turtle's date is loud and ugly; the laughs come from expressions and outrageous behavior rather than lines (development) → Turtle wants to leave, Johnny begs him to stay, and a rare verbal punchline appears: "We're still going to split this, right?" → in the hot tub the two women announce that they are swapping partners; the goddess leaves with Turtle and Johnny is stuck with the ugly one. "setup, comedic tension, and punchline are all supplied by character actions, not dialogue." Smith's own note: the "double-date disaster" premise is not new; it works on titillation and the characters' shameless behavior, and "seemed just new enough."

### 8.3 A Library of Examples on the Punchline Side

- **Put the punch word at the end**: *The Office*—Dwight reads "the Scranton Christian Science Reader and the Scranton Daily Worker"; Jim's interview: "I think that he thinks it's a paper for people who work. Daily." Much funnier than "it's a daily paper for people who work." The ancestral example (p0033): "Take my wife... please!" dies the moment it becomes "Please take my wife!"
- **Don't go past the joke**: the punchline is usually at the end of a speech too; saying more after it will "trample the thing." Several jokes in one speech can be separated by a beat/pause/ellipsis, but it is best to trust the actor and director to leave room to breathe. **Exception (p0078)**: if the character is garrulous, if the plot has them rambling, or if the extra words are "a necessary rhythm to her speech and are true to her character," keep them. The counter-example at the other extreme is Ellen DeGeneres-style "more is more"—ramble, then ramble about your own rambling (p0089).
- **Topper**: *The Office*—Michael: "find out what motivates people more than anything else." → Dwight (O.S.): "Sex." (the camera pulls back; he is standing right behind Michael) → Michael: "It's illegal, can't do that. Next best thing." → Dwight: "Torture." Why it works: jumping from sex to torture is "so huge it's completely unexpected," and it is true (torture does motivate people) and links sex with torture; the first joke builds momentum, and the audience is hit again before it can recover. **Three limits (p0080)**: top it at most twice ("Any more and the audience will notice"); some jokes should be left to stand alone (they carry a dramatic moment, control the pace, or have already said everything they can); **in dramedy, usually no topper** (too conspicuous). Terminology note: saying "topper" in front of other comedy writers will get you laughed at, but there is no replacement word.
- **The full four-part original example of a running gag/callback**: *Two and a Half Men*, "Miss Pasternak" (p0080–83)—① Charlie gets rid of Jake, then smiles: "I'm sorry, it is Miss Pasternak, right?" (we know he is deploying his only talent); ② a few scenes later she kisses him, he sighs "Oh, Miss Pasternak," she asks why he doesn't use her name, "I don't know. This is so much hotter." (he controls the situation); ③ he wants to dump her: "Listen, Delores, we need to talk." → "Oh, call me Miss Pasternak." → "Uh... not right now." (the power reverses); ④ she announces that God wants her to be "God's instrument" and reform him: "Now, sit up straight and eat your pie." → "All right." → "'All right' what?" → "All right, Miss Pasternak." (CHARLIE GLUMLY EATS HIS PIE—the great manipulator reduced to a schoolboy).
  - Three methods: the same character repeats the original punchline in a new situation / a different character repeats it / a different character delivers a variant of the same setup; each return often carries a second layer of meaning, and **the last is often ironic or a reversal that lands the big laugh**.
  - **A standing series-level runner**: *Seinfeld*'s Jerry and Newman saying each other's names with mutual loathing; early *South Park* killing Kenny every episode ("viewers were just waiting to see how Kenny would be killed").
  - Terminology confusion (p0083): a running gag is also called a **runner**, but runner often also means "a very small subplot," which is not necessarily funny.
  - Economics (p0083 NOTE): against the benchmark of 2–4 jokes per page, **the topper and the running gag are the cheapest ways to raise the joke count**.
- **Stuck? Go back and change the setup (p0083–84)**: Smith himself labored through several drafts for a weak punchline; a passing producer said casually, "Change the setup?"—and after the joke's premise was changed, "half a dozen great punchlines popped into my head." The rule: when the punchline does not work, check whether the setup needs **a different incongruity or a bigger contrast**. The companion is Michael Reiss's (*The Simpsons*) three conditions plus a promise: "It has to be funny, it's got to serve plot, and it can't make the character look bad... you might work for two hours, but you'll get it."
- **Go for Literal (p0084–85)**: Helitzer's textbook example, "Call me a taxi." / "Okay, you're a taxi." The original example from *My Name Is Earl*: Randy hides under the table eating bad meatloaf and comes out with "This stinks, Earl." → Earl (pointing at the meatloaf): "Hold your nose. It tastes better than it smells." → Randy: "Not the meatloaf. Having to hide."—literal and ironic. Observation: literal jokes usually come from characters who are **very stupid or very clever**.

### 8.4 Action, Visuals, and Sound (p0085–0089)

- **Mannerism**: Ray Romano's deer-in-the-headlights look on *Everybody Loves Raymond* can milk ten seconds of laughter; Charlie Sheen's shrug on *Two and a Half Men* gets him out of anything; Homer's "D'oh!" Some come from the actor, some are designed by the writer when building the premise—**when creating a new character, remember to give him one**.
- **Stage Business**: the actor fiddles with something while talking; it can contrast with the dialogue to raise tension; **it can be a punchline by itself** (he finally puts the fragile ornament back on the shelf—and the shelf collapses); it can also be unrelated to the plot, existing only to lift the comic atmosphere of a scene.
- **Take (look/reaction)**: a single expression from a startled character is often the entire punchline. The script can write "(reacts)" or "(beat)" as a prompt, but "**Better yet, let the actor and the director decide**."
- **Slapstick**: the structure is the same as a verbal joke (setup → payoff). Original setup: a friend gives an affectionate pat → the pat back is too hard → a shove → the two wrestle on the carpet (a friendly gesture turns hostile, true and unexpected). *Seinfeld* example: Kramer throws an oil-filled balloon from a building to test an invention; Jerry's new girlfriend happens to stop at the landing spot; Jerry shouts and she misunderstands (dialogue develops the setup); the balloon falls—**not showing the aftermath is also a big laugh**. Limitation: "very broad, even unrefined," used only when the show allows it.
- **Funny Visuals**: humiliating uniforms ("House of Wieners"); a couple eating in a sexually suggestive way; **hiding something** (an ex-girlfriend, a broken vase) where the gap between "what is said" and "what is seen" keeps producing small laughs and accumulates into the big laugh of discovery; "But who could possibly be so stupid?" → Stupid enters (worn out but always used, because it is a convenient way to bring people on stage).
- **Funny Sounds**: screaming next door, a car crash outside, falling down the stairs, a toilet flushing at the worst moment, a whisper repeated loudly.
- **Alliteration**: *King of the Hill*, the doctor: "considering you seem like a man who butters his bacon"—try "butters his steak." **Limitation**: it rarely gets a laugh by itself, but it can keep the comic momentum alive in a serious passage (p0088).
- **Small Truths, Big Laughs**: *The Office*, Dwight dialling: "Surf's up, Jim. Watch me ride this wave of inspiration! (GRINS, INTO PHONE) Yes, I'll hold."
- **The converse limitation for a spec (p0167)**: **do not pile visual jokes into a spec**—someone reading for ten minutes catches dialogue jokes more easily, and "a surprising number of industry pros aren't even able to appreciate visual jokes (in a script)"; some executives claim they only read dialogue and skip stage directions (p0112). Visual jokes are an asset once shot, not a selling point on the page.
  - ⚠️ **This sits in open contradiction with two positions elsewhere in the same skill**: the switch table says single-camera "relies more on visual jokes," while diagnostic item ㉓ asks "is there a scene that should have used a visual joke but was written as dialogue?" **Ruling (this pack's; none of the three books offers one): write visual jokes, but they cannot be the only source of laughs in a scene—keep at least one dialogue joke per scene as "paper insurance," and hold pure visual jokes to under ten percent of the whole draft.** The cold open is the exception: a whole cold open may be wordless.

### 8.5 A Library of Device-Driven Jokes (Waller-Bridge, *Fleabag: The Scriptures*)

Only specific devices not named in the main text of SKILL.md are collected here; the three basic uses of direct address and the season-arc escalation are in SKILL.md §VII.

- **The list joke (length itself is the joke)**: S1E2 (from L6300), Harry reads out the browsing history: "Anal, gangbang, mature, big cock, small tits, hentai, Asian, teen, MILF, big butts, lesbian, gay, facial, fetish, bukkake, young and old, swallow, rough, voyeur ... and public." → long pause → Fleabag: "Why you being so sexy?" → Harry: "Don't make me hate you. Loving you is painful enough." → "Ok, sorry ... but I really think you should write that down." Mechanism: the length of the list produces the laugh, **and then the same comic device is used to deflect a sincere moment**, proving the character's sickness.
- **The anticipatory joke**: S2E5—"(to camera) He's going to make a bassoon joke." → Martin actually makes it. The prediction to camera is itself a joke, and it turns the audience into accomplices.
- **Live judgment to camera (serial reversals)**: S2E2, the law office—"They've definitely fucked." / "Actually maybe not." / "Oh I've got it." / "They haven't." / "But they're going to."
- **"Don't say it—said it"**: S2E2, the climax of the church fair—"Just don't say it… Just said it." (the same grammar is recorded in SKILL.md §VII as three beats plus a fourth).
- **Using props for character self-description** (avoiding monologue): S1E3, the shoe shop—"I want to be that person." (the cool shoes) / "I have been that person." (the bad shoes) / "But most of the time I am that person." (the boring boots) "Like everybody else."; it ends on "everyone wants to be this person."
- **One action carries the entire drama**: S1E3, the garden kiss—"She doesn't move. She doesn't kiss back or recoil. She just bears it." Then the aggressor says, "You're an asshole."

## 9. The Nine Rules for a Sitcom Pilot and the Eleven Sections of a Series Format (Smith, ch. 20, p0291–0298)

### 9.1 The Nine Rules for a Pilot Script (p0295–0298, in the book's order)

First, remember what makes a pilot difficult: within "the length of one ordinary episode" you must introduce brand-new characters and settings, establish the time frame/humor style/theme/episode structure, tell a great and funny story, and prove it has legs—**all without letting anyone see you doing it, and without overwriting**.

1. **Watch other pilots**: find the pilots and pilot scripts of successful shows and break them down the way you would a spec target—**pay particular attention to how much exposition is delivered and how**, and identify the elements that "make you believe the show has legs."
2. **Study the market**: know what is in development around town and which kind of show appeals to which network; cable buys by demographic (age, gender, race, education). The book's judgment sentence: **"You might have the best new space-western concept ever conceived, but it's not likely that Lifetime is going to buy that pitch."** (The buyer landscape of 2009; the platform names need updating, but the method has not expired.)
3. **Decide between a premise pilot and a typical-episode pilot** (the book comes down clearly on one side): a premise pilot dramatizes "the event that creates the series premise" (a Martian/caveman/mermaid falls into someone's backyard and causes havoc; the ending is that the person lets it move in)—fun to write, because the premise's hook is the material itself; **the drawback is that it cannot show what an ordinary episode looks like** ("that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week"), and **it repeats poorly**. "**Therefore, most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot**"—a half hour "like the 17th, 52nd, or 89th episode of the series," with almost no exposition when meeting new characters and settings, "less is more as the pilot artfully reveals the minimum amount that we need to know."
   - A concrete sample: **Fleabag S1E1** is a pure typical-episode pilot—no "event" pushes the protagonist into a new situation; characters and relationships are laid out one by one, and each character's first appearance gets a single to-camera defining line (Waller-Bridge, *Fleabag: The Scriptures*, S1E1 notes; see §11).
4. **Character appeal comes first**: a weak structure or jokes that do not land are survivable, but **the protagonist must be fresh, relatable, appealing**—not necessarily likeable, kind, honest, or cheerful, but possessing drive, purpose, a soft spot, a unique skill, "or at least some small spark of passion." Three examples: Michael Scott (*The Office*) is a pompous and often cruel idiot, but he is Dunder Mifflin's top salesman and always wants to do the right thing (and then fails to); Earl (*My Name Is Earl*) is a lazy thief who has hurt so many people that he needs a list, but out of fear of karma he stubbornly makes amends; Cartman (*South Park*) is vicious and scheming and happiest when harming others, but "incredibly clever and persistent" in advancing his insane plans. The concluding sentence: **"in comedy, it's usually the troublemakers that we like best."**
5. **Be funny from page one**: "the reader will not cut you slack because this is the pilot, and she will not wait until page six 'when the comedy really kicks in!'"—the method is to **literally count the laughs in the opening scenes** and confirm you are not imagining them (density standard 2–4 per page, p0039/p0083).
6. **Proximity counts**: there is a reason so many sitcoms are set at home or at work—"you need your characters to keep bumping into each other so they can keep generating conflict and comedy in episode after episode"; you do not have to make another office/living-room show (single-camera can go anywhere), but **the premise must contain some location or dramatic device that regularly squeezes the characters together**.
7. **Keep it simple**: aim for a "dynamic but straightforward plot," with a few surprising turns, fresh humor, a satisfying ending, and perhaps **a little cliff-hanger to make people want episode 2**; introduce the protagonist and the main conflict early, and **limit and delay exposition as much as possible** ("We can learn about Cousin Billy and those nuts at the office another time").
   - A copyable pilot act-break demonstration (Landau 1e Tip 19, item 10, p0249–0294): **the pilot of *The Big Bang Theory***—the cold open ends with Sheldon and Leonard meeting their new neighbor Penny (her arrival changes their relationship forever); at the end of Act One, Wolowitz also meets Penny and falls for her, creating a competition and leaving "who will get Penny"; the end of Act Two is the dramatic resolution—Leonard helps Penny retrieve her television from her ex-boyfriend's apartment so that she can get closure; she is officially single, Leonard is still smitten, and the long-term will-they-or-won't-they is established. Landau's general rule: in a two-act sitcom the break between Acts One and Two is crucial—**something breaks the balance of the world, and Act Two is the effort to restore harmony** (because all sitcoms have happy endings); a three-act sitcom has two act breaks and therefore two mini-cliffhangers.
8. **Know your rights**: "**You can't sell what you don't own.**"—to adapt a book/film/comic/article/true story, **option or buy the rights before you write or pitch**; the first question producers and executives ask about an adaptation is always "Do you own the rights?", and if you cannot answer, "you'll look like an amateur and that contact will probably lose your number." When there is no money, you can sometimes negotiate a **free option** on material that is not commercially hot.
9. **Get readers** (once the concept is set or the first draft is finished; pay particular attention to the logical holes and the passages they do not understand); **register it with the Copyright Office or the WGA before you send it out**—"Unlike an ordinary spec, this is an entirely original work." (Compare p0152–153: **for a spec of an existing show, a registration number is actually not recommended**; you do not own the premise, characters, or sets, and marking it makes you look "insecure or inexperienced.")

### 9.2 The Eleven Sections of a Series Format Document (p0292–0295, transcribed section by section)

Definition: veteran writers sometimes pitch with a format instead of a pilot script—"a brief selling document that describes the series premise and hints at how future episodes would develop"; you can also bring both. **The length is usually 10–15 pages, with plenty of white space.**

| # | Section | Specifications and criteria from the book |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title page** | Series title, creator's name, the creator's rep's (or the creator's own) contact details; for the layout, see Appendix A |
| 2 | **Sell copy on the first interior page** (optional) | A one-line logline / the protagonist's personal creed / a statement or question expressing the theme / a quotation; the purpose is to make people want to hear more. **Many formats omit this section; done badly it looks cutesy** |
| 3 | **Series premise description, 1–3 pages** | Characters, relationships, setting, time frame, story type, **the source of continuing conflict**. "it must convince the reader that the show has **legs**, a premise rich enough to generate a hundred or more episodes down the road." One method is **to use the pilot story to carry this information** (comedy and drama "disguise" it). **In this section, introduce a character in a single phrase**; let her actions and dialogue define her |
| 4 | **Pilot story, 1–2 pages** | If the previous section did not use the pilot to do this. Use a real story to demonstrate the premise—"it's the best way to prove that the series will actually be funny," and at the same time prove that you can write the show |
| 5 | **8–10 loglines/springboards for future episodes** | Prove the show has legs and hook people with funny concepts; for a serialized show, write instead **several main story arcs for season one**, or mix the two (thread the arcs through the springboard list) |
| 6 | **Regular character list** | One paragraph per character, more for leads and less for supporting characters; **you must point out the key relationships that generate conflict and comedy (the character mix)**; detailed character descriptions belong here—do not stuff them back into section 3 (it will sink that section) |
| 7 | **Market analysis** (optional) | Recent ratings performance of comparable shows plus the shows currently competing for the same audience. "It's best to keep this section very short and objective"; include it only when the numbers strongly support it; **the book also records the opposing view—some argue for leaving it out entirely**; dullness dilutes the excitement of the creative part |
| 8 | **Budget summary** (optional, 1–2 pages) | Provided by an experienced producer, or a cost estimate for the pilot/first episodes; if there are money-saving production methods or co-financing arrangements, write them here |
| 9 | **Unique aspects** | A star already attached, based on published material (which you have optioned), a strong producer or financing partner. **Warning**: "Do not assume that this (used-to-be-a-star) actor or that (working-his-way-up) producer will help you to make a sale"—if the person is not currently hot, **attaching them will sink your project** |
| 10 | **Credits of the creator and attached names** | With no credentials and no famous names, **omit the entire section** |
| 11 | **Closing sell copy** (optional) | "a corny closer can kill a sale" |

Three overall limitations: ① this is only a blueprint; the order and combination can be adjusted for each project; ② **the format itself is a sample of your writing ability**—"keep those adjectives and adverbs at bay," and prove everywhere that the show is timely, unique, and **funny**, because "**you can't sell a sitcom with a document that reads like an annual report**"; ③ attachments (the article that inspired the show, ratings data, partners' letters of intent) do not count toward the 10–15 pages.

## 10. The Multi-Camera Production Week and the Cast & Set List (Three Books' Data Side by Side, Each Dated)

### 10.1 Smith's "Five Days Before Shooting" Model (*Writing Television Sitcoms*, p0203, **2009**)

| Day | Content |
|---|---|
| **DAY ONE** | **table reading**—producers, writers, cast, department heads, and network and studio executives attend (everyone except the executives sits around one big table or a ring of tables); the actors read through the draft to be shot that week → notes are given → the writers revise, and the director and departments begin preparing the physical elements. "If the script looks bad, the writers face a very long night." |
| **DAY TWO** | Usually another read-through and more notes; if the script is ready, the director begins designing camera positions and blocking, and there may be a full **run-through**; **some shows fix this day as rewrite night regardless of the state of the script** |
| **DAYS THREE AND FOUR** | The actors learn their lines in earnest and the script is locked; the director blocks the scenes and walks the camera positions; more notes, more revisions |
| **DAY FIVE (shooting day)** | Possibly two full run-throughs of the episode—first a formal **dress rehearsal**, then notes, final changes, perhaps a meal, then the **final shoot**; after that comes editing, **assembling the best parts of both takes into the finished product** |

The equipment and variables noted on the same page: multi-camera uses **3–5** cameras shooting the whole episode continuously (stopping only for retakes or scene changes); single-camera uses one camera over several days and from different angles; whether there is a live audience, whether the dress rehearsal is recorded as backup material, and whether canned laughter is added all vary by show. The rhythm is **one episode per week** (with the occasional week off to catch up), and the production season lasts **9–10 months**.

### 10.2 Blum's Sample One-Week Shooting Schedule (*Television and Screen Writing*, ch. 11, p0146–0147, **script dated 9–15 February 2000; book published 2001**)

Using *Becker*, "One Angry Man," as the example; the draft notes that "the following are only approximate times; the final exact times will be announced in advance each time":

| Day | Location | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **Wednesday (Day 1)** | Cooper Conference Room → Stage 31 | 11:30–12:00 **production meeting**; 12:00–13:00 **table read**; 13:00 **network notes**; from 13:00 **rehearsal** |
| **Thursday** | Stage 31 | 9:30–13:00 rehearsal; 13:00–13:30 lunch; 13:30 rehearsal; TBD **producers' run-through** |
| **Friday** | Stage 31 | 9:30–13:00 rehearsal; 13:00–13:30 lunch; 13:30 rehearsal; 14:00 **network run-through** |
| **Monday** | Stage 31 | 9:00–13:00 **camera blocking**; 13:00–14:00 lunch; 14:00 continue; TBD run-through |
| **Tuesday (shooting day)** | Stage 31 | 12:00–15:00 rehearsal; 15:00–16:00 producers' run-through; 16:00–19:00 dinner/hair/makeup/wardrobe; **19:00 official shoot in front of the audience** |

How to read it: **network notes arrive on table-read day → three straight days of rehearsal, each ending with two levels of run-through notes (producer level and network level) → Monday camera blocking → Tuesday night shoot before an audience.** This is the external clock that explains "why a sitcom script changes every day and why there are so few sets."

### 10.3 The Third Version: Two Real Week Schedules Recorded by Landau 1e (**2014**)

- ***How I Met Your Mother* (multi-camera, Bays/Thomas, Tip 21, p0294–0314)**: the room breaks story on the whiteboard (sometimes bringing in two or three rough beat-sheet pages first) → a writer goes out to write a rough outline (fixing only the shape) → brought back into the room to fill it in → goes out for **a week** to write the first draft → hands it in, and one of the two gives notes → a few days to write the second draft → back to the writers' room for a group rewrite → **Monday table read plus revisions; Tuesday run-through on stage plus more revisions; shooting begins at nine on Wednesday morning and runs through Friday.** And **the writer of that episode is always kept on stage** so that jokes can be changed live and alts tried. "We are always trying to beat the jokes, find the extra twist or surprise or a way to call something back that we never thought of until Tuesday night before shooting on Wednesday morning."
- ***Modern Family* (single-camera, Lloyd)**: the group breaks story for **three days** → cards go up on the board → only when everyone is sure the episode works does someone say "we can write it" → one person/a team goes out to write an outline → **deliberately let one or two weeks pass** before coming back to read it, with half a day for notes → the writer delivers the script two or three weeks later → the whole group rewrites for one or two days ("that should be just for refining jokes. You shouldn't be changing story at that point") → table read (about 100 people) → another one or two days of rewriting to the final draft. The single-camera labor comparison: **"we take eight or ten hours to shoot four minutes worth of a show, so we can stop and refine it"**; the multi-camera advantage at the opposite end is that the jokes have already been "tested" through a week of rehearsal, "but you can't stop."

**Remember the three as divergent; do not reconcile them**: the shooting day falls on **one night, Day 5** (Smith, 2009) / **one night, Tuesday** (Blum's *Becker*, that week in 2000) / **three days, Wednesday to Friday** (*HIMYM*, 2014, because it imposes a single-camera editing style on the multi-camera form; an episode often has 40–50 scenes, sometimes 80). Before choosing a template, confirm which kind the target show belongs to.

### 10.4 Page Count and Act-Length Parameters (Three Books Side by Side, **in Conflict**)

| Source (year) | Page count and allocation for a multi-camera/tape half hour |
|---|---|
| Smith, Appendix A (**2009**) | tape format **45–50 pages** (because dialogue is double-spaced); film format about **30 pages**; animation **40–50 pages** |
| Landau 1e, p0296 (**2014**) | Teaser **1–2**｜Act One **17–20**｜Act Two **17–20**｜Tag **1–3**｜**total 40–48 pages**; a single-camera sitcom is written in film format, **at least 30 pages on average** |
| Blum, ch. 11, p0137–0143 (**2001**) | multi-camera videotape script **40–50 pages**; examples: *Frasier* 2 acts, scenes A–H, **about 50 pages** (it does not write "The End"; it ends with "End of Act Two"); *Friends* teaser + 2 acts + tag, generally **26 scenes** (sometimes lettered A–Z), about **50 pages**; *Ally McBeal* 4 acts, scenes A–J, **50–55 pages** (hour-long but written in sitcom format, with double-spaced dialogue) |

**Act lengths (Blum, ch. 11, p0135, 2001)**: teaser **30–60 seconds**; Act One **10–12 minutes**; Act Two **10–12 minutes**; tag (button/coda) **no more than 1 minute**; the whole show **2–3 acts, each roughly equal in length**. —Note that this is compatible on paper with Landau's "Act One/Act Two 17–20 pages" (a multi-camera page ≠ one minute), but incompatible with film format's "one page ≈ one minute": **when you change formats, change the page-to-minute conversion rate as well.**

**Number of sets (the two books agree; this is the hardest production constraint in these notes)**: Blum p0143 records Dan Wilcox's experience—"**a show with three sets is ideal—one main set and two subordinate sets**. But he has also successfully used five sets in production"; Smith p0130–0133 records that multi-camera shows usually keep **3–5 standing sets plus one or two swing sets** (used occasionally, pushed out from the wings or pulled from storage), that **a spec should add at most one or two new sets**, and that a Hawaii/Italy episode is a sweeps-period staff stunt; putting one in a spec tells people you are "out of the loop or just not very bright."

### 10.5 The Cast & Set List: Field Table and Sample (Blum, ch. 11, p0143–0146, **2001**)

Purpose: once the script is shootable, the producer finalizes the cast & set list **to determine which characters each scene needs and how it should be set**. To save money, **group props into standing sets wherever possible**; otherwise the producer has to book a swing set or a new shooting location. **Explicit exception (the book marks it): "But if you're writing a script for a TV movie, this kind of list is useless."**

The sample is *Frasier*, "The Heat Is On":

| Field | Sample content |
|---|---|
| Header | **Series title / episode title / episode number** (#40570-163) |
| **CAST** | The leads are listed with **character name + actor name** (the leads are already set); **guest and other roles are also listed, but casting is not set** |
| **SETS** | Grouped as "interior/exterior/exterior-interior": interior—Frasier's living room/kitchen, hotel room; exterior/interior—van |
| **Act and scene breakdown** (for shooting) | **Act One/Act Two → scene letters (A, B, C, D, E, H) → scene heading (interior/exterior + location + time) → the characters who appear in the scene**; set changes within a scene are written as "switch sets to: … continue"; the margin gives **page numbers** (16)(17)(19)(24)(32)(36)(43) and **day/night codes such as "night/1"**; the end of each act is marked "End of Act One/End of Act Two" |

An episode's "production list" is therefore six fields: **act → scene → set → characters → page position → day/night.**

**Cross-source corroboration**: Blum p0137's multi-camera format rule 5 is "**the characters appearing should be noted before each scene begins**"; Landau 1e p0296's multi-camera format specification records the same thing—"**(CHARACTER LIST) immediately below the slug line, in parentheses, indicating which characters the scene requires**." Two books 13 years apart give the same rule, so it can be treated as a stable element of multi-camera format. (Note: Smith's Appendix A puts **the cast list on the "deliberately omitted from a spec" list**—it is a production-draft component. The three do not contradict one another: do not write it in a spec; hand it in once you are on staff.)

**Five limitations to read against this table**:

1. **The segment counts do not match the notes' own stated ranges; keep both side by side**: S1's format note says "cold open (1–3 minutes) → TITLES → **3–7 segments** → button", while S2's note says "cold open → TITLES → **3–5 segments** → button"; but the episode table counts **S1: 4/6/4/5/4/6, S2: 6/5/5/5/7/8**. That is, **the later S2 episodes clearly exceed the range S2 itself declares** (E5 has seven segments, E6 has eight), and the season finale is the longest. When using this table as a template, follow the actual counts, not the declared ones.
2. **The buttons for S2E2 and S2E3 are not listed separately in the notes**—the final segments of those two episodes close directly. Do not supply a button for them.
3. **Structural changes in S2 (notes §0, item by item)**: the time is set one year after S1; the engine restarts = Dad and Godmother get engaged, and the wedding falls in E6; the main flashback subject shifts from Boo to **the mother's funeral** (E4), and Boo is reduced to one- or two-second references (the script marks `FLASHBACK FROM SERIES ONE`); **every episode has a long monologue for a supporting character**, placed consistently around the climax (E3 Belinda's "menopause / People are all we've got"; E4 the Priest's vestments and "castrated himself"; E5 Anthony's "Hair. Is. Everything."; E6 Martin's "I am not a bad guy, I just have a bad personality." and the Priest's homily "Love is awful!… It feels like hope."); the objects all settle in E6—the statue (returned to Godmother → "**She was based on your mother.**" → she takes it away again), the fox (E3 setup → E6's last comic shot), the bassoon (Jake's piece is called "Where's Claire?"), Claire's hair-extension ponytail (cut badly in E5 → left on the seat in E6 as she runs for the airport), and the Bank Manager's hamster Stephanie.
4. **Bookend symmetry**: S2E1 (the engagement dinner at the restaurant) and S2E6 (the wedding, with everything except the final bus stop taking place in Dad's house and the side alley in front)—**the first and last episodes of a season are both family gatherings in an enclosed occasion**; S1's equivalent is the mother's death-anniversary lunch in E5 and the sexhibition in E6.
5. **anti-climax as an ending technique (S2E6's seven beats)**: love is spoken (three "I love you"s) → gently rejected ("It'll pass.") → returned ("I love you too. Ok.") → the bus reads "Cancelled" → the fox (pure comedy) → the statue (mother) → the audience is sent away (the device). **Not one of the seven beats is "she gets him," yet each beat brings her closer than the one before to "she can stand alone."** The device completes its full journey from defense → exposure → goodbye, which is also the structural reason "a third season does not need to exist" (Harry Bradbeer: "If she has something else to learn, then we have more story to tell").
