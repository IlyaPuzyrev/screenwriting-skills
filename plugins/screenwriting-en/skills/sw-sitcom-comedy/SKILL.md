# Half-Hour Comedy (Sitcom & Half-Hour Comedy)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; all terms anchor back to their originals — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md). Do not invent translations.

Core proposition: **Being funny is not something you stuff into the dialogue in post; it grows out of the story premise.** Comedy writers layer tension on three levels—premise → sequence/scene → dialogue/action; comedy planted in one layer will, in the next layer, "automatically produce more, bigger, and more seamless laughs" (Smith, Writing Television Sitcoms, p0042). So the first move is not to think up jokes but to ask, "Is this premise itself a joke-producing machine?"

Writing a half-hour show is not scaling down the parameters of an hour-long show; it is **switching the whole system**: page count, layout, scene count, joke density, structural units, and the operating logic of character relationships all change together. This skill governs that switch. It does not rewrite general methods—for dialogue see `sw-dialogue`, for scene value turns see `sw-scene-craft`, for hour-long act structure and act-out typology see `sw-series-structure`, for engines and bibles see `sw-series-engine-bible`, for the full picture of the writers' room see `sw-writers-room`, and for the episode-by-episode Fleabag table see `sw-series-case-studies`.

Format standards, the catalog of joke techniques, and structural templates are in [reference.md](reference.md) in the same directory.

## I. The Difference Between Half-Hour Comedy and the Hour-Long Show: The Whole-System Switch Table

| Dimension | Half-hour comedy | Hour-long show | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page count | film/single-cam **27–30**; tape/multi-cam **45–50**; animation **40–50** | 48–63 | Smith, Appendix A; Landau 1e p0296 |
| Acts | cold open + 2 acts (multi-camera) or 2–4 acts (single-camera) + tag | teaser + 4/5/6 acts | Landau 1e Tip 21 |
| Act length | teaser 30–60 seconds; each act 10–12 minutes; tag ≤1 minute | each act 10–15 minutes | Blum p0135 |
| First act | **extremely short (1–5 pages out of 30)**, with the characters and world already established | about 1/4 | Smith p0121 |
| Scene count | multi-camera: **15–20 scenes** per episode, 3–5 scenes per act, long scenes; single-camera has many short scenes (HIMYM often 40–50, sometimes 80) | depends on format | Landau 1e Tip 21 |
| Sets | **3 ideal (1 main + 2 secondary), 5 maximum** | far more | Blum p0143 |
| Structural unit | beat: main line 5–9, subplot 3–5, thread 3–6 each | scenes and act outs | Smith p0117–0119 |
| Joke density | **2–4 real laughs per page**; in multi-camera, almost every line of dialogue is expected to produce a joke | still needs a comic perspective | Smith p0039/p0083 |
| Stakes | small but crucial to the characters (**"tremendous trifle"**); "sitcoms are smaller" | life-and-death / career / institutions | Landau 1e p0298; Smith p0119 |
| Silence | in multi-camera, silence = dead air; in single-camera, silence can cringe "but in a good way" | silence is a resource | Landau 1e Tip 21 |
| Character growth | within an episode, "selfish → realization → learns a lesson... resets to zero next week"; over the long term it relies on **expansion**, not change | season-spanning arc | Smith p0139; Lloyd |

1. **The engine is relational density, not events.** Blum records producers being fussy about "the degree of intimacy between the characters," because that is the key to comedy that develops steadily (p0148). The first episode of Fleabag has no "event that pushes the protagonist into a new situation"; it simply spreads out a web of relationships in which people hurt one another.
2. **Multi-camera and single-camera are two different writing actions, not two layout styles.** Multi-camera is like a stage play: few scenes, long scenes, and you must constantly design characters' entrances and exits within the same set (Michael Patrick King considers **multi-camera harder**, because in single-camera "you could just CUT TO: the next scene"); single-camera is like "a little feature film every week," relying on fragmented scenes and cross-cutting, and leaning more on visual jokes. Some executives believe the two have "HUGE creative differences" and, when hiring for single-camera, look only at single-camera specs—**so keep both kinds of sample ready**.

## II. Premise-Driven Comedy: The Three-Level Method

Three sentences (Smith p0092–93): **① Weave the funny elements into the story premise; ② exploit and compound them at the sequence/scene level; ③ grow dialogue and action out of the humor inherent in the story. "It's all about building rather than repairing."**

### 2.1 Level One: The Four Comic Sources of a Premise

**Predicaments / Character Mix / Style of Comedy / Casting** (p0045–0061).

**A predicament is a joke-producing machine.** A predicament inside a scene only lasts a few pages; a continuing predicament woven into the premise keeps producing jokes until it is resolved, and **an unresolved predicament makes the audience watch in a state of expectation, so that even unrelated jokes seem funnier** (p0046). A permanent series predicament (the "no phone, no lights, no motorcar" of Gilligan's Island) is the first place to mine when looking for episode stories. The preconditions: the audience believes the characters will not really be hurt, and has already been signaled that "this is a comedy."

For the names, original examples, and usage limits of the **nine classic predicaments** and the **six character mixes**, see [reference.md](reference.md) §II.

### 2.2 "Every Show Is Its Own Genre"
Subcategories (domestic, odd couple) are not genres: Two and a Half Men and The Simpsons are both family shows but have completely different properties, and **each show's premise defines a set of properties the audience expects**. Before writing an existing show, answer the style questionnaire: does the humor come mainly from predicaments, character relationships, or the environment? Belly laughs or smiles-and-nods? Primarily visual or primarily verbal? Does it build to an explosive comedy run at the end, or is it even throughout?
**Two beginner mistakes**: **Too Funny to Pass Up** (however funny it is, throw out the idea that does not belong to this show); **The Genre Shuffle** ("blending genres throughout is not the same as shifting"). The ideal episode **"expands (but does not change) a show's premise."**

### 2.3 Level Two: Compounding at the Sequence and Scene Level

A sequence = a string of consecutive scenes carrying one continuous action; a scene = one place in one stretch of time, and a change of place or time makes a new scene. Three moves:
① **Compound Story Predicaments**—compound predicaments at the story level, so that tension "does not double but rises exponentially" (p0063); you can stack the same kind (Big Lie → Bigger Lies) or Mix and Match; predicaments at this level are "more finite, occur in real time," appearing, developing, and resolving within one continuous passage. ② **Stir Up the Character Mix**—temporarily form an "unnatural alliance," so the audience simultaneously waits for the story predicament to be resolved **and** for the alliance to collapse, with the original configuration restored at the end.
③ **Three reminders**: **Escalation** (build to a comedy climax at the end; many shows fix a raucous comedy block at the end of every episode); **Look to the Series Premise** (the best stories come from the protagonist's foibles); **Be Consistent** (check the joke strength of each passage before writing dialogue—by the time you are seduced by your own clever lines, it is too late).
Character fundamentals (Sheldon Bull): sitcom characters "have no self-edit button," act more impulsively, and have enormous reactions to problems ordinary people can shrug off.

## III. Joke Mechanics (Level Three)

The model: a **setup** (straight line) introduces a topic and implies some complication/incongruity → creates tension; the **punchline** resolves it with a turn in an unexpected direction → releases the tension. "Setups are the true unsung heroes" (p0068). Smith's caveat must be remembered alongside it: the greatest value of these terms lies in **editing and repairing, not creating**.

### 3.1 Five Rules for Setups

1. **Ordinary and believable**—first pull the audience in with a reasonable setup, then hit them with an unexpected punchline; the moment a setup looks odd, the audience knows there is an ambush.
2. **Two-part structure**: it must contain at least ① the topic + ② **one or two beats of development** (created by complication or incongruity). Without the second part, there is not enough tension and the punchline seems merely supplementary information. Smith's controlled comparison (cutting Jack's long dissection of Liz's life in the 30 Rock pilot down to one line) concluded that it was "still funny but not as funny, and it seemed more overtly hostile, untrue to the character" (p0071)—**the development section determines both the strength of the joke and the truth of the character**.
3. **It can be split and spread out**: the parts can be scattered across one scene or even planted several scenes earlier.
4. **No telegraphing**: "You brought a whole beer keg." → "Yeah. Where can I fill it up?"; add one more line, "Is that thing full?", and the joke dies.
5. **Life itself is half a setup**: shared everyday hardships and shared cultural experience can serve as the first part.

### 3.2 Six Rules for Punchlines

Definition: "a sudden twist that makes both sense and nonsense"—it resolves the setup, but comes from outside the direction the audience has been led to expect (p0077).

1. **Last things last**: put the punch word at the end of the sentence. "I think that he thinks it's a paper for people who work. Daily." is far better than "it's a daily paper for people who work." (For the same principle, see the periodic sentence in `sw-dialogue`.)
2. **Don't go past the joke**: the punchline usually also comes at the end of a speech; saying more after it tramples it. Exception: verbosity is this character's rhythm.
3. **Topper**: one setup yields two or three escalating punchlines ("Sex." → "It's illegal... Next best thing." → "Torture."). Three limits: **top at most twice** (any more and the audience notices); some jokes should be saved for later (to carry a dramatic moment, to control rhythm); **dramedy usually does not use toppers**.
4. **Running gag / callback**: a complete joke returns two or three times afterward; a new punchline that refers back to the original joke is a callback. Three methods: the same character retells it in a new situation / a different character retells it / a different character delivers a variation on the same setup. Each return carries a second layer of meaning, and **the last one is often ironic or inverted to land a big laugh**. Terminology note: a running gag is also called a runner, but runner also often means "a very small subplot," which is not necessarily funny.
5. **If you are stuck, go back and change the setup** (swap in a different incongruity or increase the contrast)—not keep grinding on the punchline.
6. **Go for Literal**: take the literal meaning ("Call me a taxi." / "Okay, you're a taxi."). Literal jokes mostly come from **very stupid or very smart** characters.

### 3.4 Character-Specific Ways of Being Funny
Ten comedic nuances: Understated Response / Exaggerated Response / Irony / Nervous Babbling / Pulls No Punches / Space-Shot POV / Wordplay / Double Entendre (+ two emotion-driven variants). Each is **a character's fixed way of being funny**, not the writer's bag of tricks: **before writing dialogue, decide which one or two this character uses, and give him no others.**

### 3.5 Five General Principles

- **Hanging a lantern on it**: have a character shout "That's crazy!" to get an outrageous premise past the audience (p0178). Implausible things must be foreshadowed first, and **if you plant a seed, you must harvest it**.
- **Bigger Really Is Better**: don't tell a neighbor—broadcast to the whole country that your boss is bald; don't offend one colleague—give the entire office a lecture on diversity. As long as it does not violate the character's nature and the show's premise, **think bigger**.
- **Small Truths, Big Laughs**: "Surf's up, Jim. Watch me ride this wave of inspiration! (GRINS, INTO PHONE) Yes, I'll hold."
- **Reiss's three conditions**: every joke must simultaneously **be funny, serve the story, and not make the characters unlikable**; and even in the hardest spot there is always a joke to be found there—"you may have to look for two hours, but you will find it."

### 3.6 Density Standard

**2–4 real laughs per page** (p0039/p0083). The way to enforce this is not to estimate but to **count**: circle the genuine laughs in red ink and fix it if you fall short; count the opening scenes one by one—readers will not be lenient just because it is a pilot, nor will they wait until page six for "the comedy to really start" (p0297). At the other extreme: no producer will ever say "this script is too funny," but it must not be **too jokey** either—**the amount and type of jokes must be stable from beginning to end**. Audience response comes in three grades: **smile / chuckle / laugh** (physical comedy produces a laugh; witty dialogue produces a smile). **Toppers and running gags are the most economical way to raise the joke count.**

## IV. Half-Hour Structure

### 4.1 The Beat Sheet (One Page)

**5–9 beats for the main line; 3–5 for each subplot; 3–6 for each line of a thread structure (the Seinfeld-style four equal-weight lines).** A beat = a moment/discovery/event that changes the protagonist's goal or raises the tension, often in two halves of "action → reaction," compressed into one sentence: "Character One blabs secret—claims (new lie) to cover up." List multiple lines separately first, then weave them; **start every beat with the name of the central character** and then rewrite it as his reaction (Ellen Sandler). If the story on this page does not build, does not make sense, is not funny, and has no surprises, go back to the drawing board. Terminology flag: in drama-series writers' rooms, "beat sheet" instead means a multi-page outline of numbered scenes written in telegraphic style.

### 4.2 Three Acts and Page Counts for a Single Main Line

Typical path: the protagonist encounters a new problem/opportunity → pursues it by **outlandish** means (not reasonable ones, or there would be no story) → obstacles escalate and the goal becomes more urgent → at the end it backfires, exposes him, humiliates him, and he "learns the error of his ways. Sort of."
**Act One is extremely short** (the inciting incident arrives within one or two beats); **Act Two is the longest** (1/2–2/3 of the script, "think development, not repetition," often ending with a big revelation plus a major decision); **Act Three is about 1/4** (acceleration, rise to the climax, confrontation, truth, resolution).
**Page counts (film format, 30 pages, single main line): 1–5 pages / 15–20 pages / 5–10 pages.** In a thread show, each of the four lines gets about 1/4 (7–8 pages), and it is impossible to predict on which page the acts will fall.

> ⚠️ **This set of page counts and the "10–12 minutes per act" in the switch table are two mutually exclusive schemes, and their demands on Act One differ by a factor of four (1–5 minutes vs. 10–12 minutes). Selection rule: first decide the number of acts.**
> - **Two acts** (cold open + 2 acts + tag, common in multi-camera): 10–12 minutes per act; Act One is not short.
> - **Three acts, single main line** (film format, 30 pages): 1–5 / 15–20 / 5–10 pages; Act One is extremely short because the characters and world are already established.
> - **Pilot exception**: episode 1 must establish the world and characters, so Act One will necessarily be longer than 1–5 pages; **do not fail it on this criterion**.
> Diagnostic item ⑦ applies only to non-pilot episodes with a three-act single main line.

### 4.4 Dramatic Structure vs. Broadcast Format

"Act" has two meanings: an act in dramatic structure vs. a segment between commercials. **Commercial breaks do not necessarily line up with dramatic act turns**—the commercial that comes after Act One in the broadcast sense may fall in the middle of Act Two in the dramatic sense. The `ACT ONE, ACT TWO` you type in an outline/script is broadcast format. Method: beat sheet first, then in the outline move the key plot points to just before the show's usual commercial positions; it is fine if they do not all align, but amplify a beat where you can—**a newcomer who writes a strong act break in a spec earns major points**.

### 4.6 The Key Dramatic Moment and the Block Comedy Scene

Matt Williams: every episode must build toward a **key dramatic moment** (a decision / discovery / reconciliation); everything moves toward it and then away from it, and **nine times out of ten it falls inside the block comedy scene** (p0126). Corollary: decide on this moment first, then decide which scene it falls in; that scene must also be the episode's biggest comedy passage.

## V. Characters: Who Deserves to Be Put in This Machine

- **They do not have to be likable, but they must have drive, purpose, a soft spot, and a distinctive skill**—at minimum "some small spark of passion" (p0296). Michael Scott is a pompous and often cruel idiot, but he is the company's top salesman and always wants to do the right thing (and then fails to). **"In comedy, it's usually the troublemakers that we like best."**
- **Proximity counts**: the premise **must contain some setting or dramatic device that regularly squeezes the characters together**, so they keep colliding episode after episode. You do not have to make another office/living-room show (single-camera allows roaming), but this device is indispensable.
- **Conflict must occur among the regular characters**: when two series regulars argue over "which way the toilet paper should hang," the audience will watch.
- **Complementary traits** (Miller): **situations are not funny; characters are**—an incubator, an apartment, a hospital, a restaurant are none of them funny in themselves. The traditional route is the Aristotelian "imitation of men worse than the average" (It's Always Sunny, Curb); the contemporary softening trend is **complementary character traits** (flawed but well-meaning), which are more relatable. Three reproducible practices from the character chart in the Modern Family pilot: **describe everyone in positive terms; any two of them paired together can produce jokes; each person's point of view has its own validity.**
- **Ten behavioral character types**: Supportive Parent / Idiot Savant / Idiot Idiot / Clown / Operator / Mentor / Confidant / Irritant / Romantic Interest / Critic. The caveat matters: these are **temporary behavior patterns**, not fixed labels—Frasier is a supportive parent to his father this week and a childlike idiot the next.
- **Supporting-character naming** (Fleabag): name them by **one trait** and never change it (Arsehole Guy, Bus Rodent, Bank Manager, Godmother), while the protagonist has no name. The naming itself must be the protagonist's ability/defect—all her life she can reduce people to "a bus rodent" or "an arsehole guy"—so a later episode must make her pay for it (the Priest = the first person she cannot label).

## VII. Direct Address and Device Comedy (Fleabag)

Direct address (`(to camera)`) **is not a style but the character's psychological defense**: she wakes up one day to find an audience watching her, so she does the only thing she can—"she put on a show" ("Who is Fleabag?"). This definition is what allows it to become plot in Season 2. Three basic uses: narration-style exposition ("You know that feeling when...") / a one-line inner annotation in the middle of a conversation (say A, turn to us and say B, turn back and say C) / a silent glance ("She looks at the camera." as a reaction shot).

1. **Test the device for feasibility before writing the script.** The adaptation problem went unsolved for a year, until Vicky Jones filmed her with a phone talking to camera in the kitchen for five minutes, put the phone down, and said: "It works."—**treat the device as a testable hypothesis, not a writing decision.**
2. **A triple, with the fourth beat overturned by someone else.** Give the adjective first, then the evidence, and **the evidence is always one notch lower than the adjective**: "He's a feminist." / "I have a sister."; or "don't say it—don't say it—said it" (three turns of the head within five beats). **Give the power to overturn to the other character, not the protagonist.**
3. **She tells only jokes to the camera and the truth to people.** The season gives her one and only one long monologue **not** to camera (in the confessional), and that is the series' emotional peak.
4. **The device itself is the season arc.** In Season 1 it is an accomplice; the season finale delivers the first reversal (she hides from the camera, the camera chases her into a corner and forces out a flashback); Season 2 lets **a character see it** and escalates step by step: discovery ("Where did you just go?") → challenge ("it's like you disappear") → an order to stop → she pushes it away → she asks it not to follow (the final line is a stage direction addressed to the camera: "Goodbye."). **Once the device has walked this road, the show is over.**
5. **Visual sentences**: write the trauma as a 2–4 second image (Boo standing across the road as traffic sweeps past) and insert it at a moment when the protagonist "seems fine" (laughing, having sex, walking down the street), so the audience knows at once that she is not—do not have the character recount it. When the director supplied this image, he said, "that was all we needed."

**The standard beat for an emotional scene is "pain—laugh—pain"**: truth → a visual joke or a bitchy line → back to truth. Two accompanying rules: **let the protagonist lose the argument** (after her impassioned speech, a prop proves on the spot that she is on the wrong side, and then two lines from a supporting character escalate the theme); **the season-finale confession may be said three times, and the answer gets only two words**, followed by a seven-beat anti-climax ending (confession → gentle rejection → the other person gives something back → an everyday misfortune → a purely comic prop → an object connected to the dead → the device's farewell).

## VIII. Dramedy and "Comedy Carrying Tragedy"

**The criteria for dramedy (Douglas)**: in the 1960s, American networks institutionalized "half-hour = sitcom, hour = drama," and to this day offices are physically divided into comedy and drama sides, with a pitch allowed to go to only one VP. The properties of dramedy are continuous characters and storylines, backstory depth, and a dramatic arc, **not setups/jokes at fixed intervals**; the laughter is wry/ironic. Two rules: **nothing longer than three minutes can succeed on joke-to-joke writing**; and **apart from children's adventure, no one can write a drama with no comic perspective throughout—episodes heading toward tragedy especially need comedy** (Shakespeare puts a clown before the most tragic scene). Douglas's lesson: Frank's Place was forced to become a pure comedy because audiences found it "not funny enough"—**audience expectations are set by the time slot and format, and a dramedy must actively tell the audience what to expect**.

**Six practices from Succession**:

- **Use a joke to catch the darkest confession.** After Kendall says he ran over and killed the waiter, Roman uses jokes to downgrade the charge step by step (murderer → irresponsibler → "at worst you're a manslaughter-er"), and Shiv follows with "Oh yeah I've killed a coupla kids. Sure. Just little ones."; the script notes to itself, **"It is so dark that it is kind of funny."** The mechanism: **acceptance does not arrive as comfort but as a cruel joke inside the family, because that is the only love language this family speaks.**
- **A comic subplot is a thermostat, not a dilutant.** Greg's Luxembourg throne line keeps cutting in between the darkest passages—and in the end it is precisely this line that forces Tom into his decision. **Criterion: a comic subplot must also carry a plot function, or it is padding.**
- **Give the finale a regressive game with no plot advancement** (the kitchen "meal fit for a king"): for the first and last time, everyone shares a single intention, and **the jokes must be cooperative**; let the one line that wants to express warmth be interrupted—that unfinished pause is the foreshadowing of the ending.
- **The ordering rule**: the most powerful proposal in the room is always "an appeal to reality rather than myth," **and it only occasionally loses to the funniest one**.

## IX. The Sitcom Pilot and the Series Format

**By default, write a typical-episode pilot.** A premise pilot dramatizes "the event that creates the show's premise"; the hook itself is the material, but **it cannot demonstrate what a normal episode looks like** ("that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week"), and it repeats poorly. So **most network executives prefer a typical-episode pilot**—a half hour "just like episode 17, 52, or 89 of this show," with almost no exposition when a new character appears (p0296). The first episode of Fleabag is a typical example.

The most actionable of the nine practices: **be funny on page one and literally count the jokes in the opening scenes**; **character appeal comes first** (the protagonist must be fresh, relatable, appealing); **proximity counts**; **keep it simple and delay exposition as long as possible**; **Know your rights**—"You can't sell what you don't own"; option any source material before writing it; register the copyright before sending it out.

**Three criteria for the series-format document (10–15 pages, eleven sections)**: it must prove **legs** (the premise is rich enough to generate a hundred-plus episodes); **the premise section introduces a character in a single phrase**, letting action and dialogue define her; **the format itself is a writing sample**—a document that reads like an annual report will not sell comedy.
**Pitch execution rules**: when pitching an episode, bring **10–15 springboards** (3–5 sentences each, and tell the short version first); **when pitching a new show, bring only the one big idea plus one or two backups**, and stop pitching backups the moment the big idea is accepted. And **"Try to pitch funny—you're supposed to be a comedy writer."** For the general pitch document and the three tiers of the bible, see `sw-series-engine-bible`.

## X. The Comedy Writers' Room

**Basic division of labor**: break story collectively → write the draft individually → punch up collectively. **Roundtable writing** (tabling): 5–15 people go line by line, revising and inserting jokes, with a senior producer adjudicating; some shows hire a **punch-up person**. It is not unusual for a script to be revised more than five times, and **many writers consider it lucky if half the original dialogue survives into the shooting script**. For process and job titles, see `sw-writers-room`.

**Alts and the candy bag** (Landau 2e ch13): the draft is dissected collectively and new lines and jokes are pitched; the new lines are called **alts / alternates**. Schur's numbers: **the script swells to 50–60 pages with the alts, then is cut back to the mandated 30 pages.** Discarded jokes are stored for future episodes; Greg Daniels calls this **"the candy bag"** (Michael Patrick King's version is "the whipped cream"). **But do not hoard the good stuff**—Schur: "loyal viewers are a rare commodity"; Daniels's version is **"Pack the sausage; put it all in."** The compromise: **structural big ideas must be burned; jokes can be saved.**
**On-set alts** (the Succession version, generally applicable): a day or two before shooting, pick out 0–15 lines that might be "funnier or better or truer," send them to 3–5 writers to solicit alts, whittle 10–20 options down to 5–10 to keep in your pocket, and after the scripted version is shot, feed them to the actors as appropriate.

| Room model | Method | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Parks & Rec (Schur) | Break collectively, each writes his or her own episode; writing whole episodes collectively falls into "diminishing marginal returns" | Ensemble shows with similar voices |
| Mindy Kaling | Index cards + whiteboard; early in the season, pitch three or four "story area documents" to the platform for approval; assign one or two people per episode to write the draft (aiming for a "wonderful patchwork of voices"); once the draft comes in, the script becomes common property of the whole room | Wanting to preserve multiple voices |
| Insecure (Issa Rae) | Write every episode collectively, **and read it aloud in the room with writers/producers taking roles** (before the table read with the actors) | Auteur shows with a unified voice |
| Schitt's Creek (Dan Levy) | A small room of four to six people, together almost the whole time, strict A/B/C; **scripts are divided only after the beats have been written as dialogue** | Small budgets, precise tonal requirements |

**"Writing the beats as dialogue" deserves its own note.** When beats stop at the level of "someone gets caught, needs to escape the embarrassment, and is finally neutralized by someone else's embarrassment," no one asks, **"What is this embarrassment? How does it serve this character right now? Can this piece of physical comedy also carry an emotional function?"**—the consequence is that the line feels strange throughout the episode. The solution: once the beats are set, **go back to the beginning and write all the teaser's dialogue to verify that it holds up**.

**Comedy can be written collectively; drama cannot** (Bays): the room will overhaul comedy, but "dramatic moments I try not to touch or punch in the room—drama has to be personal; it cannot be written by committee."

## XI. Divergence Table (Which Approach When)

| Issue | Side A | Side B | How to choose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can comedy be taught? | Landau: structure, subtext, and dialogue can be taught, but **being funny itself is a gift**—"no one can teach you how to be funny," and only the conventions can be taught | In Chapter 13 of the same book, four showrunners offer entirely teachable processes (a backstory questionnaire, breaking from emotion, alts/candy bag, writing beats as dialogue, in-room read-throughs) | **Process can be taught; comic taste can only be calibrated**; use process to raise the floor |
| Collective writing vs. individual drafts | Schur: writing whole episodes collectively hits diminishing returns; Kaling: individual drafts aim for a "patchwork of voices" | Issa Rae: write every episode collectively + in-room read-through; Dan Levy: a small room together throughout | Decide by **the show's voice**: a single authorial voice → collective break + individual writing; an ensemble with many voices → can be fully collective, but someone must adjudicate |
| The function of the table read | Smith / Landau / Lloyd: test the jokes and the rhythm, and revise based on it | Dan Levy: test structure only, **not jokes** | If the jokes are in the dialogue → test the jokes; if they are in reactions/physicality/editing → test structure only |
| Should every act end on a cliffhanger? | Smith: end every act on a cliff-hanger; a strong act break earns a spec extra points | Dan Levy: almost never ends on a cliffhanger and does not play will-they-won't-they—"It's emotional stakes" | With commercial positions → an act out is mandatory; no commercials + emotional-stakes type → an "earned moment" can substitute | ⚠️ **Note when writing Chinese-language series**: the comparison table in `sw-chinese-series-practice` requires that "every episode must sell one more big 关子 (guānzi — a suspense hook at the end of a segment)" at the end, which runs opposite to this row's "an earned emotional moment can substitute." ## XII. Diagnostic Checklist

**Premise level** ① Is this premise itself a joke-producing machine? If it is only funny when you force wordplay into the dialogue, go back and change the premise. ② Is the predicament continuing or does it only last a few pages? When is it resolved? ③ In the character mix, are there two people contributing the same thing? ④ Does this episode "expand but not change" the series premise? Is there a Too Funny to Pass Up or a Genre Shuffle? ⑤ Does the premise contain a device that repeatedly squeezes the characters together?

**Structure level** ⑥ Does the main line have 5–9 beats and each subplot 3–5? Does every beat change the goal or raise the tension? ⑦ Is Act One too long (it should be 1–5 pages out of 30)? **Applies only to non-pilot episodes with a three-act single main line**; two-act shows and pilots do not run this check—see the selection rules in Section IV. ⑧ Which moment is this episode's key dramatic moment? Does it fall inside the block comedy scene? ⑨ Is the predicament escalating toward the end? ⑩ Is the strong act break written? Does each act end on a joke or a new plot point? ⑪ Is each line headed by a different character? Is the protagonist driving the story, or being led around by the visitors? ⑫ Do scenes start late / finish early? Is there a button? ⑬ Are there more than 2 new sets, or more than 5 sets in total?

**Joke level** **This item is the standing-set economics of multi-camera stage shooting** (standing sets must be built and occupy the stage long-term); **single-camera location shows do not count by it**: instead count **the number of shooting locations and company moves**—the lobby, showroom, office, stockroom, and backyard in the same building are one location, not five sets. The corresponding criterion for single-camera is "does this episode need to go into a new location for a single scene?"
⑭ **Circle it in red: are there 2–4 real laughs on this page? Are there any in the first three pages?** ⑮ Does every setup have a second-part development? Does it telegraph the punchline? ⑯ Is the punch word at the end of the sentence? Do you stop after saying it? ⑰ Has anything been topped more than twice? Is a topper used in a dramedy? ⑱ Does the running gag return 2–3 times, with a reversal on the last one? Are all planted seeds harvested? ⑲ Is there a punchline you simply cannot write? → Change the setup, or go for the literal meaning. ⑳ Does every joke satisfy Reiss's three conditions simultaneously? ㉑ Is the character using his own one or two comedic nuances? If you cover the names, can you tell who is speaking? ㉒ Are the amount and type of jokes consistent from beginning to end? ㉓ Is there a scene that should have used a take / stage business / visual joke but was written as dialogue instead?

**Format level** ㉔ Is this show film or tape? Is the page count right (27–30 / 45–50 / 40–50)? Are the capitalization of stage directions, dialogue line spacing, scene numbering, and entrance/exit underlining all done according to that system? ㉕ Has the spec slipped in scene numbers / cast list / sound slug / camera directions / "First Draft" / a date? Are there too many dialogue cues?

**Tonal level** ㉖ If you are writing dramedy: is the laughter wry/ironic, or has it turned joke-to-joke? ㉗ Is there comedy in the episode heading toward tragedy? After the darkest confession, does someone catch it with a joke? ㉘ Apart from regulating temperature, what plot function does the comic subplot carry? (Cut the ones that only regulate temperature.) ㉙ How many things does this plotline accomplish at once? **Cut anything that does fewer than two.**

## XIII. Workflow: From Zero to Second Draft

**0 Choose a show and research it** (when writing a spec): choose a show you genuinely like, that has aired at least one season and will air again; **a spec written for SHOW X is meant to be submitted to other shows**. **Reading scripts matters more than watching the show**—paper and screen are not the same thing, and your work is on paper. Make Smith's analysis chart: the scene's starting page number / a one-line scene identifier / vertical columns marked A, B, C to record each line's beats / **underline the last scene before each commercial** to mark the act break.

**1 Generate ideas** Start from the protagonist's emotions and goals and ask "What if" ("Dwight and Jim hate each other—what if they have to share a room on a business trip?"). **What counts is emotion, not situation.** When filtering, check **lag time** (will the topical reference still hold four months later?), do not write celebrity cameos, and avoid holiday episodes.

**2 Springboard (3–5 sentences)** The first sentence or two give the hook; the next few imply funny complications; **you do not necessarily give the ending** ("if you want to hear the ending, you can pay me to write it"); and it **must be written in funny language**. Test: if you cannot make the essence clear in a few lines, the concept is too muddy—and a muddy concept will not clear itself up.

**3 Beat sheet (one page)** Generate it according to the beat counts in 4.1; list multiple lines separately first, then weave them. Self-check: does it build? Does it make sense? Is it funny? Does it surprise?

**4 Add comedy at the premise level** Ask of the beat sheet: which kind of predicament is this (or which several kinds compounded)? At which beat is the character mix stirred? Is the style of comedy consistent with the show? **If you cannot answer, do not write on.**

**5 Outline (5–7 pages single-spaced, or 8–12 pages double-spaced according to the show's custom)** **"An outline is a selling tool."** Ask of every detail, "Does the reader absolutely need to know this to follow the story?" If not, cut it—**the mark of a beginner is eagerness to educate the reader**. Lay it into broadcast format and move important plot points before the breaks. At least one storyline must hook the reader within two or three pages; keep every scene description brief and **include the jokes that will be seen**; **Play It, Don't Say It**; "this will be funny here" does not count; dialogue snippets are very short and must be excellent (do not use up all your jokes).
Use Matt Williams's warning as a diagnostic standard: **nine times out of ten, an outline collapses at the two-thirds mark**—it can set up and complicate, but by two-thirds it does not know what the episode is about. So **after finishing the outline, turn first to the two-thirds mark and ask, "What is this episode about?"**

**6 First draft (about 1–2 weeks for 30 pages)** Do not polish page one on the first day—"Blair through the script," writing two or three scenes a day or even the whole first act. **Before a major rewrite, go back and redo the beat sheet and outline.** The first thing to do after finishing is **count the jokes in red ink**.

**7 Revision (the priority order cannot be reversed)** **story → sequences → scenes → moments → dialogue, watching the comedy at every level**; reverse the order and it is "a bad patch job." When listening to feedback, **do not speak**—if you have to defend a creative decision, it probably needs another look. Handling notes: **a vague note usually points at the wrong place** (the real problem behind "the basement scene is not funny" may be that the predicament was not established three scenes earlier); what you hand back must reflect what was discussed in the room; **the greatest sin in a second draft is laziness**.
For the freelance delivery timetable for each stage (verbal pitch / outline / revised outline / first draft / second draft), see [reference.md](reference.md) §V.

## XIV. Training

1. **Count jokes**: take a real episode script, circle the real laughs page by page, calculate the density, and mark which category each one belongs to (setup-punchline / topper / running gag / take / stage business / visual / sound). Then do the same with your own draft.
2. **Change the setup**: pick the three weakest punchlines, **do not change the punchline, only the setup**, and see whether each can grow six new punchlines.
7. **Device test**: for any unconventional narrative device, first shoot five minutes of yourself performing it on a phone, confirm that "it works," and then start writing.
10. **Punch-up simulation**: write 10–20 alts for someone else's scene, then whittle them down to 5–10 yourself; whatever gets cut goes into your candy bag.
