# Japanese Screenwriting Methods (日本编剧方法)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

A hands-on route into writing original Japanese screenplays, drawn from 《从零开始做编剧：10 位日本金牌导演、编剧谈剧本》 (Scriptwriting from Zero: Ten Japanese Directors and Screenwriters on the Screenplay), edited by 泊贵洋 (Tomari Takahiro), and the 荒井晴彦 (Arai Haruhiko) lecture in 《故事创作大师班·国际卷》 (Story Creation Masterclass: International Volume). For the Korean (吴胜郁, Oh Seung-wook; 崔石焕, Choi Seok-hwan) and French (奥比坦, Frédéric Auburtin) screenwriters from the same masterclass, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`. Relation to the Hollywood system: **古泽良太 (Kosawa Ryota), 内田贤治 (Uchida Kenji), and 筧昌也 (Kakei Masaya) accept that the three-act / 起承转合 shape is the most effective structure, but ask "why good works are three-act"; 三木聪 (Miki Satoshi), 园子温 (Sono Sion), 横浜聪子 (Yokohama Satoko), 高桥泉 (Takahashi Izumi), and 福田雄一 (Fukuda Yuichi) take another road — theme deferred, no plot outline, fragments joined together, character before event; 行定勋 (Yukisada Isao) treats the script as an "axis" and "material for argument."** For 小津安二郎 (Ozu Yasujiro)'s script examples, see `ozu-screenplay-style`; the editor's closing words: "Do not rely on adapted scripts — win with originals." Worksheets and plot-outline examples are in [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. Two Camps: Structure-First vs. Character/Fragment-First

| | Structure-first camp | Character/fragment-first camp |
|---|---|---|
| Representatives | Kosawa Ryota, Uchida Kenji, Kakei Masaya | Miki Satoshi, Sono Sion, Yokohama Satoko, Takahashi Izumi, Fukuda Yuichi, Omiya Ellie |
| Starting point | Subject-matter compass, premise, "if + and moreover," ending | Small-material notebook, actor, character résumé/diary, personal experience |
| Theme | Fixed before writing or brought out by the material | "Emerge unintentionally" when the script is 70–80% done (Rumoi waits for herring) |
| Plot outline | Always written; first draw a framework/table/A4 whole-series structure | Not written, or minimal ("once I write a plot outline, the script loses its explosive power") |
| Cost | Writing to structure is not necessarily interesting | Being unable to think of links between plot points "is literally hell" |

The editor: "The humanities have their way and the sciences have theirs; the right-brain camp relies on the right brain, the left-brain camp on the left brain." Choose the road that suits you, but both camps agree: **write one first and talk later** (Miki: "Turn the toy box upside down — write one first!"); **show it to others; being told "it's boring" is what makes it worthwhile** (Sono Sion / Fukuda).

## II. Structure and Pace

- **Three acts = 起承转合** (Kosawa; qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé — the four-part opening–development–turn–resolution pattern): *qi* (起) clarifies the character relationships; *cheng* (承) presents the conflicts (**if the "cheng" is exciting, you can write a good script; the more "cheng," the more changes**); *zhuan* (转) and *he* (合) are merged into the climax. His study method was to copy out the script of 《七武士》 (Seven Samurai) and to reverse-engineer TV recordings into scripts.
- **The moment the protagonist fixes a goal = the plot point** (Uchida): in 《E.T.》 (E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial), hiding E.T. in the house; in 《虎胆龙威》 (Die Hard), picking up the machine gun. Think in four 30-minute blocks. His university teacher: "You must not mistakenly believe that writing to a three-act structure will produce a good work; what we must investigate is why good works have a three-act structure."
- **Fix the ending first** (Uchida): "arriving at the airport without having thought about the destination"; Sono Sion's 《爱的曝光》 (Love Exposure) also began with two scenes (persuading his sister by the sea; the sister coming to rescue him from the psychiatric hospital) and worked backward to the first half.
- **A sense of speed and jumping** (Kosawa): several stories develop in intercut, constantly changing shots to create speed; **a boring scene = a scene without a jump**, so cut it or add a new idea to make it matter; "skip two or three levels at once"; make the next scene the opposite of the previous one; let each line of dialogue negate the one before; swing the plot left and right (influenced by 《LOST》 and 《福音战士》 (Neon Genesis Evangelion)).
- **End the exposition as early as possible** (Miki): "In my work the exposition passes very quickly"; he uses "Milo" chocolate milk to set up "Instant Swamp," and opens with a two-and-a-half-minute monologue in 108 shots.
- **Idling and fanning** (Miki): a Gilliam / 《双峰》 (Twin Peaks)-style "idling while fanning"; "a film is more interesting with more seemingly useless idling."
- **The ten-minute rule** (Fukuda / Hazumi (羽住)): in a film something must happen within 20 minutes, in a TV drama within 10, so the audience knows in advance what this episode will be about.
- **TV drama practice** (Miki / Kosawa): one block (up to the commercial break) runs about 10 minutes; if there are seven things in 10 minutes, list seven outline items and mark the commercial breaks; lay out the structure of the entire series on a single A4 sheet before writing (a setup in episode 2 pays off in episode 5); an ensemble drama in which one case runs through the whole series (《怪胎刑警》 (Gonzo: Legendary Detective)); first draw the framework of "the order in which things must be presented in advance"; fixed bits make a show easy to watch (Director Kinoshita's ramen reception in every episode).
- **The protagonist need not have a goal / need not grow** (Miki): "Must people always improve? A fool is a fool from beginning to end, and the fact that he does not grow is what makes him interesting"; 《废柴三人组》 (Damejin) is "a lifetime of summer vacation"; 《乌龟意外之速游》 (Turtles Are Surprisingly Fast Swimmers) is built on 《原野奇侠》 (Shane) (the departure of the spy friend) — "a story in which nothing happens."
- **Anti-theory** (Yokohama): "A sense of imbalance should be more interesting"; "if you write according to theory, you cannot produce anything beyond other people's imagination"; the cost is that connections are extremely difficult — show the concept to the producer first.
- **Deviation within repeated daily life** (Yukisada): the Ozu-style "since we have come here, start shooting from here and then repeat it"; the cherry-blossom fork in the road, changing course because of another person — "the most secret savor of a script"; "what I want to depict first is the richness that is not found in the repetition of daily life."

## III. Material and Conception

- **One note a day** (Miki / Yokohama / Kakei): record what is interesting or catches your attention; after six months and 180 notes, look back and see what has been drawing you; sketch character portraits plus keywords in a sketchbook (Kosawa); use an outliner (Acta / Tree) to list and reorganize notes into chapters; "turn the toy box upside down," pour out all the material, rearrange it, and find the hidden theme; Fukuda is the opposite: "Rather than taking notes, it is better to engrave things in your memory" — jokes are a game of memory.
- **"If" + "and moreover"** (Kakei): a concept = the addition of two things far apart — "beauty" + "a can," "football commentary" + "injury time before death," "swapping rooms" + "the previous tenant's lover" + "a professional-wrestler moving crew," "men" + "synchronized swimming" (《水男孩》 (Waterboys)); **"and moreover" makes a coincidence acceptable; "and then" is a letdown**; "if" alone is not enough — mixing in a different content is the entry point. Fukuda: "You must have two pieces of material that trigger inspiration; with only one, something feels missing."
- **Start from the title** (Miki / Kosawa): a title should make people ask "what is this about?" (《速成沼泽》 (Instant Swamp), 《爆裂城市》 (Burst City), 《铁男》 (Tetsuo: The Iron Man)); "A 60-Year-Old's Love Letter" is, by title alone, a brand-new love story; a familiar form + a brand-new approach = "**pseudo-déjà vu**" (Miki); Sono Sion: first tell the synopsis to others — if there is no reaction, give up; when he said the titles 《循环自杀》 (Suicide Club) and 《爱的曝光》 (Love Exposure), people laughed at once.
- **Analyze "what it is mainly about"** (Miki): 《原野奇侠》 (Shane) = "the person you meet leaves, and you return to ordinary life" → 《乌龟》 (Turtles); 《教父》 (The Godfather) = a son succeeding his father → "a manure factory"; "people who are not good at inventing stories can try using 'what it is mainly about' as a clue for a concept." Watch old films to learn structure; watch your favorite genre to the end (Sono Sion's horror films).
- **Summarize in one or two sentences** (Uchida): 《洛奇》 (Rocky) = "a third-rate boxer gets a title shot and fights the champion to a draw"; 《放课后》 (After School) = "a man helps his first love"; if you cannot do it, something has gone off course in the middle.
- **Changing the timeline costs nothing** (Uchida): "No matter how you change the timeline, it does not cost a single yen" — the secret weapon of low-budget films; "an ordinary vase will not sell, but say 'if you press it, it glows' and it might."
- **Research**: Sono Sion interviewed high-school girls, one introducing the next (hair extensions, nosebleeds); 李沧东 (Lee Chang-dong) walks while gathering material — "driving a car makes you less of a writer"; Kakei interviewed furniture designers and architects — "precisely because it is not fiction, you can invent an outrageous, enormous lie"; Yokohama's encounter with a bear went into the final shot; Yukisada's goldfish in a blender — "who did he go there with, again?"
- **Personal experience is a breakthrough, not something to copy directly** (Yokohama): "Experience does not equal a film; you have to think about how to apply experience to a film and expand the story" (a cesspit, dodgeball, a decapitation accident, "God"); Sono Sion joined a cult and fled to a radical faction's hideout — "it was all the food's fault"; 荒井晴彦 (Arai Haruhiko): "**All you have to do is expose the things you are ashamed of to others**"; Miki: "The embarrassing parts have a strong infectious power."
- **Theme deferred** (Miki / Uchida / Sono): only around episode seven or eight did they realize "all the characters in this drama are waiting for something" → Rumoi is a city waiting for herring; 《在池中》 (In the Pool): "there is no need for all three people to be cured"; only after changing the names to "Suzume" (Sparrow) and "Peacock" did the breakthrough appear (the fantasy type); **once the theme surfaces, cut redundant shots and lines accordingly**, because "I hate preachiness"; wait for the keyword while writing — "take it slowly." Omiya is the opposite: the protagonist may state the theme at the end ("a hiatus is the preparation period for an opening"), which she admits is "not clever" but powerful; Takahashi: "create a pointer line or shot" as the peak rather than a vague theme.

## IV. Character

- **Character résumé and diary** (Uchida): write the protagonist's résumé and a diary for the three days covered by the story ("running away here would be too cowardly" → "fool the other person and then run" → work backward to the setup), and imagine "if he were a classmate"; "what the audience sees is only the small island above the sea; below the surface lies a mountain-sized mass of 'wasted effort.'"
- **The "qi" (opening) only forms after the character has been fermented and matured** (Sono Sion): a poor writer thinks about gorgeous prose before the character is settled; if a character is interesting, let him steal the protagonist's scenes (Picasso's background overwhelms the flowers).
- **Premise determines character** (Kakei): work backward from the premise to occupation and personality — a beauty crawling out of a can requires a theater kid who will not question it; injury time before death requires a documentary photographer who has a reason to die; "Doraemon has value because Nobita is a failure"; a fixed image + a reversal ("looks serious but is actually a villain") is enough — "becoming a symbol is the entrance to the next stage."
- **Character = actor + flaw** (Fukuda): first think about what the actor can bring to this show that is "delicious and visible"; **start from the flaw** (Spider-Man cannot shoot webs when a woman looks at him; Batman has no flaws, so he is not loved); 80% excellent, 20% flawed; decide whether to "show the loser" or "hint that he is actually excellent"; desperate effort (especially desperate lying) makes a character lovable; villains are both bad and stupid (the pirate trio).
- **Do not create a "role" — write a "person"** (Takahashi Izumi): "If it were a scholar, one would say 'there are documents in the room,' but I think 'there should be two volumes of Doraemon there'"; focus on the person, not the event; look at the "heat" a character gives off (booking 100 people under false names to avoid a company trip).
- **Build character from an outward contrast** (Miki): a punk sitting cross-legged on the subway gives up his seat to an old woman → "Gus"; **names determine character** (Suzume / Peacock; Kinoshita "Kōnosuke"; Omiya: "A name determines the character, so I am usually very careful").
- **The three-person group** (Miki): "With one against one it is hard to judge which set of values is right, and the plot is hard to turn; once the relationships among three people change, the plot can turn together with the values."
- **Add an outsider** (Kosawa): in 《外事警察》 (Gaiji Keisatsu), the female police officer who is selected without quite understanding why shows the audience how peculiar the organization is; "a despicable hero."
- **Let characters enter as you write** (Takahashi): "I was just thinking 'Mr. Kobayashi hasn't appeared,' and then he appeared; since he is here, let him do something"; Fukuda "creates the story while changing the characters' roles," and "I welcome going off track."
- **Arrange characters like building a flower bed or preparing hot-pot ingredients** (Omiya); contrasting characters (Director Kimijima vs. Director Kinoshita).
- **Villains**: Sono Sion: "No one is 100% bad" (for Korean screenwriters' approach to villains, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`).

## V. Dialogue

- **Dialogue comes from a conversation with yourself** (Omiya): "To find out how I feel, I use a lot of words to express it"; "the words that come out of an hour or a month of inner conversation become the dialogue of the script"; do not use song lyrics as reference (it turns into a karaoke video); first fix the color tone and onomatopoeia (sunset / bang).
- **Keep ambiguity; value the echo of sound** (Yokohama): "You cannot tell whether it is true or false," and different people understand it differently; "I value the echo of the sound more than the meaning"; **say only what happened, not "I am sad"** (remove the emotion); a long speech that "speaks the trauma" is like a poem.
- **Write the dialogue first, then generate the story** (Fukuda): after fixing the characters, write dialogue first and create while changing roles; "hand the tsukkomi (ツッコミ — the straight man) role to the audience" so the conversation does not stop (otherwise it is "boke, tsukkomi, boke, tsukkomi" and exhausting to watch); with triple boke (ボケ — the funny man), "everyone is an idiot," and the dialogue can keep going.
- **True dialogue** (Arai): "Don't hit my face, because I am an actress" came from an ex-girlfriend; in an age of concise dialogue, it is more important to write each scene well so that "I love you" moves people because of the setup.
- **Adapting dialogue** (Takahashi): manga dialogue works only because the drawings support it, so it must be rewritten for the screen; the written language of a novel must be replaced with spoken language; invent new dialogue without changing the meaning; turn a passive protagonist active.
- **Pointer dialogue** (Takahashi): take one concrete line or shot as the peak and widen the plain at the foot of the mountain.
- **Regional accents and catchphrases** (Miki / Omiya): Kinoshita's "Time to go home," "Either one is fine," "It's nothing, I won't die"; for 《法尔戈》 (Fargo)-style onomatopoeia, see `sw-american-case-studies`.
- Reading dialogue aloud: after finishing, read it aloud once, or have people role-play a simulated rehearsal (for the Korean and French approaches to table reads, see `sw-korean-french-screenwriting`).

## VI. Comedy and Jokes (Fukuda / Miki / Choi)

- **The joke system**: assign boke / tsukkomi / natural boke (in 《33 分侦探》 (33 Minutes Detective), Inspector Otagawara is the "big boke" who believes in the detective, while the assistant cuts in); hand the tsukkomi to the audience; fixed forms (the eternal line "let me use the full 33 minutes," the moving shot in every episode) leave an "invisible tsukkomi."
- **The detective says what must never be said**: "This and that is just that and that!" "The twins are accomplices!" — assert first, then proceed; borrow tricks from detective fiction to add information.
- **What is a sense of reality?** Fukuda: "Where does a sense of reality come from? From everyday life? Or from TV dramas you have seen before?" — splashing water → spraying insecticide → a paramedic secretly filming; expand the range from the news and other people's absurdities, "change your perspective and ask him with love why he did it"; "a writer having a narrow range of reality is never something to be proud of."
- **One tear-jerker is enough; more than one laugh is needed**; if one person in an audience of 100 does not laugh, you want to ask "what was not interesting?"
- Miki's 脱力系 (datsuryoku-kei — the "power-drained" deadpan style): gags before story; a housewife becomes a spy; a staircase that only goes down; "changing from a black T-shirt to a white T-shirt" and losing something important — **big oversights begin with tiny details** (parallel parking in 《雌雄大盗》 (Bonnie and Clyde)).
- Arai: "If the audience laughs at three or four out of ten gags, that is good — keep throwing more."

## VII. Original vs. Adaptation, Constraints and Collaboration

- **Originals are born from a "situation"** (Yukisada): a clothing company asks a model to wear clothes → a funeral film with everyone in mourning dress; a band's lyrics → burning textbooks; a shampoo commercial → a trilogy with 柴崎幸 (Shibasaki Ko) as a bad woman; a mobile-phone commercial → an end-of-life video; "**Decide on constraints that suit you and then work; restrictions on freedom actually produce innovation; 'please create freely' is the hardest note of all**." Uchida: "Cooking with only cabbage and eggs is simpler"; Kosawa: "The stronger the restriction and lack of freedom, the more interesting the content can become."
- **A suitably bad script is a good script** (Yukisada quoting 鸭下信一 (Kamoshita Shin'ichi)): it leaves room for imagination and excites the crew — "I want to do this"; write even what might be cut and ask "is it necessary?" — "the script exists for argument"; **partner with the screenwriter to "buy time"** (he describes → the other writes → change the differences → swap in the good parts); "a script becomes more precise when many people write it."
- **The script is an axis** (Yukisada): a film "is born three times and dies twice" (script → shooting → editing); if the central axis is not solid, you do not know what to aim at; original = the method of depiction — the ellipsis of 北野武 (Kitano Takeshi) — not the story; do not fear "a certain resemblance," and do not fear superficiality.
- **Notes on adaptation** (Arai / Yukisada / Takahashi / Richards): examine the source from a critic's perspective — "a source you love very much is hard to adapt because love numbs you"; it is fine to keep only the title (《W 的悲剧》 (The Tragedy of W) nests a "bit player chasing a dream" story inside the original); "from what viewpoint did I discover what in the novel?" (伊藤千寻 (Ito Chihiro): "one step forward and it is a film"); the director first tells the screenwriter which part moved him; "when adapting, the screenwriter must not know too much." Three writers played rock-paper-scissors for the beginning, middle, and end, each wrote for a week, then they stitched it together (Arai).
- **Professional outlook** (Kosawa): the script is "their own work" for everyone involved; take requests seriously; "a professional should re-create like someone threading a needle"; commercial success first, then novelty / social impact / personal challenge; "write scripts that can be converted into money"; being able to revise is an essential quality; create work "you cannot understand unless you watch closely"; "the pleasure of making is far greater than the pleasure of watching."
- **Tenacious will** (Takahashi): a newcomer writes three to five scripts a year, ten hours a day; if you have a theme you will pursue for a lifetime ("pain" → gentleness; the pendulum first swings toward pain), you can write anything.
- **Sono Sion**: "If a work cannot provoke strong disgust... it is not truly a good work"; "there is nothing that cannot be done" (too much monologue, four hours, a title sequence only after an hour); structure as a tree: branches and leaves → trunk → roots; either build the structure first and write, or push forward with "serialized" bluster; "devote yourself to the original" — only without the guarantee of source material is it fresh.
- **Korean and French comparisons** in `sw-korean-french-screenwriting` (Oh Seung-wook's undercover research and "liar who tells the truth," Choi Seok-hwan's genre promise and two reversals, Auburtin's "write the dialogue last" and Italian-style table read).

## VIII. Diagnostic Checklist

1. Am I structure-first or fragment-first? After choosing, have I at least finished one script?
2. Does the title make people ask "what is this about?" How did three people react when I told them the synopsis? Have I found the person who said "it's boring" and asked "where?"
3. Is the concept the addition of two things far apart? Did I use "and moreover" rather than "and then"?
4. Is the ending fixed (even if only as one image)? Is the "cheng" rich enough and exciting enough? Does every scene have a sense of jump? Does each line of dialogue negate the line before?
5. Is the exposition ended as early as possible? Does something happen within 10 minutes? For a TV drama, have I listed seven events in each 10-minute block?
6. Character: have I written the résumé/diary? Did I work backward from the premise to the occupation? Have I thought of the actor's flaw? Am I writing a "person" rather than a "role" (those two volumes of Doraemon in the room)? Is the name fixed?
7. Material: have I written one note a day? Am I using research/personal experience as a breakthrough rather than copying it? Have I exposed the things I am ashamed of?
8. Theme: do I let it surface on its own (and cut the excess once it does), or fix it first? Is there any preaching? Is there a pointer line or shot?
9. Comedy: are boke and tsukkomi assigned? Is the tsukkomi handed to the audience? Does my "sense of reality" come only from dramas I have watched?
10. Constraints: what is the constraint this time (budget, location, actor availability, commission terms)? Am I using it as a weapon?
11. Does the script leave room for argument and change? Will the crew be excited when they read it?
12. Must the protagonist have a goal/growth? Is the version without growth truer? Have I written the repetition and deviation of daily life?

## IX. Workflow (an Original Japanese-Style Script)

1. **Toy box**: pour out six months of notes; circle the "if..." and the shot/image you most want to film; find two pieces of material that can be added together.
2. **Title and one line**: write a title that makes people ask "what is this about?"; summarize it in one or two sentences; tell three people.
3. **Choose the path**: structure-first → fix the ending, draw the four-frame / A4 whole-series chart, write the plot outline; fragment-first → write the character résumé/diary, write dialogue first, push forward in "serialized" fashion.
4. **Characters**: work backward from the premise to the occupation; actor + flaw; names; outsider; three-person group; contrasting characters.
5. **Plot outline** (if writing one): describe objectively; write the "event" that moves the protagonist (even a small one); do not write "he thinks this"; mark 起承转合; after a reversal, go back and plant the setup.
6. **Script**: jump, negative dialogue, end exposition early, idle and fan; dialogue from inner conversation and real research; keep ambiguity.
7. **Read**: read it to a demanding person; find someone who says "it's boring"; once the theme surfaces, cut the excess.
8. **企画書** for TV drama (kikakusho — the series proposal): synopsis / characters (catchphrases, mottoes, POV character, contrasting character) / synopses of the first two episodes + episode titles / planning purpose and selling points + comparable works — "an original must be more accessible than an adaptation."
9. **Shoot**: for an independent film, fix the shooting date to force the script out; when there is no money, work with no money; leave the script open to argument; accept that "a film changes because of its actors."
