## 9. Four Samples of Chinese Script Formats (Yao Kougen)

This section supplies the concrete examples behind the comparison table in Section 3, 3.2. All four formats come from the book by Yao Kougen (姚扣根), 《电视剧写作概论》 (Introduction to Television Drama Writing), Chapter 2, Section 2.3 (pp. 0063–0081). Yao cites the classification in the book by Zhu Baishui (朱白水), 《怎样编写电视剧》 (How to Write a TV Drama) (Taiwan Television Culture Company, 1984 edition) and states plainly that "the format of the 'body' within a scene **has no unified standard**" (Yao, p. 0064). **Each sample below is transcribed in the layout of the original book and can be copied directly; which format you use depends on who will read this draft, and for what.**

All four formats share the same set of scene-heading rules (Yao, p. 0064, verbatim): before the body of each scene, specify **location; interior or exterior; time; day or night**; "some also mark the **scene number** and **the characters who appear in the scene**."

### 9.1 Format 1: Separate Visual and Audio Columns (Yao, pp. 0064–0068)

Method (Yao, p. 0064, verbatim): **strictly divide the visual part and the sound part, writing them side by side, left and right**—some instead divide them top and bottom, or arrange them together while marking a clear distinction between visual and audio (for example, marking the visual column with conspicuous symbols)—so that "when the director, actors, and crew hold the script in their hands, **they can see everything at a glance and avoid confusion**."

**Sample A (Yao, pp. 0064–0065, transcribed verbatim)**:

```
VISUAL                                          AUDIO
SCENE: 5   TIME: DAY   SET: LIVING ROOM
·The second hand of the wall clock ticks. The camera tilts down; the living room is quiet, empty.
·A woman, buttoning her clothes, comes out in a hurry.      ·Urgent knocking at the door.
                                                            WOMAN: Who is it?
·The woman opens the door in a panic; a man rushes in nervously.   MAN: (V.O.) Me—open up!
                                                            ·Sirens and footsteps in the distance.
·The man grabs the woman and, flustered, they flee through the side door.
                                                            MAN: Damn it, the police have found out about us. Run!
```

Yao's analysis (p. 0065): visible concrete images (the wall clock, the frantic escape) belong to the visual column; the knocking, sirens, and dialogue belong to the audio column. "**The two parts are kept strictly separate and do not interfere with each other.**"

**Sample B: the opening of Episode 1 of 《人间四月天》 (April Rhapsody), written by Wang Huiling (王惠玲)** (China Translation & Publishing Corporation, 2000 edition; Yao, pp. 0065–0068). The scene heading occupies three separate lines:

```
SCENE: By the stream behind Xiashi village
CHARACTERS: Village woman, villagers
TIME: Early morning, summer 1980

·SUPER: Summer 1980
·The village woman washes clothes on a bluestone slab by the stream.
VILLAGE WOMAN: ...
```

Layout points (Yao, pp. 0065–0068): the visual column is led by a "·"; dialogue is led by "CHARACTER NAME:"; on-screen text stands on its own line ("·SUPER: Summer 1980").

**This passage is also a model for a biographical-drama teaser (Yao, pp. 0065–0068)**: five scenes accomplish four time jumps—the village woman washing clothes on the bluestone slab by the stream (summer 1980) → the slab is lifted, and "**the slab turns out to be a tombstone, inscribed 'Tomb of the Poet Xu Zhimo (徐志摩)'**" → winter 1898, the Xu family's zhuazhou (抓周 — the first-birthday object-choice ritual; the child picks a bamboo dragonfly, which the old lady interprets as "rising straight to the blue clouds, soaring into the sky") → summer 1904, the seven-year-old Zhimo flies a kite, rolls down a grassy slope, and "has his first doubt about life" → autumn 1931, in the same desolate grass, a search team finds the wreckage of an airplane → "SUPER: Xu Zhimo (AD 1897–1931)." All the jumps are stitched together by **the same visual motif (bluestone slab / wild grass / flight)**.

**When to use it**: dramas in which sound design itself carries the narrative (music, sound effects, voice-over, layered ambience); drafts that must show the sound recordist and post-production "who hears what, and when"; openings of biographical or period dramas with complex visual-audio relationships.

### 9.2 Format 2: Stage-Play Script Format (Yao, pp. 0069–0071)

Method (Yao, p. 0069, verbatim): "It consists basically of character dialogue, narration, and a small number of cue words; **there is very little careful description of characters' appearance, inner life, or atmosphere**. This script format is especially suited to **interior drama** that is driven by dialogue."

**Sample: the opening of Episode 1 of 《阿信的故事》 (Oshin), written by Hashida Sugako** (Chunfeng Literature and Art Publishing House, 1985 edition; Yao, pp. 0069–0071). Layout features: the scene name stands on its own line ("Shima Peninsula" / "Tazawa house (morning)" / "Bedroom" / "Oshin's room"); **stage directions are enclosed in square brackets [ ] for the whole passage**; dialogue uses "CHARACTER NAME: (emotional cue) line."

```
SHIMA PENINSULA

[V.O.] It is early spring in the fifty-eighth year of Showa (1983). At the Tazawa house
in a small town on the Shima Peninsula, the grandmother, Oshin, has suddenly run away.
Oshin is already eighty-three. Her second son, Tazawa Jin, has built sixteen large
supermarkets in the surrounding towns, and today is the auspicious opening of his
seventeenth. The Tazawa family's prosperity is entirely the fruit of Oshin's painstaking
management. Yet on the very day that symbolizes the family's grand expansion—the opening
of the seventeenth supermarket—Oshin has gone off alone, something no one in the family
can believe. Naturally, no one can guess why she left.

TAZAWA HOUSE (MORNING)

TAZAWA JIN: (glancing irritably at his watch) If we don't leave soon, we'll be late.
TATSUNORI: Shall I tell them to postpone the ceremony for an hour?
TAZAWA JIN: It isn't just family—we've invited a great many guests, even the mayor is
        coming. Could the Tazawa family afford such a loss of face?
TATSUNORI: But if Mother isn't there...
TAZAWA JIN: That's the old woman's temper. When the time comes, she'll suddenly appear.
        ...Besides, what reason could she possibly have for leaving home?
TATSUNORI: Mother has been strongly opposed to this opening from the very start...

OSHIN'S ROOM

ATSU: (dejected) So Mother really did plan to run away! ...Five of her favorite dresses
      are missing, and two sashes... And the handbag I bought her in Paris is gone too.
MICHIKO: It's hard, being a daughter-in-law.
```

Two techniques (Yao, pp. 0069–0071): ① **opening on a mystery (why she left) + the promise that the whole drama will answer it in retrospect**—the standard opening move of a pure-dialogue play; ② **the concrete action of taking inventory of the wardrobe upgrades "a momentary wandering off" to "a planned departure"**, while a single grumble from Michiko sketches the mother-in-law/daughter-in-law relationship—that is, **all the premises are established through an argument**.

**When to use it**: interior drama, sitcoms, dialogue-driven family drama; rehearsal drafts that actors will read aloud directly; projects that do not need to establish atmosphere (the sets are fixed).

### 9.3 Format 3: Film-Script Format (Yao, pp. 0071–0072)

Method (Yao, p. 0071, verbatim): "It attends to the image on screen and is arranged basically **with a single shot or a group of shots as the unit of the sentence**. It has a strong sense of the camera, large jumps between images, emphasizes visual composition, uses few descriptive words, and **conveys image and sound in the most concise language possible**."

**Sample: the opening of Episode 1 of 《十六岁的花季》 (The Flower Season of Sixteen)** (Yao, pp. 0071–0072). Layout features: the scene name is set in square brackets ([Campsite] / "Gate of No. 101 Middle School"); the body is broken into short lines; dialogue is written directly on the line, and inversion is also allowed ("'He Damen, step forward.' Instructor Pan called He Damen's name in front of the group.").

```
[CAMPSITE]

The ranks are neat and straight.

INSTRUCTOR PAN: "Eyes front. Hands must be at this position. Forward march."

HAN XIAOLE: "Sir, if he does that, his backside sticks out."

The students giggle.

HAN XIAOLE: "In this heat, we'll all turn into roast ducks."

A wave of laughter (suppressed).

"He Damen, step forward." Instructor Pan called He Damen's name in front of the group.

HAN XIAOLE: "Sir, nothing—my stomach just growled."

INSTRUCTOR PAN: "Our new recruits are barely two years older than you, and they train six hours a day. You train one hour."

Outside the gate of No. 101 Middle School. Parents gather; someone passes in a cake, someone passes in a bottle of medicated balm.
```

Structural points (Yao, p. 0072): Han Xiaole interrupts three times in a row = **three escalations, three beats of "the students giggle / a wave of laughter (suppressed)"**, and the instructor finally counters with statistics; then the film cuts to the parents outside the school gate (a cake for a grandson with stomach trouble / medicated balm), creating a "**counterpoint between inside and outside the school**."

**When to use it**: visual, action-heavy, fast-paced drama; youth drama, action drama, short-run series; drafts that must let a director see the shot breakdown at a glance.

### 9.4 Format 4: TV-Novel Format (Yao, pp. 0072–0081)

Method (Yao, pp. 0072–0073, verbatim): "It is relatively free: while attending to the description of television images, it adopts a narrative close to the novel, setting out the scene, environment, and atmosphere in some detail, and frequently inserting explanatory passages about characters' psychology or the development of the plot. In its prose it pursues literary readability... **This format is currently quite widely used; it not only provides a script for shooting but also lays the groundwork for publication as a TV novel.**"—This sentence explains the origin of the most common format in Chinese domestic scripts.

**Sample: 《新岸》 (The New Shore)** (a story about sent-down youth (知青, zhīqīng), featuring Liu Yanhua and Gao Yuangang; Yao, pp. 0073–0080, a long transcription). Yao then gives the only craft analysis in the book (p. 0080), which can be used directly as a writing brief for this format:

- Overall verdict: "**The three passages—digging river mud, the ox cart going to town, and the hospital visit—flow in one breath and fully mobilize the various elements of image and sound.**"
- **The three-step progression of "digging river mud"** (the standard use of a repeated motif): the first time, "each is guarded and they ignore each other"; the second time, "the wary look turns into a calm, gentle expression"; the third time, "the cart sinks into the mud and the two strain to drive it out; then Liu Yanhua comes to the pond and, in the glow of sunset, washes the mud from both their shoes."
- **Sound design**: natural ambience, calls to the draft animals, the creak of the ox-cart wheels ("ge-niu-niu"), and the tinkle of the bronze bell on the ox's neck.
- **Space as a visualization of relationship**: in the ox-cart scene, "the two go from one in front of the cart and one behind it to sitting together in front of the ox, where they have their first conversation, and then to one in front and one behind again; **the change in distance implies the change in feeling**."
- **Prop chain**: the yellow rubber shoes appear three times (**taking them off → wedging them under the cart → washing them**); the contrast between cornmeal buns (wotou) and white-flour sugar cakes (the Gao family: "Save me some white flour... and griddle a few sugar cakes" → the cakes are offered on the cart and refused → during the hospital visit, "the cracked cornmeal bun in the coarse porcelain bowl").

**When to use it**: the default option; projects published simultaneously as a novel of the same title; submission drafts and sample scripts in which psychology and atmosphere must be written for a producer or executive to read. **Note**: this format puts psychological description on the page, but the camera cannot photograph psychology—so Yao immediately follows with the shot-by-shot comparison below, showing that the psychology in a literary script must be converted into shots by the director.

### 9.5 The New Shore: Literary Script → A Ten-Shot Side-by-Side Comparison (Yao, pp. 0080–0081; the book's only literary script → storyboard comparison)

After the long transcription from The New Shore, Yao sets out, verbatim, the storyboard in which the director covers the "hospital visit" scene in **ten shots**. The left column is the narrative content of that passage in the TV-novel format; the right column is the director's conversion into shots.

| # | What the literary script (TV-novel format) says | The director's shot breakdown (Yao, pp. 0080–0081, verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gao Yuangang enters and sees Liu Yanhua on the kang (heated brick bed) | **MEDIUM CLOSE-UP–FULL SHOT (PULL BACK/TRACK)** Gao Yuangang hurries in, looks, and freezes: Liu Yanhua is lying on the kang. |
| 2 | His worry | **MEDIUM CLOSE-UP—**Gao Yuangang looks on with worry. |
| 3 | Her condition (breathing, lips, unconsciousness) | **MEDIUM CLOSE-UP—**Liu Yanhua breathes rapidly, her lips are cracked, she is unconscious. |
| 4 | Takes a towel, ladles water, soaks it, lays it on her forehead, sits on the edge of the kang and watches | **CLOSE SHOT—(PULL BACK/TRACK)** Gao Yuangang hurriedly takes a towel from the line, ladles a gourdful of water from the vat, soaks the towel, gently lays it on Liu Yanhua's forehead, and sits on the edge of the kang, watching anxiously. |
| 5 | She wakes | **MEDIUM CLOSE-UP—**Liu Yanhua wakes and opens her eyes slightly. |
| 6 | His anxiety | **MEDIUM CLOSE-UP—**Gao Yuangang's anxious expression. |
| 7 | Her "indescribable feeling" and "closing the door of her heart" (the literary script writes psychology) | **MEDIUM CLOSE-UP–CLOSE-UP (PUSH IN)** Liu Yanhua's eyes moisten; tears well and roll. Afraid that Gao Yuangang will see this indescribable feeling, she laboriously raises her hand and pulls the towel over her eyes, shutting the door of her heart. Yet a teardrop rolls out from under the towel to her cheek, leaving a tear track. |
| 8 | He holds back tears and looks around | **CLOSE SHOT—**Gao Yuangang holds back his tears and looks around. |
| 9 | The split yellow rubber shoes and the cracked cornmeal bun (the literary script writes "a certain feeling one is made to sense") | **CLOSE-UP—(PAN)** Below the edge of the kang, the pair of yellow rubber shoes he knows so well are split; on the small table, the cornmeal bun in the coarse porcelain bowl is cracked... |
| 10 | He is heavy-hearted | **CLOSE SHOT—**Gao Yuangang is heavy-hearted. (Exits frame.) |

Yao's reading (p. 0081, key points verbatim): the medium close-up shows "what he sees upon entering, and the relationship between the two"; shot 7 "**pushes in from a close shot to a close-up, precisely capturing the teardrop rolling out from under the towel and the tear track left on her cheek**"; Gao Yuangang's state of mind is handled with a "**POV shot + close-up**" (the split yellow rubber shoes, the cracked cornmeal bun), and "this seemingly casual glance lets us sense a certain feeling hidden deep in the protagonist's heart."

→ **Conversion rules for the screenwriter** (reverse-engineered from the table above): ① every sentence in the literary script such as "he worries / she is anxious / he is heavy-hearted" occupies its own separate shot in the storyboard, so **each change of emotion must start a new sentence**; do not knead it into a long paragraph; ② **unfilmable psychology** in the literary script ("indescribable feeling," "closing the door of her heart") must be attached to a filmable action (raising her hand to pull the towel over her eyes) and a filmable physical piece of evidence (the teardrop rolling out from under the towel); ③ when writing "he senses a certain feeling," you must supply, in the same passage, **two props** that let him sense it (the split rubber shoes, the dried cornmeal bun); otherwise the director has no shot to film.

## 10. Planning-Document Samples (Yao Kougen)

This section supplies the concrete examples behind the "six documents" comparison table in Section 2. Source: Chapter 15, "TV Drama Planning," of Yao Kougen's 《电视剧写作概论》 (Introduction to Television Drama Writing) (pp. 0328–0348). Yao's list of planning documents (p. 0329, verbatim): "Generally, planning content includes: **plot summary, character design, main locations, and creative statement**."

### 10.1 Plot Summary = Logline: Definition and Three Functions (Yao, p. 0329)

Definition (verbatim, including three Chinese renderings): "A concise summary of the plot (**Logline**)—sometimes translated as **high concept**, sometimes as '**计程绳**' (jìchéngshéng — 'taxi-meter cord')—refers to one sentence, or perhaps two or three sentences, that can sum up a story, expressing as concisely as possible **the dramatic premise, the genre, and the thematic value**."

The three functions of high concept (Yao, p. 0329, verbatim): "1. Concisely summarize the story; 2. Roughly position the genre; 3. **Demonstrate that the story has extraordinary plot power and is attractive without the participation of stars**."

### 10.2 Six Loglines, Transcribed Verbatim (Yao, pp. 0329–0330)

| # | Work | Logline (verbatim) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Film **《辛德勒名单》 (Schindler's List)** | "A businessman who seems devoted to sensual pleasures saves hundreds of Jews during the Nazi Holocaust." |
| 2 | Film **《史密斯夫妇》 (Mr. & Mrs. Smith)** | "A marital cold war becomes a spy rivalry, turning the couple's everyday conflicts and couples therapy into a violent operation of espionage and counter-espionage." |
| 3 | British series **《安娜·卡列尼娜》 (Anna Karenina)** | "Ladies of society must suppress their inner passions and submit to the rules of high society, while Anna risks everything in pursuit of her own happiness, preferring social ostracism to being anything less than a true woman." |
| 4 | American series **《越狱》 (Prison Break)** | "A structural engineer robs a bank in broad daylight and enters prison to help his wrongly convicted brother escape, thereby uncovering a vast conspiracy." |
| 5 | American series **《绯闻女孩》 (Gossip Girl)** (Yao's note: "called the youth version of 《欲望城市》 (Sex and the City)") | "It is about a group of wealthy young men and women in New York—designer clothes, exclusive parties, and other fashionable urban elements—as well as the false friendships and scheming among friends." |
| 6 | American series **《绝命毒师》 (Breaking Bad)** | "A high-school chemistry teacher with a wife and a disabled son is diagnosed with terminal cancer; he decides to use his knowledge of chemistry to manufacture drugs, so that after his death he can leave his family some money." |

→ The writing method reverse-engineered from the six examples (where Yao's "three elements" from p. 0329 land in each): **Examples 1, 4, and 6 follow a three-part "identity + abnormal action + cost" pattern** (businessman → saves Jews / engineer → robs a bank and enters prison / chemistry teacher → makes drugs); **Example 2 is a "genre displacement" pattern** (displacing married life into a spy operation); **Examples 3 and 5 are a "group situation + one exception" pattern** (the ladies all submit; Anna does not). **Example 5 is worth noting: it describes only a world and an atmosphere, not an action, and is the only one of the six that does not contain "someone does something"—Yao includes it all the same, which shows that "ensemble + list of elements" is a legitimate logline form in series television.**

### 10.3 The Exception for TV Drama: Length Requirements (Yao, p. 0330)

Verbatim: "Sometimes a TV drama can be summarized in one or two sentences, **but sometimes, because the content is complex and covers a wide range, it takes a good deal more space to explain clearly**." Yao then gives five complete "plot summaries," each about **200–400 Chinese characters**—this is the book's actual standard for the length of a "story synopsis."

### 10.4 Five Sample Plot Summaries

**Sample 1: 《北京人在纽约》 (A Native of Beijing in New York) (Yao, pp. 0330–0331, transcribed in full, verbatim)**

> Wang Qiming, a cellist in a Beijing symphony orchestra, and his wife Guo Yan come to New York to pursue their artistic dreams.
> Wang Qiming works in a restaurant and becomes entangled in an emotional dispute with its owner, Ah Chun. Guo Yan goes to work in a sweater factory, where the owner, David, takes a liking to her and pursues her relentlessly. Wang Qiming's struggle to make a living goes badly, and he prepares to return to China; but before boarding the plane, he decides to divorce his wife, earn some money in America first, and then return to art. Wang Qiming falls into Ah Chun's arms and, with her help, runs a sweater factory. After parting from her husband, Guo Yan marries David. **Wang Qiming exploits Guo Yan's lingering feelings to obtain business intelligence and destroy David's factory.**
> After succeeding, Wang Qiming brings his daughter to America. She is deeply disappointed by her parents' present circumstances and intensely hostile to Ah Chun; rebellious, she leaves home.
> **Deserted by family and friends, Wang Qiming ignores Ah Chun's warnings, rashly invests in real estate, and goes bankrupt in the economic downturn. Finally, to salvage something from total failure, he takes the only money he has left and goes to Las Vegas.**

→ Four paragraphs, each ending in an irreversible change of identity or relationship: arrival in America → divorce and a change of sides → the daughter leaves home → bankruptcy and departure for Las Vegas. **Not one thematic judgment appears in the whole piece; the judgment is handed to the reader through the "ending sentence."**

**Sample 2: 《大长今》 (Dae Jang Geum) (Yao, p. 0331, compressed into three paragraphs)**

> ① Her mother, Lady Park, discovers a plot to use food for intrigue in the royal kitchen and is instead falsely accused and killed; her father is caught up in court intrigue and brutally murdered; "**on her deathbed, Lady Park hopes that her daughter Jang-geum will enter the palace and become the highest-ranking kitchen matron in the royal kitchen, to avenge her**."
> ② Jang-geum enters the palace and the kitchen, and "in the workplace struggles of the kitchen she is constantly framed by the faction that controls the kitchen, nearly loses her life, and is banished to a remote island."
> ③ "**Suffering creates another opportunity for Jang-geum**; she devotes herself to medicine and integrates it with royal cuisine, enters the palace again... and becomes Korea's first female royal physician, granted the title Dae Jang Geum. At the same time, she finally uncovers the truth and clears her mother's name."

→ **Three paragraphs = three identities (kitchen woman → criminal → healer)**; each turn between paragraphs is driven by "being expelled"; a single sentence of the mother's dying wish nails down the goal of the whole series at the end of the first paragraph.

**Sample 3: 《白色巨塔》 (The White Tower) (Yao, pp. 0331–0332, key points transcribed verbatim)**

> Associate Professor Zaizen Goro of Naniwa University Hospital, in order to realize his ideal of becoming a professor in the First Department of Surgery, breaks with his mentor Professor Azuma, who has been pushing him aside, and with the financial power and connections of his father-in-law Zaizen Mataichi... launches a fierce battle for votes... **Just as he is at the height of his ambitions, a latent crisis slowly emerges: because of his careless attitude toward ordinary patients, a patient named Sasaki Yohei dies from his misdiagnosis. And at this moment, he is in Germany attending an academic conference.**... After Zaizen wins the first-instance verdict, Sasaki's family insists on appealing. With the help of the upright lawyer Sekiguchi and Dr. Satomi, they win the final victory. **Although Zaizen wins yet another election—the election for membership in the academic society—he collapses under the double blow of losing the lawsuit and being ravaged by illness.**

→ **A synopsis written with two interlocking lines**: one is "a winning streak in elections," the other "accountability for a misdiagnosis," and the two converge in a single sentence ("won the victory, but... collapsed"). **The sentence "And at this moment, he is in Germany attending an academic conference" is a model for hiding a moral judgment inside a scheduling detail.**

**Sample 4: 《上海往事》 (Shanghai Past), a biography of 张爱玲 (Zhang Ailing, known in English as Eileen Chang) (Yao, p. 0332, framework transcription)**

> Framework: **1955, goes to America → the trunk left by her deceased mother opens childhood memories → 1937, flees her family → University of Hong Kong → 1941, returns to Shanghai and lives by her writing → 1944, meets 胡兰成 (Hu Lancheng) → 1955, goes to America → 1995, dies alone in America.**
> Key sentences: "They wrote out a marriage certificate, wishing for '**岁月静好，现世安稳** (suìyuè jìng hǎo, xiànshì ān wěn — "quiet years, a peaceful world")'"; "Having left Hu Lancheng, Eileen Chang could only wither."

→ **A biographical synopsis = a string of years + a frame that closes at both ends** (1955 appears twice: once as the narrative present at the opening, once as a return to the same point on the timeline). **The object (the trunk left behind by her deceased mother) serves as the switch for all the flashbacks.**

**Sample 5: 《长恨歌》 (The Song of Everlasting Sorrow) (Yao, pp. 0332–0333, divided into three years)**

| Year | Content of this section | Irreversible change at the end of this section |
|---|---|---|
| **1943** | Wang Qiyao, a sixteen-year-old schoolgirl → Mr. Cheng photographs her for a magazine cover → she becomes a Miss Shanghai candidate | "**kept as a mistress in a gilded cage**" |
| **1958** | She gives injections in the lane neighborhood for a living → Kang Mingxun → the Kang family disapproves → she secretly gives birth to a daughter, Weiwei | Kang leaves for Hong Kong |
| **1978** | Weiwei is eighteen → Mr. Cheng, an "老克勒" (lǎokēlè — an old-school Shanghai gentleman), becomes a regular guest at the Wang home → Kang Mingxun returns to help his daughter go abroad | "Mr. Cheng has an incurable disease; he loves Wang Qiyao as always" |

→ **The executable format Yao gives (p. 0333)**: **the "plot summary" of a long-running series = 3–5 paragraphs, each with one time anchor (a year) or one change of relationship; each paragraph must end with an irreversible change of identity or relationship.**

### 10.5 Two Author's Tips That Accompany These Five Synopses (Yao, p. 0337)

- "**The screenwriter's knack is to create one tense, attention-grabbing event after another, and to keep the synopsis flexible, as if it were a riddle, so that later, according to your own creative strength, you can repair it, perfect it, and make it personal.**"
- "**You can use a story tree or a flowchart to break down and abbreviate the entire plot, perfecting the plot and fermenting the ideas as much as possible at the synopsis stage. Once the synopsis is approved, when you sit down to write the first page, you are no longer creating—you are typing.**"

### 10.6 Sample Plot Flow: The Episode in 《亮剑》 (Drawing Sword) in Which the Japanese Special Forces Unit Is Destroyed (Yao, pp. 0336–0337)

Yao's detailed template for "plot flow" (p. 0335, verbatim): "**What happened, when, and where; which subsequent events these events triggered; how they are connected; what the specific motives of the characters' actions are; how the action unfolds; and what the result is.**" The accompanying example is the plot-flow treatment of the passage in Drawing Sword in which the Japanese special forces unit is destroyed; key details (Yao, pp. 0336–0337, key points verbatim):

- Colonel Yamamoto "studied in Germany and created a lean, elite special forces unit, the first of its kind in the Japanese army invading China";
- "To reform their tactics, **a battlefield observation group** of 200 officers was formed to observe special operations";
- "**Li Yunlong was anything but a by-the-book commander; he divided his forces to set an ambush, and the result was an unintended triumph: he annihilated the Japanese battlefield observation group and at the same time relieved the desperate threat to the Eighth Route Army headquarters**";
- Chu Yunfei and Li Yunlong "brought only their bodyguards, yet routed the Japanese military police in the county town"; "the military police had secretly colluded with a turncoat in the Independent Regiment";
- The battle for Ping'an County town "covered more than a thousand square kilometers and committed a total of 300,000 troops on both sides";
- The double ending: "**Li Yunlong loses his newlywed wife, Xiuqin, and is severely criticized by Eighth Route Army leaders for acting without authorization.**"

→ Contrast with the "plot summary": **a synopsis says "what happened"; a plot flow says "because of what, therefore what."** A plot flow must contain causal connectives such as "anything but," "as a result," "both... and...," and "and also suffered," and its final sentence must state **both the gain and the loss** (destroying the enemy + losing his wife + being criticized).

### 10.7 What This Section Does Not Record

- **No concrete sample is given for the "main locations" document** (Yao, p. 0329, lists only the name of this item, with no example)—**not recorded**. #### Yao's Concluding Analysis (p. 0297, key points verbatim)

- "These 41 scenes take a series of seemingly chaotic, complex, and unrelated passages and, **through their interconnections and echoes, through the setup and resolution of their respective conflicts, compose a complete story**, expressing each character's personality and emotional exchanges, and finally fuse into the profound theme of love."
- **The metaphorical design of the opening**: "The episode opens **with the nurses' excitement over rehearsing 《罗密欧与朱丽叶》 (Romeo and Juliet), establishing a metaphor**. Before this, Dr. Mark's marriage has run into trouble; after this, a patient dredges up Dr. Susan's failed romance. **These two doctors will later come together; the hint here lays the groundwork for their later romance.**"
- **The unity of theme (this episode is "The Test of Love")**: "Fear and hesitation before marriage; separation and endurance after marriage—**love, like a flowing river, stirs up whirlpools and waves in everyone's life**... **each segment seems less than sunny; instead it is full of gloom, striving to show a trace of warmth in the cold wind and fine rain**." Point by point: Hathaway's "real worry is whether the man she is marrying is her true love"; Susan and her sister are "in fact both failures in love"; Doug "doubts whether he can be accepted"; the suicide victim's wife is determined to leave him; the nun "already has a boyfriend but still hesitates over whether to marry."
- **Naming the structure (a key term)**: "A brilliant ensemble piece: the independent slices of each character's life switch back and forth within a '**synchronic**' space, **repeatedly heightening the same emotion**, weaving a perfect web of plot... **This multi-threaded, event-intersecting scattered-point structure (散点式结构, sǎndiǎnshì jiégòu — 'scattered-point' structure) possesses an extremely strong artistic tension.**"
- → **The recipe (Yao's executable version, p. 0297)**: one episode = **five cross-episode continuing lines + five single-episode accidental lines**; the ten lines share one abstract theme; a "rehearsal/prop" metaphor in the opening states the theme; **Scenes 1 and 41 bookend each other with the same family scene**.

### 11.2 Scene-by-Scene Breakdown of Episode 1 of 《北京人在纽约》 (A Native of Beijing in New York) (Yao, pp. 0236–0252; the book's most complete analysis of a Chinese domestic single episode)

Episode 1 story line (Yao, p. 0236, verbatim): "Wang Qiming and Guo Yan have just arrived in America... they get off the plane and wait for their aunt to pick them up. The aunt helps the young couple rent a basement room in a slum area and contacts a restaurant called 'Xiangyuan Lou,' introducing Wang Qiming to a part-time job there." Yao's structural judgment: "'Airport pickup,' 'settling in,' 'going to work'—everything is within the everyday order... **yet it clearly shows the subtle changes in the characters' relationships: Wang Qiming and his wife Guo Yan go from trust to anger, disappointment, and estrangement after discovering that they are being ignored. This is a complete dramatic scene.**"

The one English line at the opening (Yao, p. 0236): "At New York airport, Wang Qiming goes through immigration; the customs officer stamps his passport with a thump and says, '**Welcome to New York!**' Wang Qiming freezes for a moment, chews over the words with effort, and finally understands this common English sentence."

#### Segment 1: The Ecstasy of Entering the City, in 20 Shots (Yao, pp. 0237–0238, transcribed verbatim)

> (1) The car drives toward downtown. (2) The shot pulls back from a close-up of Wang Qiming to a close shot of Wang Qiming and Guo Yan; they look at each other, embrace, and smile. Wang Qiming excitedly hugs Guo Yan and shouts, "New York! Manhattan!" (3) A high-angle shot of the New York skyline at night. (4) The car moves forward. (5) Inside the car, a melody begins; Wang Qiming becomes excited by the non-diegetic music and waves his hands as if conducting. Guo Yan looks out, overwhelmed by the sights. (6) The New York nightscape, a forest of skyscrapers. (7) Wang Qiming seems to be conducting an orchestra. (8) A New York street at night, ablaze with lights. (9) Wang Qiming keeps conducting; Guo Yan smiles excitedly. (10) A New York street at night. (11) Wang Qiming shouts, "America! New York!" (12) A New York street at night, neon billboards. (13) Wang Qiming shouts, "Wang Qiming is here!" Guo Yan leans on him happily. (14) The camera tracks forward with the car; a full shot of pedestrians moving along the roadside. (15) Wang Qiming waves out the window. (16) **The back of the aunt's head; she turns slightly to glance behind her, a little puzzled by the young couple's fuss.** (17) Wang Qiming and Guo Yan do not notice the aunt's reaction at all, wholly immersed in excitement. (18) A low-angle view of the New York nightscape. (19) In the car, Wang Qiming conducts even more vigorously to the non-diegetic music. Guo Yan keeps looking around on her own. (20) The car moves forward.

Yao's three-point evaluation (p. 0238): ① **display foreign scenery, but the scenery must be clearly tied to the plot**—"avoiding the problem in some film and television works, where pure exotic scenery is the only thing attended to, so that the display of scenery and the story and characters' psychology remain two separate layers, never joined"; ② **set up motif music**—the 《新大陆》 (New World Symphony) "serves a narrative function, as an auditory image detail running through the whole series"; when the same melody returns in the later half, "it no longer has the excitement of those years; it only sounds slow and sorrowful"; ③ **vary the narrative rhythm**—"color shifts from monotone to rich; space shifts from the enclosure of the airport to the openness of the street." He also states clearly the real function of this passage (highly executable): "**The change of rhythm, from the oppression of the airport to the exaltation of the street, also provides a full and powerful setup for the further oppression of the slum basement to come. In terms of narrative technique, this passage exists entirely to set off the next scene. The soaring along the road is merely an audiovisual sensation; the muted dejection of the destination is real life.**"

#### Segment 2: The Basement Night Talk—Yao's Defense of the "Eventless Scene" (pp. 0240–0242)

Verbatim: "Saying trivial things, doing disconnected things, expressing a trivial feeling—these are not the focus of dramatic narration. Those scattered fragments, the feeling of memory, worries about the future, a gentle sense of failure, ideas never clearly expressed, no perceptible causal connection—none of this can drive the plot forward... **But this prose-like narration, with the contradictory feelings it reveals, seems like a digression, yet stillness defeats action; it moves toward the romantic, the blurry, the casual, and has a special aesthetic value.**"

A three-sentence craft summary (Yao, p. 0242, **usable directly as a writing instruction**): "In this night-talk scene in A Native of Beijing in New York, **psychology is written where there is no event; a stir of the heart is written in a quiet environment; emotion is written where there is no passion**. Emotion is a state of mind, and it is the most contagious, the most infectious."

#### Segment 4: The Four Rounds of the Meeting with Ah Chun (Yao, pp. 0244–0250)

Why it must be developed fully (Yao, p. 0244, two points verbatim): ① "**The first scene of a major confrontation** has a signature function of leading the whole series... Their first meeting serves to test and to lead. Once interest is established, the audience will have expectations." ② "**As the finale of Episode 1, this scene has the function of luring the audience into the next episode. The last passage in the structure of a TV series is an important point of audience excitement and serves to set up suspense. And usually the ending of Episode 1 is the lead-in that states the problem of the whole series, pointing the way toward the climax of the series' conflict.**"

The setup uses contrast (Yao, p. 0244): "The director gives us a minute-and-a-half-long shot of a walk across the street... **this kind of passage also relaxes the audience's nerves and lowers their expectations to zero.**" The planted prop is **a glass of beer** ("Frank takes out a bottle of beer; Wang Qiming nods")—"**in this scene, this glass of beer serves as a hidden pretext, stringing together a series of exquisite exchanges of dialogue**."

| Round | Ah Chun's Action | Key Dialogue (Yao, pp. 0245–0250, verbatim) | Yao's Analysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One** | Polite probing; she gets rebuffed | Ah Chun: "**Not only people from Beijing can speak Mandarin, you know.**" / Wang Qiming: "Do you just grab whatever you can and play it?!" | **Two** | Politeness turns to anger | Ah Chun: "**I'm in charge here.**" / Wang: "Let's call it a transition." "Sorry—when I used to come to restaurants, I always came to eat; I never thought about..." / Ah Chun: "**Then please leave this place at once.**" | She throws him out after her status is slighted |

**The episode's "first and last line" structure (Yao, p. 0250; the loveliest discovery in the book; verbatim)**: "In this episode, the first sentence the protagonist understands is: '**Welcome to America!**' And the last sentence he hears is: '**You work for me!**' Between the first sentence and the last, the main segments of the episode form several points, each built on Wang Qiming's feelings after arriving in America. **The true meaning of 'welcome' is 'work for me'; when Wang Qiming grasps this subtext, he immediately falls from the peak of his most inflated self-regard to the valley of his most wounded self-regard.**" He adds a method: "The screenwriter establishes the starting point and the landing point, then alternates tension and relaxation, unhurried, forming a process... **through several changes of location, Wang Qiming is tossed high and then slammed down hard. The last point in this process is one the screenwriter must not easily let go of and allow to proceed in the natural order of time. When the episode reaches this dramatic point, it stops short.**"

**Two layers of "segments must form a composite whole" (Yao, p. 0251, verbatim)**: "First, **segments can use various artistic techniques, pursuing the different effects of film, prose, and drama**; second, **segments must submit to a unified meaning, to the meaning of the episode-segment, and to the meaning of the whole series**."

**An important permission (Yao, p. 0252)**: Wang Qiming's true goal is not established until, just before boarding the plane, he is stung by Ah Chun's contempt (Ah Chun's famous line: "**Let me tell you, America is neither hell nor heaven—it's a battlefield!**")—"**one could say that only when Wang Qiming is called a 'loser' does the screenwriter give the protagonist a clear goal: to become a success!**" → **The protagonist's true goal can be established after Episode 1.**

### 11.3 A Comparison of the Two Scene Tables

| Dimension | One ER Episode (Yao, pp. 0289–0297) | Episode 1 of A Native of Beijing in New York (Yao, pp. 0236–0252) |
|---|---|---|
| Number of scenes | **41** | 5 segments (including one 20-shot dialogue-free passage) |
| Number of lines | 10 (5 continuing + 5 accidental) | 2 (emotional entanglement + survival in a foreign country; see Section 4, 4.2) |
| Naming of structure | **Scattered-point / synchronic space** (Yao's terms) | **Dramatic**, composite of segments |
| Device for unifying theme | The rehearsal metaphor in the opening + the ten lines sharing "The Test of Love" | The first and last lines ("Welcome" → "You work for me") |
| Opening and ending | Scenes 1 and 41 use the same family scene | The first English sentence understood and the last Chinese sentence heard |
| Closure of each line | Each line has one small turn; **not all are required to resolve** (B4 leaves only "Benton's brother reminds him that Jen is married") | One big reversal in the finale; **the goal is withheld until the next episode** |

## 12. The 36 Situations and the 36 Comic Situations

Both source lists are in Yao Kougen's Introduction to Television Drama Writing. **The 36 situations** = Georges Polti's list; Yao gives all the names in Chapter 9, p. 0207, and in Appendix 1, pp. 0349–0363, rewrites each one in a Chinese context and supplies 1–3 dramatic examples for each. **The 36 comic situations** = the list by Li Tianji (李天济), from his 1981 Nanjing Film Screenwriting Workshop lecture, "A Comprehensive Talk on Film Comedy"; Yao transcribes the entire list verbatim on pp. 0207–0208.

Yao's attitude toward the two lists (p. 0207, verbatim): on one hand, he criticizes "Georges Polti's claim that all the flavors of life are contained in these 36 dramatic situations—that is going a bit too far... **confining the rich variety of life to certain situations actually kills human discovery and creation**"; on the other hand, he permits borrowing: "But... these situations have not entirely lost their charm in today's life; **they can still be borrowed**." This is a clear divergence from American how-to books, which emphasize originality and avoid formula.

### 12.3 How the Two Lists Differ in Use

| | The 36 Situations | The 36 Comic Situations |
|---|---|---|
| Level | **A character's predicament** (what situation a person is placed in) | **The shape of the outcome** (how things finally go crooked) |
| What it pairs with | Character relationships (who against whom) and time (how long) | The moment of **deflation / dislocation / backlash** |
| How they combine | Yao's "**composite principle**": one protagonist is in several situations at once (p. 0363) | Can be layered onto a situation: the same situation with a different comic premise turns tragedy into comedy (the example for Entry 32, "recklessness," gives both the tragic Zhang Fei and the comic Li Kui; Yao, p. 0362) |
| When to consult | At the 立项 (lìxiàng — project approval) and character-relationship stage | At the episode-breakdown and scene-breakdown stages, especially when writing sitcom and light-comedy elements |
