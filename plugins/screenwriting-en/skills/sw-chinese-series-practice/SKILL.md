# Mainland Chinese Series Craft and Production Chain (Chinese Series Practice)

> **Output language = the language of the question.** Always anchor terminology to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

The writing system, document chain, adaptation craft, and production/censorship chain of mainland Chinese television drama. Five sources, each with its own job:

- **姚扣根 (Yao Kougen), 《电视剧写作概论》 (Introduction to Television Drama Writing)** (Shanghai Theatre Academy screenwriting textbook series; Shanghai People's Press, January 2016, 1st edition, revised and enlarged) = **the academic system**: the triple character of television drama, typology, four script formats, structural laws at the episode unit, the four levels of suspense, scene theory, and the 36 situations.
- **张巍 (Zhang Wei) et al., 《电视剧改编教程》 (A Course in Television Drama Adaptation)** (China Film Press, September 2014, 1st edition) = **the adaptation craft**: the five-source typology, 卖点迁移 (màidiǎn qiānyí — sell-point migration), the thematic coordinate system, segment-by-segment copy / delete / adapt, and six episode-by-episode, scene-by-scene case studies.
- **张明智 (Zhang Mingzhi) and 宋培义 (Song Peiyi), 《电视剧出品人与制片人教程》 (A Course for Television Drama Presenters and Producers)** (an industry training textbook of the Television Producers Committee of the China Federation of Radio and Television Associations; China Radio, Film and Television Press, October 2016, 1st edition) = **the production chain**: the four forms of a script and 戏眼 (xìyǎn — the one-line core attraction of an episode), hard running-time data, eight administrative systems, two stages of censorship, the 分场景表 (fēn chǎngjǐng biǎo — scene breakdown sheet), contracts, and copyright.
- **赵彬彬 (Zhao Binbin), 《影视剧片段改编教程》 (A Course in Adapting Film and Television Fragments)** (China Film Press, January 2016) = **fragment adaptation**: the **compression** craft — finished film → an 8–20 minute stage fragment — the opposite direction from Zhang Wei's **expansion**.
- **The Chinese translation of Douglas's 《美剧编剧入门》 (Writing the TV Drama Series)** (translated by 严敏 (Yan Min); Shanghai Joint Publishing, April 2009 = the English 2nd edition) and **the Chinese translation of Blum's 《电视与银幕写作》 (Television and Screen Writing)** (Huaxia Publishing House, 2003) = **terminology**: the official Chinese renderings set against today's common renderings.

The American methods themselves are not here: for episode-level act structure see `sw-series-structure`; for the engine and bible see `sw-series-engine-bible`; for the writers' room see `sw-writers-room`; for the 场号制 (chǎnghào zhì — numbered-scene convention) layout specification see `sw-format-adaptation` Section II.2; for general dialogue method see `sw-dialogue`; for American series case studies see `sw-series-case-studies`. This skill covers only “how mainland series do it themselves” and “how they correspond to and differ from the American system.”

For format samples, planning-document samples, single-episode scene tables, the lists of 36 situations / 36 comic situations, and the terminology dictionary, see [reference.md](reference.md) in this directory (Sections 1–12); for the comparison tables of the six adaptation cases, the index of 26 fragments, and the red-line classification table, see [reference-cases.md](reference-cases.md) (Sections 13–15).

⚠️ Policies, institution names, fee rates, and the platform landscape are all dated to the year of the source book (2014 / 2015 / 2016); verify against the current documents before citing.

## I. Table of Correspondences and Differences Between the Mainland Chinese and American Series Systems

| Issue | Mainland Chinese series | American orthodoxy | Which to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structural unit | **Episode / segment / scene.** “Drama needs division into scenes and acts, film needs division into shots and scenes, **television drama needs division into scenes, segments, and episodes**” (Yao p0188) | act + act out | For documents going to Chinese platforms, use episode–segment–scene; when pitching to the American side, prepare a separate act table |
| The lineage of the end-of-episode closure | **Li Yu's 《闲情偶寄》 (Casual Expressions of Idle Feeling), “收煞” (shōushā — the closing hook)**: “hold the situation for now, taper off the gongs and drums, **leaving the audience to wonder what comes next, not knowing how this matter will end**” (Yao p0260) + Song-dynasty storytelling's “listen to the next chapter for the answer” + Dickens's weekly serialization + the end-of-episode narration of radio drama (Yao p0272, p0044) | act out comes from the radio–television advertising system | To explain “why an episode must close,” use the Chinese lineage; to calculate page anchors, use the American one |
| Repetition | **In a long work, “repetition rises to a technique of an essential kind”** (Yao p0313): all four kinds — pattern / plot / detail / opening-and-ending — are encouraged | information economy; repetition = wasted space | For long series of 30+ episodes broadcast two a day, use the former; for 10–13 episode short series, use the latter |
| Protagonist agency | **A passive protagonist is allowed**: “always passive, **yet holding on to a desire within that passivity**” (Yao p0179, 《阿信》 (Oshin)); endurance, self-justification, and panic all count as counter-action | a passive protagonist = a structural flaw | For a female saga of suffering and growth, or a family-ethics drama, the former works — but she must be given “one active action within each episode's passivity” |
| Season shape | **The closed long-form series**: “Chinese television dramas are basically all shot and produced in full, then broadcast complete in one go on the television platform, **so the story is more complete and the structure more rigorous**” (Zhang Wei p0043) | open engine + season-finale hook, designed for unlimited renewal | Satellite-broadcast series are always closed; web series or projects with planned sequels may be exceptions (the TV version of 《神话》 (The Myth) kept an open ending; Zhang Wei p0211) |
| Bottle episode | **Rejected.** A rejection Zhang Wei experienced firsthand: one episode “had only two characters, and neither was the protagonist... **it obviously made anyone who looked at it feel it was very unprofessional and very non-standard**” (p0035) | a legitimate means of saving money and achieving depth | Mainland projects instead use “block-shooting several episodes in the same set” to cut costs (see [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7) |
| Episodes in which the protagonist is absent | **Forbidden**: “The protagonist must appear in every episode and have sufficiently ample screen time” (Zhang Wei p0035) | ensemble rotation episodes are legitimate | — |
| Dialogue density | **A moderate redundancy is an industrial requirement**: viewers “often may have many accompanying activities of daily life... **so that when they occasionally leave the television set they do not easily lose the main information**” (Zhang Mingzhi p0055); “**action cannot be greater than language; it must assist language, and even be appropriately subordinate to language**” (Zhang Wei p0046) | dialogue economy; action greater than language | For long television series use the former; for short web series and film-oriented work use the latter. For general method see `sw-dialogue` |<br>⚠️ **Split this into two halves**: the **volume** (dialogue at 50% or more of the total, 16,000–18,000 characters per episode) is a hard constraint, **but it holds only for 45-minute satellite-broadcast long series**; **the hierarchy “action is subordinate to language” is a conclusion drawn in the 2014 context of two episodes a day, when viewers would leave the television set** — it need not be followed when the first run goes to a video platform (where viewers can rewatch and rewind), or negotiation scenes will be written as people reciting lines.
| The stability of theme | **“The highest standard for reviewing film and television drama is not the state apparatus but the audience”** (Zhang Wei p0026); the theme must be fully equipped with an enlightener + a reward-and-punishment mechanism + a protagonist who does not wander off topic (p0232–0233) | theme may be ironic, suspended, morally ambiguous | For satellite-broadcast series aimed at the broadest audience, use the former |
| “Enter late, leave early” | **Same name, opposite meaning**: Yao's “enter late” = **first lay the groundwork and build the atmosphere, let the audience become familiar with the setting and accumulate expectation, then light the fuse** (p0367–0368) | enter late = skip the setup and go straight into conflict | For a long series with 25–35 scenes per episode, use Yao's version; for a 45–60 page American episode, use the American version. **Ruling (the interface with the next row, “no tracing things to their roots”): Yao's “setup” must be carried by action that is currently happening, not by exposition** — you may enter slowly, but you may not explain the backstory first; build atmosphere out of what the characters are doing at this moment, and the two rows no longer clash |
| Opening | **No tracing things to their roots**: “complicated causal backstory must be abandoned and avoided; **the opening is often wind blowing and rain falling: the event is already happening**... all necessary explanation must be inserted wherever a gap opens in the developing event” (Yao p0044–0045) | the pilot must establish the world and the characters | A mainland Episode 1 must do both, completing the establishment by “inserting it into the gaps of the event” |
| Unresolved threads | **Legitimate**: in a network structure, “many threads start and stop, **with heads but no tails**,” and still do their work (Yao p0286) | every line must have a payoff | — |
| Final act / episode end | **At the end of every episode you must sell another big 关子 (guānzi — a suspense hook at the end of a segment)**; closing one hook opens a new 扣子 (kòuzi — a suspense hook within a segment) (Yao p0268–0272). ⚠️ **Half-hour comedy exception**: the disagreement table in `sw-sitcom-comedy` gives the test as “commercial positions → an act out is mandatory; no commercials + emotional-stakes type → an earned emotional moment can substitute.” A Chinese-language, ad-free, emotional half-hour comedy falls under both this row and that row — **follow that row, but still record in the episode outline one debt for this episode, designated to be paid off in a later episode** (see the ledger-style unit in `sw-series-engine-bible`), so that “no hook” does not mean “no reason for the next episode.” | **The final act only does pay-off and introduces no new information** (`sw-series-structure` diagnostic item 15) | **Direct conflict; choose by medium**: individual mainland episodes always follow the mainland rule; the American item applies only to the season finale. That item in `sw-series-structure` has already been annotated. |
| Overall principle | **“Film is written for classic status; television drama is written for the ratings”** (Yao p0042) | showrunner auteur theory | This is the basis for Yao's “popularity, entertainment value, fashionableness, and maximum spatial coverage” |

Three addenda: on the **fake cliffhanger**, Yao frankly admits that “sometimes **a feint is better than none**, because at least there is suspense” (p0272), while the American view treats it as cheating the audience; on the **technical boundary between serial and series**, Yao divides them by “whether a scene has an obvious ending” — with an obvious ending, it “cannot generate the next wave of wind and waves... **and thus turns the serial into a series**” (p0252); on the **ready-made material bank**, Yao directly offers Polti's 36 situations and 李天济 (Li Tianji)'s 36 comic situations and says outright that they “can still be borrowed” (p0207), whereas the American view distrusts formulas.

### Before I.2: How to Convert “Pages” in a Chinese Script (the conversion principle has moved to `sw-format-adaptation` II.2, since it is medium-agnostic; only the series-specific numbers remain here)

Chinese scripts have no page standard, only a character-count standard, yet clients, platforms, and various writing guides all speak in “pages.” This pack uses the following chain for conversion; when using it, you must state that these are converted pages:

1. One American page ≈ one minute (this comes from American layout, not Chinese). So “N pages” should first be read as “**N minutes of screen drama**.”
2. There are two sources for the Chinese character count per minute: a series episode of 40–45 minutes runs about 12,000–15,000 characters (i.e. **300–370 characters per minute**); a dialogue-driven episode runs about 18,000 characters per episode (**about 400 characters per minute**), while a heavily action-driven one runs 12,000–15,000 characters per episode (**about 270–330 characters per minute**).
3. Choose the value: **370–400 characters per minute for a dialogue-driven long series; 300–330 characters per minute for action-and-reaction-heavy work (single-camera comedy, suspense, action).**

**Convert by running time, not by applying a long series' numbers to every episode count**: a 28-minute half-hour show at 330 characters per minute comes to about **9,200 characters per episode**, not 16,000–18,000; a 12-minute short series comes to about 4,000 characters. When you write “about N pages,” state the basis for the conversion (character count ÷ characters per minute = converted pages), or the same draft will be read as twice the length under two different ideas of a “page.”

### I.2. “One Case, N Episodes” Is a Spectrum, Not a Yes/No Question

Yao's boundary (an obvious ending to the scene → the serial becomes a series, p0252) **is a warning about a cost, not a prohibition**. Mainland long series actually fall anywhere on this spectrum; the variable is simply “how many episodes one case / one event occupies”:

| Episodes per case | Form | What you gain | Cost | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Procedural (series) | You can enter at any episode; reruns and second-run sales are friendly; easy to cut into clips | Characters do not accumulate; by around Episode 12 the audience begins to find it repetitive | Sitcom, workplace procedural, daily broadcast |
| 2–3 | Serialized procedural (the mainstream mainland long series) | A complete satisfaction every 2–3 episodes, while still holding on to character change | 24 episodes can hold only about ten cases; the subject looks under-mined | Workplace drama, police procedural, period case drama |
| 4–6 | Block form (one movement, one major event) | Each block can be developed to its full depth; large emotional capacity | The first two episodes are slow — exactly where platforms most fear that “the first three episodes won't hold viewers” | Family drama, ensemble drama, adaptation of a long novel |
| The whole season as one case | Pure serial | Maximum tension | No single episode stands alone; reruns and second-run sales suffer | Miniseries, short-run series |

**Three criteria for choosing a point**: ① if the platform wants second-run and rerun value, lean toward 1–3 episodes; ② if the subject's sources of dispute/cases are limited (count how many types there are at most), lean toward 4–6 episodes when the sources are few, or by Episode 8 the seams will show; ③ how much space the protagonist's change needs — do not put a slowly changing character into one case per episode.

**Two legitimate hybrid methods**:
- **Ledger style** (recommended for the 2–3 episode tier): the case still closes as usual, but each unit leaves one debt designated for a later episode to pay off, so “an obvious ending” no longer equals “no next wave of wind and waves” — this neatly sidesteps the cost Yao warned about. See item 18 of `sw-series-engine-bible`.
- **Three-track system**: Case A closes within the episode, Case B runs across 3–6 episodes, and Line C runs through the whole season, all three running at once. The cost is that every episode must serve three rhythms at the same time, and the episode outline must mark, episode by episode, how far each track has gone.

**What not to do**: say nothing in the proposal. If you do not choose a point on the spectrum, the 24 episodes you write will be half procedural and half serial, and the platform's first read will ask, “What kind of show is this, exactly?”

## II. Hard Constraints on Scale and Running Time

- **Episode count**: Zhang Wei p0033–0034: “total length is generally between **20 and 60 episodes**. Television serials under 20 episodes are currently very rare,” with each episode about **40 minutes**; Zhang Mingzhi p0055: “Chinese television dramas are currently generally **30–50 episodes** long.” Prime time broadcasts **two episodes a day** (a convention that began with 《孽债》 (Sinful Debt); Zhang Wei p0017). **Precondition: 20–60 episodes is the market range for satellite-broadcast daily series** (a figure supported jointly by two-a-day broadcasting, per-episode pay, and advertising schedules), not a universal law of series; **streaming short-run series (12 / 16 / 24 episodes, released all at once or in batches) do not apply** — for those, argue episode count by the “engine loses pressure” argument in item 10 of `sw-series-engine-bible`: write as many episodes as the engine can supply without repeating itself; if that is fewer than 20, write that many anyway; do not pad to fill the range.
- **Episode running time** (Zhang Mingzhi p0048): **total length 45 minutes, with the main program no shorter than 41 minutes 30 seconds; the opening titles usually 60–90 seconds, the closing credits 120–150 seconds.** → **Write an episode to 41.5 minutes, not 45**, and leave room for the “next-episode preview” (p0143). **Precondition: this is the technical delivery standard for satellite broadcast** (the time taken by titles, credits, and advertising slots is hard). **Streaming leaves no slot for a next-episode preview**, and the main-program length is set by the platform itself, often floating between 35 and 50 minutes; in that case 41.5 serves only as a **writing target** (to ensure the episode's capacity is genuine), delivery follows the running-time field the platform gives, and you should not write “leave a preview slot” into a streaming episode's ending.
- **Character count** (Zhang Wei p0076–0077): **a 30-episode script is at least 400,000 characters, with dialogue mostly taking more than 50% of the total capacity**; popular novels on the market mostly run 150,000–300,000 characters, so “the vast majority of adaptation work is not simply dividing the novel into scenes and shooting it, but a wholly new act of creation.”
- **Episode count is variable**: contracts state that “the episode count is subject to the final approval of the State Administration of Press, Publication, Radio, Film and Television,” and adding episodes = adding investment proportionally (Zhang Mingzhi p0302; the institution's name as of 2016). Screenwriter pay is calculated per episode (Table 7-1, p0146) — this is the economic structure behind “padding.”

The triple character of television drama (dramatic art + moving-image art + the television medium) and its typology (six classification schemes, with complete entries for the seven types: single-episode drama / series / medium-length serial / long serial / documentary drama / sitcom / studio drama) are in [reference.md](reference.md) Section 1.

## III. The Document Chain

There is no single document chain for mainland series; **three coexist**, and before you put pen to paper you must ask clearly which one the other side uses.

> **The client's format requirements take precedence over every convention in this skill.** The three chains below, the line format of the episode outline, and the per-episode character-count and scene-count ranges are all **defaults**, not hard constraints. If the other side gives you a page count, character count, column set, or template, follow theirs; this skill's conventions serve only to fill the blanks they have not specified. A typical conflict: the client asks for “one 100-character paragraph per episode,” while this skill's episode-outline convention requires three things — 戏眼 (xìyǎn) + event + ending state. In that case compress the three, do not exceed the character count, and save the expanded version separately for your own use (see “Who is this page for?” in the diagnostic checklist of `sw-series-engine-bible`).

**A. Yao Kougen (academic/planning register, p0328–0348)**: three systems of script development = adapted script / commissioned work / speculative script (spec). Adaptation sources divide again into “**traditional classics**” (传统经典, chuántǒng jīngdiǎn — a diachronic, vertical combination: classic novels, plays, films) and “**group-selected classics**” (群选经典, qúnxuǎn jīngdiǎn — a synchronic, horizontal combination: web novels, comics, games, “selected by accumulating numbers through voting, clicks, purchases, and reading and viewing”). The planning documents = **plot summary + character design + main locations + creative statement**, plus a **sample script**:

For a comparison table of the length and required contents of the six documents (plot summary / character design / main locations / creative statement / plot flow / sample script), see [reference.md](reference.md) Section 2.

⚠️ **Yao's book does not use “episode outline / scene outline”** (its chain is story synopsis → sample script → full script); “character biography” appears on p0147 as “a character's event biography.”

**B. Zhang Mingzhi (producer-side review register, p0067)**: **① story outline** (time, place, main characters, character relationships, plot development; “one can fully judge whether the story has a distinctive flash of brilliance, whether it has novelty”) → **② 分集梗概 (fěnjí gěnggài — per-episode synopsis)** (“one can see the whole work's chapter structure, the 起承转合 (qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé — the four-part opening–development–turn–resolution pattern) of its conflicts, and **each episode's ‘戏眼’**”; when several people write together, it “**carries all the more importance for holding the whole work together**”) → **③ 分集剧本 (fěnjí jùběn — single-episode script)** (“its main content is a small amount of plot exposition and **a large amount of actor dialogue**”; “**one can even compile the television shooting budget from it**”) → **④ 分镜头本 (fēn jìngtóu běn — director's shot-by-shot script)** (⚠️ “in current Chinese television production, usually because the production schedule is tight and the story volume large, **very few directors make a formal shot-by-shot script**”).
→ **“戏眼” is the core word in the review of a Chinese per-episode synopsis: every episode must have a 戏眼 identifiable in one sentence.** The budget is calculated from the episode scripts; change the script and the budget moves.

**C. Contract register** (《聘请电影（电视剧）编剧合同》 — Contract for Hiring a Film (Television) Screenwriter, clause 1.4, p0330): **script idea → master outline and character biographies → per-episode synopsis → first draft of Episodes N through M (may be delivered and accepted in batches) → the script's overall final draft**. 《电视剧剧本委托创作合同》 (Television Drama Script Commissioned Writing Contract) (p0318) uses another chain: **story outline → episode outline → first draft → second draft → final draft before the start of shooting**, corresponding to payment in six installments (see [reference.md](reference.md) Section 7).

⚠️ **“Scene outline” appears in this batch of sources only in Zhang Mingzhi's description of the American writers' room** (p0060; it is the Chinese rendering of *outline*), and is **not a standard mainland deliverable**. The mainland equivalent is the **分场景表** — the scene breakdown sheet, a production document prepared by the script supervisor after the final draft (see [reference.md](reference.md) Section 3).

## V. Structural Laws at the Episode Unit

### 5.1 The Single-Episode Skeleton: 3 Minutes + 15 Minutes + Episode End (Yao p0259, quoted as written)

> The opening three minutes must grab the audience; then every 15 minutes comes a small 扣子 (kòuzi — suspense hook), and at the end of each episode you sell a big 关子 (guānzi — suspense hook)... As for needing a suspense every 15 minutes, **in the past this was for inserting commercials... now it is a matter of the segmentation within the episode: taking 15 minutes as one plot point and building what is called “片断戏剧结构 (piànduàn xìjù jiégòu — fragmentary dramatic structure)”**. That is, **every segment must have a “local vivid point,” and you must from time to time tighten the kite string you have let out**, raising one small climax after another... a savage event is about to explode and send flesh and blood flying... sorry, that will have to wait until tomorrow.

**The key: in the ad-free era the 15-minute node was retained and reinterpreted as an intrinsic need of “fragmentary dramatic structure.”** Hard rule: “**every 15 minutes is one segment, and this segmentation and the 45 minutes of each episode remain basically fixed**... at most there is some flexibility of a minute up or down” (p0272). Production reality: “sometimes... according to the director's taste, the plot is expanded or shrunk at will, so that the segment ending is moved earlier or dragged later. **But once this is done, it destroys the segment's suspense and the episode-end suspense and hurts the ratings. A strict producer generally will not agree to it.**”

**Segment-boundary check (run it again after marking the segments)**: the 15 minutes gives only a **position**, not a **reason**. Every segment boundary must land on a **transfer of information or power**; verify it against the three tests for an invisible act in `succession-series-writing` II, S1 — ① whether the story day changes, ② whether the location or mode of transport switches, ③ whether the core question turns; at least one of the three must hit. If none hits, it is a fake boundary cut by the clock, so move it to the nearest real transfer. When streaming has no ad slots, this step replaces the stopwatch.

### 5.2 The Four Levels of Suspense (Yao p0270–0275)

1. **The series' overarching suspense**: “several small suspense points interlock to form the overarching suspense, and the unveiling of the overarching suspense depends on the resolution of one small suspense point after another.” Two ways of posing it: state it directly and then extend it into branches (《雍正王朝》 (Yongzheng Dynasty): the treasury is empty → fundraising in Jiangnan → recovering the embezzled treasury silver); or **bring forward the main suspense before it has appeared, then go back and narrate how this situation came about** (《还珠格格》 (My Fair Princess): Part One's “the real and the fake princess,” Part Two's “hunting down the princess”). The overarching suspense of a series = **“the endless attraction and rejection between the sexes”** (《神探亨特》 (Hunter), 《大饭店》 (Hotel), 《编辑部的故事》 (Stories from the Editorial Board): “the two can never get to the point of talking marriage; **the author keeps blocking the audience's expectation that they will finally end up together**,” p0271).
2. **Episode-end suspense** (⚠️ **a series released all at once needs a different justification**; **when a platform and a satellite channel broadcast simultaneously (the most common mainland case), both justifications must be given** — the satellite side has an overnight gap, the platform side does not, so the episode ending must be both a real 关子 (for viewers following the broadcast) and a debt designated for later payment (for viewers watching straight through). The test: **first ask which side premieres the show**; the premiering side decides which justification is primary. The mechanism of this rule is the “gap” created by day-by-day broadcast, while when a streaming service releases a whole season the viewer clicks the next episode three seconds later — **that gap does not physically exist**. Even then you must still leave an open ending, but the basis changes to “**leave a debt to be paid**” — what this episode resolves must be paid off in a designated later episode, see the ledger-style unit in `sw-series-engine-bible`; **stop expecting “overnight wondering” to sustain the tension for you**, because when watched straight through a weak ending propped up by suspense is exposed at once): the mechanism is the “**gap**” — “the time between episodes forms a gap between the two scenes of the plot... a skillful interruption of the narrative creates an extended tension... **submitting the conflict to the audience actually leaves the audience room to think and guess**.”
3. **Segment suspense** (every 15 minutes): also an opportunity for a transition. The multi-line target: “**keep as many as five or six storylines running at the same time**,” while still holding to “one problem” — “**whichever line it comes from, one problem must still be resolved before a new one can be introduced**.”
4. **Opening suspense**: its duties are the opposite of the five for stage drama/film (see Section I). For the opening paradigms of the four series (《人间四月天》 (April Rhapsody), 《阿信》 (Oshin), 《大明宫词》 (Palace of Desire), 《红色康乃馨》 (Red Carnation)), see the reference. The metaphor: “**dam the flow quickly, drawing the audience's attention away from real life or from the midst of other television information streams and toward the drama, the way a newspaper or magazine headline does.**”

**Four rules for constructing suspense** (p0262–0267): ① **ground it in character** — the time and space / events / character relationships in the situation must be made known to the audience; “**characters must win the audience's affection**... a stage play or film does not necessarily need a lovable character and a hateful one, **but television drama takes this very seriously**.” ② **travel with the situation** — an overarching suspense + a series of small suspense points; the diagnostic sentence: “**the reason some dramas cannot hold the audience is often not that the author failed to pose an overall suspense, but that there is no graduated series of small suspense points.**” ③ **use parallel montage to create an “ultimate limit”** — each time a suspense point is resolved, immediately supply a new one with a hard time limit, and **materialize the limit as a visible counter** (《红色康乃馨》 (Red Carnation): a file-copy progress bar vs. the steadily rising elevator floor indicator). ④ **the “one problem” configuration** — five requirements: bring it on stage as early as possible / reduce the number of threads / make it specific and definite / make it strong and forceful / introduce a new one the moment the previous one has just ended. The negative example = the 30-episode 《皇城根儿》 (At the Foot of the Imperial City) of 1993 (nine concurrent mysteries + four marriage-and-romance subplots; “only in the last three or four episodes does it dump the whole bag, leaving the audience ‘spun around’ and yelping”).

### 5.3 Five Techniques of Delay (Yao p0267–0268)

The beginner's typical mistake: “it is often **the bear picking corn: take one, drop one**.”

1. **Gather dark clouds over the character's head; make the disasters bigger and bigger** — “the more you solve the problem, the more tangled it becomes,” while the final deadline is already pressing close.
2. **Break off abruptly at a tense moment and leave it for later** — “**if the interval grows too long, bring it up again at the right moment. Although the narration breaks off, the situation must not weaken.**”
3. **Split one complete large action into small actions across several beats, giving each action a small suspense** — “the protagonist is bound to meet a great disaster on the way to the appointment, so on his way out the door, down the stairs, and along the road, set up all kinds of small problems; **only after solving these small problems can he meet the final big problem**.”
4. **Add plot material** — expand into social breadth and psychological depth; the conflict draws in new characters, and the new characters bring new events.
5. **Adjust the rhythm, tension and release** — place it within the contrasts of “**strong and weak (force), fast and slow (speed), large and small (amplitude), and solid and void**.”

The rhythm mnemonic (Yao's summary of 《24 小时》 (24), S2E1, p0205): “**When quiet, like a tree; when in action, like lightning and thunder; when driven by emotion, as loose and rambling as flowing water; when narrating events, concentrate superior forces the way the military does in an offensive.**”

### 5.4 The First Three Episodes (Zhang Wei p0035–0037)

“You must make sure to **establish the series' situation as early as possible, create the series' core conflict as early as possible, throw out the series' overarching suspense, and bring the protagonist on stage as early as possible**.” “In traditional thinking, Chinese aesthetics often emphasized keeping the good things for later. But now such a view is obviously out of date.”

**The submission-driven version** (same page): “the reviewer... has a great many films to watch every day... will certainly put his main energy and focus on the show's **first few episodes**... so even considering it purely from the angle of passing review, the adapter should put the most exciting part of the story... at the opening, **especially the first three episodes**.”

A companion rule: **a core character who only comes into force in the second half must show his face in Episode 1** (《马文的战争》 (Ma Wen's War) moved Li Qin's first appearance from “when Li Yi and Yang Xin marry” up to Episode 1, p0124).

For the table of quantitative per-episode indicators (scene count / plot points / main events / climax spacing / number of sudden reversals / character count), the three duties of a scene unit, and the double-line formula of career line + private-life line with examples, see [reference.md](reference.md) Section 4.

### 5.5 The Two-Step Situation Method and the Composite Principle (Yao ch. 9, the propulsion mechanism that replaces the “act”)

The propulsion mechanism of a long series (p0193): “at the opening, a television drama must **set up a situation** that becomes the driving force of the whole series... prompting the characters' inner activity to condense into concrete motives and lead to concrete action. From there, the main character's action pulls along other characters' actions, generating new conflict, forming new events, and producing **local changes in the situation**... advancing layer by layer toward the climax.”

- **The screenwriter's two-step method** (p0202): “**the first step is to find a way to set up a situation; the second is to let the characters stumble, crawl, and tumble inside that situation, always maintaining a state of action.**” — Yao's version of the series engine.
- **The action prescribed by the situation must repeat**: revenge situation → “the grudge cannot be easily untied; the act of revenge often has to be carried out again and again”; running-in situation → “a long process of both maintaining independence and merging”; misunderstanding situation → “once it is created, the more it is explained, the less clear it becomes.”
- **The composite principle** (p0203–0204, for long series only): **hang 4–5 situations of different types on the long-series protagonist at the same time** (in 《大宅门》 (The Grand Mansion Gate), the Second Madam carries a hereditary feud / the family fortune / an unruly son / competition from peers / the survival of the old brand), **and require at least one of them to transform midway** (“‘revenge’ turns into ‘pursuit,’ ‘running-in’ becomes ‘competition,’ ‘romance’ sometimes turns into ‘revenge’”).
- **突转 (tūzhuǎn — sudden reversal) is the device that replaces act out in mainland series** (p0210): “**because television drama emphasizes the alternating appearance of each action line and emphasizes creating a small climax on each action line in every episode, it uses the technique of sudden reversal more**... **the ‘sudden reversal’ of television drama is dispersed through almost every episode and every segment.**” Work backward: if the turn is toward the negative, first make the positive case to the full; and vice versa (p0218).

### 5.6 Structural Types (One Disagreement That Must Be Preserved)

- **Yao Kougen** (ch. 13): **dramatic / essayistic / psychological / 引戏员式 (yǐnxìyuán shì — narrator-presenter)** — four types standing side by side, the latter three not taking conflict as their skeleton. Two other classification schemes: by character arrangement (one person, one matter / one person, many matters, the “string of gourds” form / many people, one matter / many people, many matters, the “ensemble” and “exhibition” forms); by the way the plot unfolds (open / closed). Three thread types: single line / double line (main-and-sub or parallel) / network (“**a net has a main cord**... there is always a main line playing the governing role, though it is sometimes not obvious”).
- **Zhang Wei** (p0032, p0038): **“In television drama it is almost impossible for an essayistic structure to exist, let alone a psychological structure or a poetic structure. Nearly all the television dramas on the market belong, without exception, to the dramatic structure.”** Two basic kinds = chronological (applicable to “almost all television dramas”) and time-crossing (most common in time-travel material).
- **Which to use when**: for tribute dramas, platform commissions, and satellite-broadcast series of 30+ episodes, follow Zhang Wei (dramatic + chronological); for single-episode drama, documentary drama, auteur projects, and short web series, Yao's essayistic / psychological / narrator-presenter types may be used, but you must accept the risk of “loose structure and weak appeal” — Yao himself writes this (p0288).
- **Zhang Mingzhi's third set** (p0073, producer's angle): ① the evolving fate of one or several families (mansion drama); ② changes in one or several sets of character relationships (family melodrama); ③ the issuing and completion of a mission (cop drama, espionage drama); ④ a character's growth (wuxia drama, youth idol drama); “**the above structural types may be used in combination within one television drama**.”

## XI. Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Scale**: is the episode calculated at 41.5 minutes? 25–35 scenes, of which 4–5 are strong main scenes? (**Unit**: when diagnosing, compare the **scene numbers** on the scene breakdown sheet directly against this range; one scene unit usually corresponds to one numbered scene, and an occasional long scene unit may span two — do not convert first, and do not go looking up the theoretical distinction between “setting” and “scene”; that is a concept for writing, not a unit for measuring a draft. Handed an outline labeled “52 scenes,” it is 1.5 times the range.) About 13 plot points per episode? Does 30 episodes reach 400,000 characters, with dialogue at 50%?
2. **Episode skeleton**: is there something gripping in the first 3 minutes? Is there a “local vivid point” every 15 minutes? Is the big 关子 at the episode end a real hook or a feint (a feint is allowed, but do not feint every episode)?
3. **Opening**: does the first sentence write “something that is happening now,” or is it tracing back to origins? Is the explanation inserted into the gaps of the event?
4. **First three episodes**: have the situation, the core conflict, the overarching suspense, and the protagonist all been delivered within the first three episodes? Does a core character who only comes into force later show his face in Episode 1?
5. **Every episode**: is the protagonist present and given enough screen time? (**Exception**: the protagonist may be absent for an entire episode, but must satisfy the two preconditions in S7 of `succession-series-writing` — he is still exerting his will during the absence, and this episode is about his absence; and a stock of presence has already been built up. **The question to ask is “did action arise during the absence,” not “is he present.”**) Is there one main event? Is there any episode left with only a two-hander between two non-protagonist characters?
6. **Suspense**: is the overarching suspense one question or nine mysteries (the disease of 《皇城根儿》 (At the Foot of the Imperial City))? Is there a graduated series of small suspense points? Each time a suspense point is resolved, is a new one with a hard time limit immediately supplied, and is the limit materialized as a visible counter?
7. **Delay**: which of the five techniques are used? After leaving something hanging, is it brought up again when the interval grows too long? Is there “the bear picking corn” — a good pivot used once and then dropped?
8. **Situation**: how many situations are hung on the protagonist at once? Does one transform midway (revenge → pursuit, running-in → competition)? Has the action prescribed by the situation repeated enough times?
9. **Lines**: do the career line and the private-life line each have a goal, obstacles, and a climax? Are the two mutually causal? Does any subplot steal the show? Are there more than 5–6 parallel lines? Whichever line it is, does it hold to “resolve one problem before introducing a new one”?
10. **Repetition**: which are used — pattern repetition (a three-step pattern cycled once per episode), detail repetition (one prop running through the whole work), opening-and-ending repetition? Does the **content** of each repetition change (otherwise it is mechanical repetition)?
11. **Adaptation**: have you summarized the source in one sentence? Have you counted how many protagonists and how many lines it gives you, and whether that is enough to fill the target episode count? Have you made the segment-by-segment [copy / delete / adapt] table? Have you migrated the sell points (all the sell points specific to the source's audience replaced, the keywords retained)? Is the expansion done by the four methods or by padding? Have the new characters passed the “three questions”? Is there a pure-friendship line between new characters?
12. **Dual time frames**: are the switches sewn together by the same object / place / setting? Is any single line too long, or the intercutting too dense?
13. **Theme**: is there an enlightener (and not the protagonist)? Are actions that violate the theme punished and those that comply rewarded? Has the protagonist's motivation gone off topic?
14. **Format**: has one of the four formats been chosen and made consistent throughout the draft? Do scene headings mark place / interior-exterior / time / day-night? Are revisions marked with 19A/19B and the “66 deletion”?
15. **Scheduling**: are set names consistent throughout the draft? Are similar locations distinguished? Can day and night scenes in the same set be grouped? Have you written a batch of small scenes — “few actors, no special location, unaffected by weather” — for the contingency plan? Are there expensive props that must be destroyed, avoidable extras, or night scenes?
16. **Censorship**: have you gone item by item through the ten categories in Article 5 of the General Rules (2015)? Is the antagonist the main object of portrayal? Do you expose investigative methods and detection details? Is the line between good and evil clear? How are extramarital love affairs / one-night stands / abnormal sexual relations handled? Do you “reopen the verdict” on a historical figure whose judgment is settled, treat revolutionary history in a playful manner, or promote reincarnation / witchcraft?
17. **Expert review**: have you written the military, the armed police, state security, public security, the judiciary, doctors, lawyers, or minority languages and scripts, revolutionary pioneers, specific insignia, or the costume, makeup, and props of a specific historical period? Does the schedule leave time for expert review and revision?
18. **Delivery**: which document chain is the other side using (Yao-style proposal + sample / the producer's four forms / the contract delivery sequence)? At every stage, have you obtained written approval **signed by both the producer and the director**? Does the contract contain a review deadline and a default clause? Have you secured the restrictions on major changes (creative intent / main characters / main events / style), reversion of rights, remake permission, the three royalty tiers, and credit remedy?
19. **Start of shooting**: was the final draft completed before shooting began? Do you know whether the project has passed filing and been approved for project registration, and whether the financing is in place?
20. **Fragment adaptation (if working in the compression direction)**: has it passed the veto gate (it is not two people sitting and talking)? Has it been consolidated into one main setting + one transitional area? Is the cut-in point “the moment the motive has just come into being”? Is the ending your own?

## XII. Workflow: A Mainland Series from Planning to Submission

1. **Choose the subject** (Yao p0329, four principles): it is genuinely meaningful, of great interest, and **within your capacity**; you can command **rich first-hand material**; from a mass of phenomena you have **made a new discovery**; “**you are not creating for the sake of creating**.” In parallel, work through Zhang Mingzhi's three factors of market positioning (subject × audience × the creator's grasp of the subject) and goal-setting (social benefit first or economic benefit first), and make a judgment about “audience demand a year or even longer from now” (a television drama takes “at least a year or more from production to distribution and broadcast”).
2. **If adapting**: IP assessment (Zhao Junkai's three foundations + Zhang Mingzhi's two judgments) → confirm the adaptation rights and, if necessary, dual authorization → summarize the source in one sentence and count the number of protagonists and lines → the segment-by-segment [copy / delete / adapt] table → fix the thematic coordinate system → make the sell-point migration list.
3. **Fix genre and scale**: one main genre (it may be composite, but write only one in the project-approval document); episode count (20 / 30 / 40 / 50); 41.5 minutes per episode; estimate the total character count.
4. **Write the planning documents**: plot summary (or a 200–400 character segmented synopsis, each segment with one time anchor and one irreversible change at its end) + character design (three innovative points for the protagonist) + main locations (with location concentration already considered) + creative statement.
5. **File for the record / apply for project approval** (or do both in parallel): determine whether the subject falls under major revolutionary, major historical, or major theoretical-documentary material (the dual-review channel); subjects involving ethnicity or religion go separately through the Ethnic Affairs Commission / Religious Affairs Bureau; co-productions or foreign actors are reported to the Foreign Affairs Department. (As of 2016; verify against current documents.)
6. **Story outline → per-episode synopsis**: write one sentence of **戏眼** for each episode; lay out the first three episodes; lay out the four levels of suspense; mark one climax every 3–5 episodes and at least two sudden reversals across the series; hang 4–5 situations on the protagonist and arrange one situation transformation; lay the career line + private-life line. **Do not write any further until you have the producer's and director's dual signatures.**
7. **Character biographies and master outline**: psychological and spiritual content goes into the biographies, not into the episode scripts (Zhang Wei p0046). Give the party who needs to unburden himself a “**confidant**” (this may be a new character, or the enemy may carry it; Zhang Wei p0072–0073).
8. **Sample script (Episode 1 or the first two episodes)**: self-check against Yao's list of duties; choose one of the four formats and apply it consistently throughout; 25–35 scenes per episode.
9. **First draft in batches → second draft → final draft before shooting**: advance stage by stage through the contract gates; accept that “the script is revised until the final day of shooting” but write the additional payment into the contract; pass the script review meeting / seminar; where specific professions, groups, or costume, makeup, and props are involved, pass expert review and legal counsel.
10. **After the final draft**: the script supervisor breaks the script into a **scene breakdown sheet** and a scene-order table, which enter the block-shooting schedule; revisions use 19A/19B and the “66 deletion”; the creative script for product placement is finalized before shooting, with the screenwriter helping to realize it.
11. **Submission for review**: the finished film goes through content review + technical review, is revised according to the notes “until content review passes,” and may be distributed only after obtaining a Television Drama Distribution License. Know that the story structure will be moved once more on the editing table by the producer and the distributor.
12. **Going overseas (optional)** (Zhang Mingzhi p0201–0207): “international elements must be integrated from the production stage”; actively adapt to the buyer's requirements for program format, episode running time, and content rhythm; the standard way to take a long series overseas is “**to adapt the long serial into one or two television-film versions of about 100 minutes each**.”

## XIII. Exercises

1. **Copy the formats**: write the same 3-page scene once in each of Yao's four formats, and feel out which one a director understands fastest and which is easiest to publish.
2. **41.5-minute calibration**: take one broadcast episode of a mainland series, use a stopwatch to time the opening titles, closing credits, and main program, list its scene table (25–35 scenes), mark the 3-minute opening hook, the small 扣子 every 15 minutes, and the big 关子 at the episode end, then count whether the plot points come close to 13.
3. **The two-step situation method**: hang five situations of different types on one protagonist (revenge / running-in / misunderstanding / pursuit / competition), write for each “the action the character must repeat,” and designate in which episode one of them transforms.
4. **Sell-point migration**: pick any bestselling novel, list three sell points and the corresponding audience profiles, judge item by item which are ineffective for television audiences, and propose replacements (you must keep the source's keywords as the shopfront).
5. **The segment-by-segment method**: choose a 90–120 minute film, cut it into 8–12 segments by timecode, label each segment, and from the table write a 30-episode expansion plan (where each of the four methods is used, how many characters are added to how many, how many relationship lines are added to how many).
6. **Four-level suspense table**: make a table for a 30-episode series: one sentence for the overarching suspense; one sentence for each of the 30 episode-end suspense points; pick three episodes and write out three 15-minute nodes; which of the four series' opening paradigms Episode 1 uses.
7. **Red-line rewriting**: find a passage of a novel containing an extramarital affair, gray morality, or a feudal-superstition premise; rewrite it once for each of the six evasion techniques in Section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md), and record what each version loses.
8. **Reverse exercise**: turn the strongest scene in one of your own episodes into a 10-minute, 4-character stage fragment with a single main setting (write your own ending, in Zhao Binbin's format), then compare: what makes this scene work in the series, and what makes it work on stage.
9. **Scene breakdown sheet**: fill one of your own episode scripts by hand into the nine-field table, sort by set name + atmosphere, count how many sets, how many night scenes, and how many scenes with extras are needed, then go back and revise the script to cut the number of sets by a third without losing drama.
10. **Contract drill**: use the six screenwriter-contract items in Section 7 of [reference.md](reference.md) to compare against a real contract clause by clause, and list the clauses you would ask to change, with reasons.
