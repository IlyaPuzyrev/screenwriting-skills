# Chinese Domestic Series Practice (III): A Comparison of Six Adaptation Cases by Zhang Wei

> The reference materials for Chinese domestic series practice are divided into four volumes: this volume (I–VIII: genre taxonomy, planning documents, script format, per-episode metrics, adaptation, excerpt adaptation, the production chain and censorship, and a glossary) / [reference-samples.md](reference-samples.md) (IX–XII: four format samples, planning-document samples, a sample per-episode scene table, and 36 situations and comic situations) / [reference-cases.md](reference-cases.md) (XIII: Zhang Wei's six adaptation cases) / [reference-rules.md](reference-rules.md) (XIV–XV: an index of 26 excerpts from Zhao Binbin, and the red-line table from 《通则》 (General Rules)).

## XIII. Comparison Table of Zhang Wei's Six Adaptation Cases

The six cases are Examples 1 through 6 in Zhang Wei's 《电视剧改编教程》 (A Course in TV Drama Adaptation) (p0082–p0308), each demonstrating a different adaptation path. **Overview**:

| Case | Source work (length / type) | Series (episodes) | Adaptation path | Screenwriter (as credited in the book) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 《杜拉拉升职记》 (Du Lala's Promotion), bestselling workplace novel, under 200,000 characters | 30-episode urban drama | **Sell-point transfer** (卖点迁移, màidiǎn qiānyí): workplace bible → coming-of-age story | Teng Yang (Zhang Wei was a writer on the series) |
| 2 | Ye Zhaoyan's 《马文的战争》 (Ma Wen's War), a 30,000-character novella with six characters | 24-episode family-ethics drama | **Expansion by adding characters**: 5 → 11 | Zhang Wei, Hou Ying, Peng Peijun |
| 3 | The life of Mei Lanfang (a real celebrity) | 40-episode period drama (**not produced**) | **Adding a character trait + hybrid genre** | Teng Yang |
| 4 | The film 《神话》 (The Myth), 90 minutes | 50-episode time-travel drama | **Segment-by-segment copy/delete/adapt + genre inversion** | Zhang Wei, Liu Yu |
| 5 | Yan Geling and Xiao Ma's 《铁梨花》 (Iron Pear Blossom), a 160,000-character novel | 43-episode Republican-era women's legend drama | **Theme substitution**, "almost a clean break with the original novel" | Zhang Wei, Chen Qiongqiong |
| 6 | Du Liang's 《亮剑》 (Drawing Sword), a novel | 30-episode military drama | **Whole-pot transplant** (连锅端, liánguōduān) + truncating the timeline | Zhang Wei, Gao Boyang |

⚠️ **The warning attached to Example 3 must travel with the case**: according to item 231 in the reference filmography at the end of the book (p0318), this 40-episode television version of 《梅兰芳》 (Mei Lanfang) "**was not put into production for various reasons**" (screenwriters: Xiao □, Zhang Wei, Wang Qinan; director: Chen Guoxing). **This chapter analyzes a script that was completed but never broadcast—every "ratings consideration" in it is a hypothesis formed during writing, never tested by the market. It is the only one of the six cases with no broadcast data to back it.**

### 13.1 Example 1, 《杜拉拉升职记》 (Du Lala's Promotion): Novel → 30-Episode Urban Drama (Zhang Wei, p0082–0118)

- **Source work: length and type**: A bestselling workplace novel published in September 2007, which sold more than 100,000 copies that same year; in February 2008 Shanghai Media Group bought the adaptation rights (p0082). **The hard capacity data**: "**a plot capacity of under 200,000 characters is nowhere near enough for 30 episodes of television**… For adaptation work, therefore, **the more important task is not 'adapting' (改, gǎi) but 'constructing' (编, biān)**" (p0084). Series data: a peak rating of 6.46 in the Beijing region; more than 100 million total views in the first 14 days on one video platform (p0082).
- **Thematic coordinates**: The series switches tracks from "workplace bible / practical office philosophy" to "**the growth story of the protagonist, Du Lala—a girl who achieves personal growth through her career, a growth that includes promotion but, more importantly, a fresh understanding and elevation of responsibility, family, friendship, love, and even the self**" (p0086). The façade words are retained: "workplace," "white-collar," "foreign company," and "urban" all stay, "**with little subversive effect on the novel's theme or style**" (p0085). The coordinate-system metaphor (Figure 3, p0089): "**Theme and style determine the direction of a script on a given plane (X = style, Y = theme), while character relationships are the 'Z coordinate' that governs its volume**."
- **Major character surgery**:
  - **Daisy: ex-girlfriend → ex-wife** (p0097–0104; the finest piece of character surgery in the book). Diagnosis: "**In the novel, Daisy is like a boss in a video game: readers consume the process of Du Lala's confrontation with Daisy, not Daisy's characterization.**" The series works by the opposite mechanism: "**Television is exactly the reverse—viewers care about a character first and then care about that character's fate; only by consuming the character can they consume the derivative products of that fate, namely intricate plot.**" Effect of the surgery: "**it fixes a loose emotional relationship onto a solid social attribute**… this change of status gives a reasonable explanation for all of Daisy's mad behavior. At the same time, it is tantamount to adding obstacles to the course of Du Lala and Wang Wei's love." The audience-psychology basis: "**No one is wrong at their own starting point; the greatest conflict arises only when one person's starting point contradicts another's.**"
  - **Rose: ambiguous superior → an open, known rival who is both enemy and friend** (p0104–0107). The diagnosis of the protagonist's passivity: "Du Lala does not so much take the initiative as choose sides… **although this matches the early stage of most viewers' own careers, for a protagonist such a setup lacks agency**"; another risk: "**in the opening stretch of the series, Rose's screen time overshadows the protagonist**." After the rewrite, Rose is a "**good person who behaves badly**" (坏好人, huài hǎorén), against Daisy as a "**bad person who behaves well**" (好坏人, hǎo huàirén): "**on the one hand she suppresses Du Lala's promotion; on the other she nominates her as an outstanding employee**." The declaration-of-war line (script 14-13): "Being friends—that's fine. But in the workplace, it doesn't work!"
  - **New character Vivian (workplace idol / confidante)** (p0107–0110): not in the novel. The "**deferral–crossroads**" technique for managing expectation: "Once this life-mentor conversation is over, the audience's expectation of Vivian has shifted from deferral to standing at a crossroads: **if the next dramatic situation has Vivian making things hard for Du Lala, Vivian will be built into an out-and-out schemer; if Vivian helps Du Lala instead, then this emotional deferral will push the audience's affection for Vivian to its peak.**"
  - **New parents, plus Li Hongming (ex-boyfriend) bound to Xia Hong (best friend)** (p0089–0097): one reason (p0089)—this series "is necessarily different from the '**car, apartment, and dead parents**' (有车有房父母双亡, yǒu chē yǒu fáng fùmǔ shuāngwáng)… idol drama; **family ties or friendship ties are an important component that grounds Du Lala in reality and brings the audience close to her**." **Characters deleted: Wang Qiang** (p0097).
- **Structural handling**: The novel has only one through-line, the workplace, and "Du Lala's rise is mainly an **ascent**"; the series has three lines (workplace + romance + family), and it extends the workplace axis much further—"**Du Lala's promotion arc is changed from the novel's administrative supervisor to HR manager into a climb from complete novice to middle management**" (p0112). **The five suspense lines newly built for the series (p0113, item by item as in the source)**: ① Laid off from a private company and starting from zero at DB, can she get promoted? ② Betrayed by both her best friend and her ex-boyfriend, can she rekindle things with Li Hongming, and what becomes of Li Hongming and Xia Hong? ③ Having lied to her mother that she has bought an apartment, can she actually buy one of her own? ④ Her father has cancer—can she raise the money, and can he be cured? ⑤ Wang Wei has lost confidence in women after being hurt by his ex-wife Daisy—can he truly fall in love with Du Lala? The premise judgment: "**'A girl who wants a promotion' cannot constitute the suspense that makes viewers watch a television series.**" **The rhythm law (p0113–0114)**: "**Every 3 to 5 episodes brings an emotional or event peak; sometimes the two coincide.**"
- **Transferable techniques**:
  1. **First summarize the source in one sentence, and count how many protagonists and how many lines it gives you** (p0083).
  2. **Bringing a character on with drama (人物带戏出场, rénwù dài xì chūchǎng) = two linked scenes** (p0114–0117): the first establishes character and tosses out a small suspense (Daisy, in scene 1-20, buys bags at an LV store without looking at prices and pays with a supplementary card, while the sales assistants remark that she is "probably some rich man's mistress"); the second resolves the small suspense and tosses out a bigger one, and ends by hooking the character to a main-line figure (scene 1-21: her American boyfriend breaks up with her, Daisy hurls the LV bag at the television—and there on the screen is none other than Wang Wei receiving an award—**Daisy freezes**). The general principle: "**We cannot solve problems of visual presentation with adjectives and declarative sentences.**"
  3. **Land the theme by using one scene to block two escape routes** (script 30-19, p0086–0087): the first question, "Are you going to leave me?", blocks the escape route of "something has gone wrong with our feelings"; the second, "Can time change your decision?", creates the career/love dilemma. "**If she gave up everything for Wang Wei, she would be nothing but a woman desperate to marry who fought her way up only to land a wealthy, eligible bachelor. In the end, the Du Lala who chooses to walk away quietly is the Du Lala after 'promotion.'**"
  4. **The contradictory demands audiences place on IP adaptation** (p0117): "They want a series that **still looks like the original novel but must be more exciting than the original novel**, and they do not want adapters to make drastic changes to the fixed impression they have already formed of the original."

### 13.2 Example 2, 《马文的战争》 (Ma Wen's War): A 30,000-Character Novella → 24-Episode Family-Ethics Drama (Zhang Wei, p0119–0154)

- **Source work: length and type**: Ye Zhaoyan's novella of the same name, **30,000 characters with six characters in total**, winner of the 10th 《小说月报》 (Fiction Monthly) Hundred Flowers Award in 2003 (p0119). Why it was adaptable (p0120–0121, two reasons): ① "the story's unusual 'one wife, two husbands' relationship is very rare—**there is no precedent for it in Chinese series**"; ② "**limited by its length, the novel does not describe any character in excessive detail, which leaves ample room for television adaptation**." Series data: in October 2008 it premiered simultaneously on Beijing TV and Shanghai TV; its average rating in Shanghai was 2.46, making it the year's ratings champion; it was dubbed a "**marriage textbook**" (p0119–0120).
- **Thematic coordinates**: The novel "uses a story of extramarital love to confront the irresponsible, selfish, and cold attitude with which some people face marriage and love… **but for television it is too 'sharp'**"; the series "focuses on the marriage and love of middle-aged people… **television chose a gentler angle from which to express the theme; the emotional relationships of the main characters are preserved to the greatest extent, while sensitive elements such as the 'extramarital affair' are deleted**" (p0122). The positive theme = "**cherish the person in front of you**." **The differentiation sell-point formula (p0124)**: "In past marriage dramas, most told of couples going from harmony to estrangement; few told of divorce leading to reconciliation… **place the focus outside the besieged city; begin with the emotional entanglements of a couple who have walked out of the walls and show how life goes on after divorce. From inside the besieged city to outside it, examine marriage from an entirely new perspective.**"
  - **Policy constraints (an industry item)**: "**In 2006, SARFT (the State Administration of Radio, Film and Television) announced that it would begin rectifying family-ethics dramas; series touching on controversial subjects such as extramarital affairs would face certain restrictions when broadcast.**" (p0121) Three cuts made on this basis: all content involving extramarital affairs and "the third party" was **deleted outright**; Ma Wen and Yang Xin's divorce was changed to result from **hot-headed youthful impulsiveness**; Li Yi was rewritten as **a single man who is already divorced** (p0122).
- **Major character surgery**:
  - **A comparison table of identity changes (Table 3, p0137: the original table plus a "purpose of the change" column)**: Ma Wen, temporary gatekeeper → **software engineer** (raises the character's social level); Yang Xin, laid off and unemployed → **designer → PR → real-estate agent (salesperson → supervisor)** (every job change drives the plot and creates connections or conflicts with other characters); Li Yi, unmentioned → **designer** (creates an obstacle: colleagues cannot marry); Huang Xiaofen, runs a small eatery → **runs a law firm** (lawsuits, prenuptial agreements, property donations); Lin Zhifeng and Wang Dafei, **newly added → entrepreneurs** (broaden the social strata, create conflict). Three reasons: ① "**characters and subjects that are too marginal can hardly resonate with viewers**"; ② the novel was written around 2003, and the wave of layoffs "**would reduce the series' contemporary relevance**"; ③ if the original setting were followed, the main locations might be concentrated in **corridor apartment blocks (筒子楼, tǒngzi lóu)**, whereas "**careful attention to costumes and settings can make a series more pleasing to the eye**." The functional definition of identity design (p0136): "**For a family-ethics drama, the more complex the protagonist's identity and profession, the more it can attract viewers of different backgrounds and tastes.**"
  - **Character relationships grow from 5 to 11 people** (Figure 9 → Figure 10, p0137–0138): new characters Ma Hu (son), Sun Rong (Li Yi's ex-wife), Li Li (Sun Rong and Li Yi's daughter), Lin Zhifeng, Wang Dafei, and Yang's mother; Huang Xiaofen's identity changes from "Li Yi's friend" to "**Yang Xin's friend**"; Li Qin and Ma Wen are given a "**primary-school classmate**" relationship. Verdict: "'Adapting' accounts for only a small part of this; **it is mostly 'constructing.'**"
  - **The art of relocating material** (p0122): "The novel mentions that Li Qin once resisted marriage; the series gives this plot element to another character, Huang Xiaofen, Ma Wen's blind-date partner. **This is a small but very important adjustment that makes the personalities and emotional development of all three characters more plausible.**"
  - **Three conclusions about character adaptation** (Figure 12, p0151–0152): every character "**extends from the foundation of Ma Wen and Yang Xin**"; every character must have "meaning" for the plot ("**even next-generation characters like Ma Hu and Li Li influence the adults' emotional development**"); "**the old have the old's stubbornness, the young have the young's willfulness, and every character should influence the others in his or her own distinctive way**."
- **Structural handling**:
  - **One "obstacle" generates the entire plot—the "apartment" chain** (Table 2, p0132–0133).
  - **The scene chain of Episode 1 (Figure 8, p0127–0130, 10 scenes)**: ① Yang Xin and Li Yi discuss marriage at the office and part unhappily. ② Ma Wen dates a woman he met online at home; she discovers women's personal items in the apartment. ③ Yang Xin comes home and catches them, introduces herself as the ex-wife, and ruins the date; their son Ma Hu comes home and says both of them forgot the parent-teacher meeting. ④ Li Yi discusses marriage with his elder sister Li Qin, who refuses to let Yang Xin move in with him. ⑤ Li Yi agrees to marry; Yang Xin wants to discuss living together with Ma Wen after the wedding. ⑥ Ma Wen goes to Yang's mother's home to fix her computer; she hints that she hopes they will remarry. ⑦ Ma Wen comes home; the two recall how their divorce came about; Yang Xin announces she is remarrying, and Ma Wen is shocked and refuses to let Li Yi move in. ⑧ Ma Wen goes up to the roof to clear his head; Ma Hu thinks he is about to jump, Li Yi calls the police, and the onlookers make him lose his footing and fall. ⑨ Li Qin is displeased and unwilling to let Li Yi marry Yang Xin. ⑩ Ma Wen, injured from the fall and resting at home, gives Yang Xin's phone number to a reporter. **This episode accomplishes three things**: it sets up two sets of character relationships; it establishes the basic conflicts (the love triangle, Li Qin's prejudice, Yang's mother's fondness for Ma Wen); and it resolves the parts of the novel that could not be used (the peeping-at-her-bath scene is removed, and every trace of the extramarital affair is removed).
  - **A core character who only becomes important in the second half must still appear in Episode 1** (p0130): Li Qin "does not matter much in the first half of the series, but has a crucial effect on the plot in the second half"—in the novel she does not appear until Li Yi and Yang Xin marry.
  - **The programming logic of the opening** (p0126–0127): "Counting on the usual practice of most stations **airing two episodes** in prime time, a series' broadcast will run for about **half a month or even longer**, and **viewers often decide whether to keep watching after only one or two episodes**." The comparison example, 《金婚》 (Golden Wedding): "Although the first episode ends with a wedding, it leaves the audience a question: **how will two such different people hold hands through a lifetime?**… **Events proved it: on its first day on air, Golden Wedding's rating shot up to 8.78.**"
  - **The ending: open rather than a grand reunion** (p0123): "Both divorced couples come to regret their impulsive divorces, yet discover that it is 'already too late.' **Compared with simply having the two couples mend their broken mirrors, this slightly helpless open ending is more likely to make viewers reflect.**"
- **Transferable techniques**: ① **When the novel's opening cannot be used, say clearly why**—the novel opens with Ma Wen barging in while Yang Xin is bathing; "this detail, while deftly sketching a set of distorted relationships, also sets a blackly comic tone for the whole novel," **but "it works against the construction of 'Ma Wen,' the series' central character"** (p0127–0128); ② **give characters obstacles or misunderstandings so that domestic trivia becomes "meaningful"** (p0122); ③ an explicit warning against following trends (p0124): "**A series made by blindly following a trend often cannot withstand the test of time and viewers: ratings start high and fall low, or it is a commercial success but not a critical one.**"

### 13.3 Example 3, 《梅兰芳》 (Mei Lanfang): The Life of a Real Celebrity → 40-Episode Period Drama (Zhang Wei, p0155–0187) ⚠️ Not Produced

- **Source material: length and type**: the life of a real celebrity. The script was written by four screenwriters, "**using the concept of 'seasons,' with 10 episodes as one stage**," covering four stages: episodes 1–10, the young Mei Lanfang; 11–20, Mei Lanfang's association with the "Mei faction" (梅党, Méi dǎng); 21–30, the "**Mei–Meng romance**"; 31–40, Mei Lanfang during the War of Resistance. **This chapter analyzes the 10 "Mei–Meng romance" episodes written by Zhang Wei** (the designations 1-x through 10-x in the text are episode numbers within those 10 episodes).
- ⚠️ **This version "was not put into production for various reasons" (item 231 of the reference filmography, p0318).**
- **Thematic coordinates**: It takes **the third of three modes for adapting historical material** (p0157–0158)—"historical events and personal fate tightly interwoven and mutually driving, even to the point where the individual acts upon history": "**although the outcomes of major historical events remain consistent with historical reality, the process is elaborated, enriched, and made turbulent by the different actions of the male protagonist**." The criterion: "**The balance point between fact and story is the key to whether the audience believes the drama and accepts the script.**" Constraints from the producer (p0157): Huayi Brothers is a private listed company, "**which requires the adapter, when adapting, to consider ratings first**," and to "make necessary selections and adaptations of any plot that helps ratings but damages Mei Lanfang's image." Constraints from the rights holders: "**One must consider not only the preferences of the production company and the audience, but also the emotional needs of Mei Lanfang's descendants, and the legal disputes that extreme disagreement of opinion could cause.**"
- **Major character surgery**:
  - **Technique 1: composite fictionalization of historical figures—Yu Xiaolou** (p0158–0160).
  - **Technique 2: give a historical figure one new character trait—"cherishing talent" (惜才, xīcái)** (p0160, p0168–0170; the most brilliant design in the book). Three difficulties: ① these 10 episodes cover his prime, so a stage of character is needed ("**finding the different stages of Mei Lanfang's character development is the yardstick for inferring the logic of the character's behavior in this period**"); ② **the problem of gender temperament**—"his stage identity is feminized. **This feminized marker makes it very easy for refinement and modesty to appear to the audience as weakness**… so one must **find him a strong, powerful masculine trait**"; ③ the marriage problem. The effect: "the screenwriter extracts the trait of 'cherishing talent' and **sets it above modesty and refinement**… by defining it, the parts of Mei Lanfang's refined character that viewers might read as weakness are **flipped wholesale into wisdom, into the stature of a sage**." **How "cherishing talent" solves the moral minefield of the Mei–Meng romance (a chain of inferences, as in the source)**: "**Because he cherishes talent, Mei Lanfang rescues Meng Xiaodong from being harassed, and makes her fall hopelessly in love with him; because he cherishes talent, Mei Lanfang becomes her mentor on both the theatrical stage and the stage of life; and even more because he cherishes talent, when Meng Xiaodong is pressed into marriage, Mei Lanfang—to save her—speaks the lie that he is already engaged to her.**… **In this way the audience overlooks the element in this relationship that violates their values.**"
  - **Technique 5: a triangle with "no villain"—replace good-versus-evil with a difference of position** (p0176–0181).
  - **Fu Zhifang grows from the label "virtuous helpmeet" into a character in conflict** (p0161–0164): Zhu'er comes begging for help → Fu Zhifang takes it on—"I'll pay" → she fails to draw the money → she pulls off the jade betrothal bracelet Mei Lanfang gave her and prepares to pawn it. The line: "**Tell me—how much money did you take from them, that you would bury your daughter's lifelong happiness?**" The design note: "**As Mei Lanfang's wife, Fu Zhifang's characterization is also directly tied to Mei Lanfang's characterization.**" "**Emotion is pushed layer by layer, and even when Fu Zhifang takes off the bracelet, the suspense of whether Zhu'er can be saved is still unresolved.**" **The power to resolve is handed to the protagonist**: Mei Lanfang comes home with money to save the girl, "**making Mei Lanfang the resolver of the matter and indirectly showing his capability as head of the family**."
- **Structural handling**:
  - **Episode 1 opens with three lines advancing in parallel (Figure 13, p0161–0165, as in the source)**: **the Fu Zhifang line** = Zhu'er begs her for help → she fails to get the money → she painfully pawns the jade bracelet → fortunately her husband arrives in time and settles the crisis; **the Mei Lanfang line** = he tries every means to bring Yu Xiaolou back to the stage → celebrates Yu Xiaolou's success → goes home → encounters Meng Xiaodong and rescues her from trouble; **the Meng Xiaodong line** = she goes on stage and is booed → Wang Renxi humiliates her in public → to save a poor mother and child, she draws an advance on her salary from her master. The intersection of the lines: "**Fu and Meng rescue the same mother and child.**"
  - **Localizing the American-series ensemble approach (the book's clearest comparison of Chinese and foreign methods, as in the source)**: "**This kind of dramaturgy, in which different characters have independent action lines in an ensemble, appears mostly in American series. In this series the technique has been localized and made more suitable for Chinese viewers: Mei Lanfang's action line remains the clear main line**, while the other two action lines, with Fu Zhifang and Meng Xiaodong as supports, help to unfold characters and convey plot quickly and, more importantly, **supply the series with other genre elements**." → **Comparison note: the American-series ensemble consists of multiple equally weighted A/B/C stories; the book advocates a Chinese approach = one absolute main line + two supporting lines, whose function is to "bring genre elements in."**
  - **Technique 3: a hybrid genre solves a niche subject** (p0164–0167). The niche diagnosis: "Viewers who understand and enjoy opera are few and far between… **it would be less a television series than an opera television program**." The hybrid list: "In addition to family ethics and period legend, the section on Mei Lanfang growing a beard to show his resolve during the War of Resistance also adds the currently popular **espionage element**." The general claim: "**Adaptation work is not done once you have found a genre similar to the source text; where necessary, hybrid genre is the main means of making an adaptation reach more viewers.**"
- **Transferable techniques**:
  1. **A four-step method for adapting a celebrity's life (p0182; usable directly as a checklist)**: ① collect real materials and sources; ② **integrate the materials into a personal viewpoint**—"**collecting more material is not equivalent to being able to adapt better; a mass of miscellaneous information easily misleads the screenwriter. Therefore, in the early stage of reading, the adapter had best not select television material while reading. Only after studying all the material and forming a clear personal judgment of the subject should one establish characters on the basis of that judgment and then select useful material.**" ③ make reasonable inferences about missing plot—"**the truth of a character is built on plausible character, not on true events.**" ④ **select and discard material**—"**material that has been definitively settled need not all be used, and unofficial histories and rumors need not all be discarded. Any material that can plausibly serve the character can be used in adaptation.**"
  2. **A general rule: when a real person's established character cannot support his or her status as protagonist, extract a "higher-order virtue" from the historical record, set it above the rest, and make that virtue the motive source for every sensitive plot point.**
  3. **Technique 4: write forbidden love through the stage / a play-within-a-play** (p0172–0174). During rehearsal Mei Lanfang says to Meng Xiaodong: "Miss Meng, our present situation is nothing more than **a young married couple chatting at home**; why play it like a quarrel, at such a fast tempo?"
  4. **Fictional plot that "lands on historical fact"** (script 5-36/5-37, the family performs 《王宝钏》 (Wang Baochuan) at home, p0182–0184): after singing, Fu Zhifang says with a laugh, "This Wang Baochuan is a poor thing… **she waits in a cold cave for 18 years, and all she gets back is a man bringing a princess of the Western Liang kingdom home with him!**" "**The women of the household all laugh; Mei Lanfang savors the meaning in his wife's words and feels a chill of fright.**" Three purposes: "**Mei Lanfang performed Wang Baochuan when he toured America, so using this repertoire now lays groundwork for later; the content of Wang Baochuan also fits Fu Zhifang's situation at that moment. Using stage text to allude to reality is thus very clever.**"
  5. **A candid admission in the author's self-assessment** (p0182): adding "cherishing talent" plus the reintegration of three historical events is, "strictly speaking, a case of 'disrespecting history.' **But this is precisely what constitutes the real screen image of Mei Lanfang in the audience's eyes, precisely because the screenwriter holds tightly to Mei Lanfang's character: in every event, he thinks from Mei Lanfang's angle, does what Mei Lanfang would do, and thinks what Mei Lanfang would think.**"

### 13.4 Example 4, 《神话》 (The Myth): A 90-Minute Film → 50-Episode Time-Travel Drama (Zhang Wei, p0188–0227)

- **Source work: length and type**: the film 《神话》 (The Myth, 2005, starring Jackie Chan and Kim Hee-sun), with **a box office of nearly 100 million yuan**; "in 2005 China's total box office was only 2 billion yuan—The Myth ranked second at the year's box office" (p0188). Series: invested by Shanghai Film Yinghuang, premiered in prime time on CCTV-8 in early 2010, "**its per-episode rating broke 4 percentage points, and the series' average rating also set a record for CCTV's New Year flagship drama**." **The subject-selection judgment (a reusable logic of "an empty genre window")**: "By 2010… time travel should be a subject with considerable market potential and market space that was still uncultivated"; add the film's existing audience—"**if the film The Myth were planned as a television adaptation, it would mean gathering hundreds of millions of viewers before a single word was written**." And note the industry prejudice it broke (p0190): "There is an unwritten saying in television adaptation: **never adapt a work that has already succeeded**."
- **Thematic coordinates**: One of the four general rules in the conclusion (p0226)—"**one cannot use mechanical expansion; one should instead perform 'deep processing' aimed at the genres the television audience enjoys**, and, while keeping the main framework of the source material from drifting, transplant the series' expressive subject into a genre more readily accepted by viewers and properly belonging to television." The core is preserved at the character level (p0227): "**yet its most essential idea—loyalty to love and to the nation—runs through from beginning to end**; and the protagonist Yi Xiaochuan, across a long passage of time, gradually changes his character and finally grows into General Meng Yi of the film The Myth."
- **Major character surgery**:
  - **The three things that "must not be lost" in adaptation** (p0192–0193): ① **the essence of the original** ("the soulful gaze before the cliff," "Yushu's dance," "the anti-gravity heavenly soldiers in the celestial palace"—"**the looks, the movements, the shots are all replicated**"); ② **the main structure** (the ancient and modern lines finally converge at one point in the celestial palace; "**once changed, it would damage the sinews and bones and lose the entire time-travel feeling**"); ③ **important character relationships and identities** ("characters are vessels that carry emotion… **this is why the 'character biography' is usually born before the script**"). The warning about balance: "If one interprets the original too little or surpasses it too little, one will be accused of imitating it badly; if one ignores the original and innovates too much, one falls into subjective fabrication—too much is as bad as too little."
  - **Gao Yao → Zhao Gao: a reversal of nature** (synopsis, p0190–0191): "**Gao Yao, originally timid and afraid of trouble, is castrated into a eunuch, and his nature reverses**"; "If I'm to be a eunuch, I'll be the highest and greatest eunuch!" He searches bitterly for the historical Zhao Gao, who held sway over the whole court, hoping to attach himself to him, "**only to discover that the real 'Zhao Gao' is himself**."
  - **Number of characters and relationship lines** (Table 7, p0213); **two methods of complicating character relationships** (p0214–0215); **extending the time span of character fates** (p0215–0216), with the formula: "series running time / film running time = average character duration in the series / average character duration in the film."
- **Structural handling**:
  - **The segment-by-segment "copy / delete / adapt" comparison table (Table 4, p0191–0192)**—slicing the film by timecode and tagging each segment. Excerpts: `0–10'10"` [ancient] Meng Yi escorts the princess; on the cliff he saves her from a bolting horse → **【Copy】**; `10'10"–26'30"` [modern] Jack and William visit the anti-gravity temple of the Disha kingdom → **【Delete】** (the series keeps no Indian story; all four Indian-line segments of the film are deleted); `39'15"–48'25"` [ancient] Yushu sacrifices herself to save Meng Yi, falls in love through her dance, and is forced into the palace → **【Adapt】** (adds **Meng Tian's secret love**; **Yushu's flying dance is replicated after she enters the palace**); `93'06"–98'08"` [ancient] Meng Yi dies on the battlefield → **【Copy】** (adds "**by the grace of a miracle doctor and a magic elixir, he miraculously revives**"). **Method note: cutting the original by timecode and tagging each segment 【Copy】/【Delete】/【Adapt】 is the most directly applicable adaptation worksheet the book offers.**
  - **The inversion of the period-drama / contemporary-drama ratio (Figures 15 and 16, p0193–0194; the hardest set of data)**: **period material makes up only 40% of the film** (contemporary 60%); **in the series, period material accounts for 81.32%** (contemporary about 19%). The characterization: "**The film The Myth is an action-fantasy comedy intercut with period drama, while the series is a period drama intercut with contemporary footage. Fundamentally, the genre of the two works' key material is exactly reversed.**" Two reasons: ① **production cost**—"too many special effects cannot be used… **otherwise, for lack of suspense, it would turn into an urban romance or family-ethics drama about modern people with nothing to do and everything in a flurry**"; ② **viewing habits**—"**Chinese viewers have not yet developed the habit of watching modern fantasy or science fiction, which is why there is still no series like Battlestar Galactica**… **because there are many period genres, and many successful, mature genres to draw on**." → **A notable divergence from American-series methodology: American "high-concept" series often make the contemporary or science-fiction world their home turf (Lost, BSG); this book holds that a Chinese series must place its home turf in a time and space that already has mature genres.** And note its industry impact (p0226): "**Most time-travel series produced afterward have made period drama their main expressive subject. In this respect, the series The Myth contributed a great deal.**"
  - **Transition discipline between two time worlds** (p0194–0198): in the film, "neither line develops on its own for more than 20 minutes," and each is "roughly equal in length to the adjacent segment"; the series "has no fixed switching rule. Many consecutive episodes show only the period portion; **if viewers have not seen what came before, they easily mistake it for a pure period drama rather than a time-travel drama**." The stitching device must be the same object or the same place (the film uses a splash of water; the series uses a treasure box).
  - **The four-part segmentation-by-genre table (Table 10, p0216–0219; the book's most directly applicable template for segmenting a long series)**:

    | Episodes | Dominant genre | Conflict |
    |---|---|---|
    | Episodes 2–8 | **Personal growth** | **The difficulty of going home**: after time-traveling he befriends Xiang Yu and survives life-and-death trials; through a contest he sets out on the road home; he rescues the Lü family and wins the favor of the two Lü daughters; he introduces Liu Bang to Lü Zhi; he catches a plague and Lü Su saves him at the cost of her life; finally he and Gao Yao learn that it will take 60 years to return to the modern world |
    | Episodes 9–16 | **Love** | **The difficulty of love**: he is betrayed by Liu Bang; in Tuan he meets Yushu, and love grows through a contest; Yushu is married off as an imperial consort; Xiaochuan tries to take Yushu away, meets Meng Tian, and is recognized as his long-lost younger brother Meng Yi; Yushu enters the palace, and the two keep their feelings buried |
    | Episodes 17–41 | **Palace intrigue** | **The difficulty of palace life**: Gao Yao pursues the ambitions born of humiliation and uses Xiaoyue, who shares Gao Lan's face, to win Xiaochuan over; the power blocs of the two princes, Fusu and Huhai, take shape; Xiaochuan and Gao Yao serve different masters, and accumulated grievance turns them into mortal enemies |
    | Episodes 42–48 | **Historical war** | **The difficulty of conquering the realm**: Xiang Yu rises in rebellion; Xiaochuan's return from death galvanizes Liu Bang; the Chu–Han contention for supremacy; Xiang Yu cannot withstand Liu Bang; the 60-year deadline arrives |

    The conclusion (as in the source): "**The film's single period love story in three-act form has been adapted into a series of four relatively independent, hybrid-genre parts.**"
  - **Ending: the same outcome, different endings—closed vs. open** (Table 6, p0206–0211); the theoretical distinction between film suspense and series suspense (p0202–0204); the expansion of dialogue (Table 5, p0204–0206; **the only example of cross-media dialogue comparison**).
- **Transferable techniques**: ① **a defense of "vertical development"** (quoting Pamela Douglas, 《美剧编剧入门》 (Writing the TV Drama Series), p0218): "**When a character cannot develop horizontally toward his goal, he must develop vertically—that is, reveal his own inner conflict, which can likewise generate tension.**" Application: "**These single horizontal sources of drive cannot fill the plot. The screenwriter therefore deliberately gives the characters vertical development, and palace intrigue is undoubtedly the genre this series extends the furthest.**" ② **market data on hybrid genres** (p0219): "**Among the period dramas and family-ethics dramas that ranked in the top three in ratings across twelve major regions in 2010, the great majority added light-comedy elements**… **the great majority of traditional genre series have already become hybrid genres, and the driving force of this hybridization comes from the conflicts and confrontations within the characters' fate lines.**" ③ a general rule of subject selection (p0226): "If the original work has no value or no core worth adapting, **then adaptation is a superfluous exercise; better to create something original**."

### 13.5 Example 5, 《铁梨花》 (Iron Pear Blossom): A 160,000-Character Novel → 43-Episode Republican-Era Women's Legend Drama (Zhang Wei, p0228–0272)

- **Source work: length and type**: 《铁梨花》 (Iron Pear Blossom), co-written by Yan Geling and her father Yan Dunxun (pen name Xiao Ma), **160,000 characters**; "**it was originally a film script Yan Dunxun wrote for his wife, and only after Yan Geling and her father revised it did it take its present novel form**." The series aired in 2010 with an investment of more than 30 million yuan; "from its April premiere, by November, when more than half the episodes had aired, **the peak per-episode rating had broken 12%, and the highest single-day rating reached 11.7%**" (p0228). ⚠️ Episode counts in the source book's OCR are slightly inconsistent (the background section says "34 episodes," while the breakdown section mentions "episodes 42 and 43"); this table follows the episode numbers in the breakdown section.
- **Thematic coordinates**: **Theme substitution** (p0229–0240). The novel's theme (as in the source): "how a woman struggles painfully to establish an identity… **when she sees through to the precious moral code within the dishonorable identity of a tomb raider, she is finally able to face herself**." The series' theme (as in the source): "**as long as one has devotion to love, loyalty to brothers, and filial piety toward parents, then no matter how many flaws one has, how much injustice one suffers, how many setbacks one endures, one can surely save oneself, save others, and even save the entire nation.**" The summary: "If the novel promotes **faithfulness and righteousness** (信义, xìnyì), the kind shared with the tomb-raiding trade, then the series promotes the traditional **clan-style benevolence** (宗族式的仁爱, zōngzú shì de rén'ài)." The main line: "how Tie Lihua, a woman who does not love her husband, spends **25 years**—discovering, in the course of fleeing, her feelings for her husband Zhao Yuangeng, and after admitting that love in her heart, setting out on the road of departure once again."
  - **The second reason for the substitution (a technical reason, not a censorship reason; extremely important; as in the source)**: "**it is not conducive to maximizing conflict**… **the original novel is more like a one-woman show for Tie Lihua, a struggle between her and a 'greater environment.' Once the theme concerns love, loyalty, and filial piety, a web of reciprocal character relationships is immediately established—that is, love is mutual and requires a concrete object, and this object is obviously more concrete and perceptible than the 'greater environment,' which lays excellent groundwork for maximizing conflict.**"
  - **The overall judgment on the scale of adaptation** (p0228, as in the source): "In theme, character design, character relationships, structural rhythm, and plot direction, the series **makes almost a clean break with the original novel**; it avoids the tomb-raiding plot that would cause censorship problems and replaces it with the genre styles of mansion drama (宅门戏, zháimén xì), bandit-suppression drama, War of Resistance drama, and spy drama… **one story is expressed through many styles, yet this mode of expression never degenerates into showing off narrative technique and audiovisual spectacle; instead it stays tightly centered on character relationships and closely follows an inner family-ethics logic.**"
- **Major character surgery**:
  - **Four ways to "clear away the elements viewers dislike" from the protagonist** (p0243–0254): ① **remove "spirit communication" and turn the tomb-raider identity into a scam**; ② **add "the Liu family's broken engagement," turning her identity from a stain into victimhood**; ③ **two moves to defuse "killing her own child"** (in the novel Tie Lihua buries her son alive in his grandmother's grave); ④ **"make all the protagonist's love interests unreliable"** (p0253; a very unusual bonus technique). The author's **self-criticism** is appended (p0253–0254; a rare piece of self-diagnosis).
  - **Zhao Yuangeng: a four-stage value-escalation curve of "concealment and exposure" (藏与露, cáng yǔ lù)** (p0254–0261; the structural design most worth copying in this chapter). Functional positioning: in the theme-expression environment he is the "**enlightener**" (启悟者, qǐwùzhě); in the character relationships he is the "**judge**." **The difficulty created by combining the two (a theoretical warning, as in the source)**: "then this character has little room to grow and change, for since whatever he does is right, he has no shortcomings that need to be tempered and transformed… **and once Tie Lihua's opponent degrades into a flat character, the difficulty of her struggle against him drops… and the scenes of the two-person relationship will no longer be compelling.**" **The solution (as in the source)**: "**if the character's own transformation cannot form a progressive growth, then develop the character through progressive external presentation—through the skilled use of the relationship between 'concealment' and 'exposure.'**"
    - **Episodes 1–15 (vs. Tie Lihua; mansion drama)**: "**As the main character who serves as a foil to Tie Lihua, Zhao Yuangeng cannot be irredeemably guilty from the start. Otherwise it would create difficulties for his later transformation, and once he himself has too many stains, it would inevitably affect Tie Lihua, who comes to identify with his values.**" The recipe (this chapter's core technique, as in the source): "**In every scene that requires opposition to Tie Lihua, show his negative side in full; toward anyone who runs counter to the theme's values, he likewise metes out harsh punishment with extreme rigidity. In all other parts, show Zhao Yuangeng's merits—for example, his filial piety toward his mother, and his attitude of confining Qiuxiang without harming her.**"
    - **Episodes 16–26 (vs. Liang Feihu; bandit-suppression drama)**: "**Make him rigid in the theme's values and utterly blacken him in matters unrelated to the theme**"—"Zhao Yuangeng not only kills Liang Feihu's father but digs up his ancestral grave; **his motive is clearly filial piety, yet his rigidity goes too far**"; he abuses public authority to summon an artillery unit for private revenge; after his eight-year-old son is poisoned to death, he goes on a rampage of killing the innocent.
    - **Episodes 27–42 (vs. Pang Ya and Jing San; War of Resistance drama)**: "**The brilliance of this character, suppressed and hidden until now, is finally released in full**"—he saves Liang Feihu at the risk of his life, setting aside past grievances; he takes revenge on Jing San and fights the enemy with valor; toward Qiuxiang, who once betrayed him, he shows gratitude and tenderness—"**Zhao Yuangeng suddenly turns from a selfish, rigid warlord into a national hero of towering righteousness, free of petty scruples, bold and unrestrained**," and the character's positioning undergoes "**a 180-degree turn**."
    - **Episodes 42–43 (War of Liberation drama)**: the closing section.
  - **Qiuxiang: a "highlight character" who breaks the genre's shackles** (p0261–0267); **Niu Dan, Zhang Ji'an, and "the necessity of flat characters"** (p0267–0270).
  - **Ensemble treatment and "little man makes good" motives** (p0242): "The original novel focuses on Tie Lihua alone to highlight the theme, while **the series presents it through an ensemble**." All motives stay within the scope of family ethics: "**rather, they are guided by ordinary clan-style emotional motives and needs for affection and revenge**"—Tie Lihua joins the War of Resistance to avenge Liang Feihu and settle scores with Zhao Yuangeng; Niu Dan enlists to escape his unrequited "sister-in-law" Liu Feng'er. The genre positioning: "**they are not 'tainted heroes' but a group of 'ordinary people with heroic spirit'**," which "cleverly coincides with the protagonists of several war dramas that have been popular on screen in recent years, such as Li Yunlong in 《亮剑》 (Drawing Sword), Chang Fa in 《狼毒花》 (Wolf Poison Flower), and Shunliu in 《我的兄弟叫顺溜》 (My Brother Is Called Shunliu)."
- **Structural handling**:
  - **The main line in four stages** (p0271, as in the source): ① episodes 1–15: Zhao Yuangeng and Tie Lihua establish a connection and become a pair of feuding spouses; ② 16–26: Tie Lihua evades Zhao Yuangeng's pursuit and continues to evade her identity as the fifth mistress; ③ 27–41: amid national hatred and family vendetta, Tie Lihua draws closer to Zhao Yuangeng and takes the initiative in shouldering her family responsibilities; ④ 42–43: Tie Lihua admits her love for Zhao Yuangeng, yet walks away. The symmetry of the ending (a very fine structural observation): "**Structurally, it corresponds precisely to the first stage's 'seeming harmony, actual estrangement'—the ending is 'seeming estrangement, actual harmony' (貌离神合, mào lí shén hé).**"
  - **Main-line / sub-line segmentation table (p0230–0231, transcribed as in the source)**:

    | Part | Episodes | Style/genre | Main conflict | Sub-line conflict |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | 1 | 1–15 | **Mansion drama** | How Tie Lihua, whose heart belongs to Liu Tianci, tries every means to escape the Zhao family mansion | Within the extended family, where Zhao Yuangeng's mother holds authority, how the five mistresses compete for favor and for household power |
    | 2 | 16–26 | **Bandit-suppression drama** | After Xu Fengzhi (renamed "Tie Lihua" in this part) escapes the Zhao mansion, she makes a living on Tiger Mountain while searching for news of Liu Tianci | How Zhao Yuangeng and his hereditary enemy Liang Feihu fight each other |
    | 3 | 27–42 | **War of Resistance drama** | How Tie Lihua and the camp represented by Pang Ya contend over the Mandarin Duck Pillow ("the dramatic task of every character turns in unison to resisting Japan") | Zhao Yuangeng's conduct of the War of Resistance, and the changes in his relationships with Liang Feihu, Niu Dan, and others |
    | 4 | 42–43 | **War of Liberation drama** | Through Tie Lihua as a courier, a smokeless contest of wits between the PLA soldier Niu Dan and his warlord father Zhao Yuangeng | The mad Zhang Ji'an is generously forgiven by his daughter and son-in-law |

    The structural relationship (as in the source): "**The series breaks the novel's narrative method, which makes Tie Lihua the protagonist and point of view; it makes the changing two-person relationship between Tie Lihua and Zhao Yuangeng the main line, and the different conflicts of four different periods the sub-lines. The sub-lines constantly affect the development of the main line, and the two mesh very tightly.**"
  - **A three-layer solution for "packaging" a family-ethics drama with multiple genres** (p0270–0272, as in the source): "**In the internal logic of storytelling, it rigorously lays out and structures the story in the family-ethics style that traditional mainstream viewers are accustomed to; in structural style, it uses multiple genres to package a family story; and in rhythm, it uses dense plotting and an undulating tempo to align with the viewing habits of a new generation of television viewers.**"
  - **A self-diagnosis of the cost (as in the source)**: "**Call it a mansion drama, and it obviously cannot rival 《大宅门》 (The Grand Mansion Gate); call it a bandit-suppression drama, and it obviously cannot rival 《林海雪原》 (Tracks in the Snowy Forest); call it a war drama, and it obviously cannot rival 《亮剑》 (Drawing Sword). Precisely for this reason, which category of classic this excellent series belongs to has become an open question.**"
- **Transferable techniques** (this chapter offers three general tools found nowhere else):
  1. **The three elements of the "theme-expression environment"** (p0232–0233): the **enlightener** ("**usually cannot be set as the protagonist**… the protagonist usually cannot accept or understand all at once; only after going through a series of events and growing can the protagonist realize, identify with, surpass, or reject the values the enlightener told him back then") / the **reward-and-punishment mechanism** ("the theme is correct and just—but words alone prove nothing! There is only one situation that makes people believe it: **when we are punished for violating the values the theme sets and rewarded for complying with them**") / **a protagonist who stays on topic** ("**for example, if the story here is about whether women are independent, while over there the protagonist's motive has become national liberation, then the audience is bound to be confused**"). A rule of context is appended: "In a war drama, delaying a battle out of respect for one's parents is a kind of 'filial piety' that will be regarded as selfish… yet the same act in a family-ethics drama… **only when the family is ordered can one govern the state and pacify the realm (齐家，才能治国平天下, qí jiā, cái néng zhì guó píng tiānxià).**"
  2. **Design character relationships as interest relations × emotional relations in opposition** (p0240–0241): "**In conventional narrative, if interest relations can be set in opposition to emotional relations, conflict is relatively easy to create.**" The author also proposes avoiding "**character sameness**" (人物顺撇, rénwù shùnpiě)—"**if two people are alike in temperament from beginning to end, and react similarly when going through things together, the phenomenon of 'character sameness' arises, characters become hard to distinguish, and viewers have difficulty telling who is who on first viewing.**"
  3. **Three criteria for arranging conflicts** (p0241–0242): **reasonableness** ("**television connects events by following causal links**… **generally speaking, the development of character relationships matters even more than objective order**"; **Figure 32, the nested-events model**—"**this whole event then becomes a 'cause,' the beginning of a larger event, and the development, turn, and resolution of that larger event continue to unfold**") / **emotional credibility** ("which conflicts to exaggerate depends on which aspect of the character you want to use them to express"; "**so the key conflict scene is placed between Tie Lihua and Zhang Ji'an, because the strongest conflicts must be placed on the evolution of the main character relationship**"; and note the opposite American-series practice—"**contemporary series, chiefly American ones, also often use 'effect determinism,' that is, first pick out the most watchable and effective conflict, then use character and plot to make it reasonable and emotionally credible**") / **rhythm** (a string of self-questions: "**when what has consistently been conveyed to the viewer is positive emotion, when is it necessary to bring in negative emotion to maintain variety and prevent emotional fatigue? When the narrative is at its tightest, how does one slip in lyricism? And how far can the lyricism go before the viewer is pulled out of the drama or thrown out of it?**").
  4. **A market judgment on the split between old and new viewers** (p0271–0272; the book's most forward-looking passage): "**Viewers are no longer satisfied with a conventional, already fixed-pattern genre work; they have lost their freshness for things they can recite backward**"; "**yet the unhurried narrative style and standards fixed since 《渴望》 (Yearning) continue to attract them**" (referring to middle-aged and older viewers); "**the different viewing habits of old and new audiences have left today's series development in a generational gap, and how to balance the relationship between viewers with two different sets of preferences has become a problem every creator has to face.**"

### 13.6 Example 6, 《亮剑》 (Drawing Sword): A Novel → 30-Episode Military Drama (Zhang Wei, p0273–0308)

- **Source work: length and type**: Du Liang's debut novel 《亮剑》 (Drawing Sword), which "**pioneered an entirely new concept of the war novel—the 'market-style war story'**"; it spans from Li Yunlong's time as commander of the Eighth Route Army's Independent Regiment fighting the Japanese in northwestern Shanxi "until his persecution during the Cultural Revolution, his tragic suicide, and his rehabilitation in 1980" (p0274). The 2005 series on CCTV-1 (screenwriters: Du Liang and Jiang Qitao; directors: Zhang Qian and Chen Jian): "**it became that year's ratings champion… in its first week on air, Drawing Sword averaged a rating of 11.42, peaked at 13.7, and took a 28.71 share**."
- **Thematic coordinates**: It distills a declaration from the novel into the series' theme—"**the spirit of drawing the sword** (亮剑精神, liàngjiàn jīngshén)" (p0276–0278). The declaration itself (spoken in the series by Li Yunlong in his graduation thesis defense; the book quotes it in full twice): "Faced with a powerful opponent, knowing you cannot win, you must still resolutely draw your sword; even if you fall, you must become a mountain, a ridge!… Even if the enemy is many and we are few, even if we are surrounded, we dare to draw the sword, we dare to fight to the last man. In one sentence: **when two brave men meet on a narrow road, the brave prevail** (狭路相逢勇者胜, xiálù xiāngféng yǒngzhě shèng). The spirit of drawing the sword is the soul of our nation's army. Where the blade points, nothing stands." Three thematic operations: ① "raise and elevate the more positive side" of the novel's theme; ② **demote patriotism to second place** (a counterintuitive move for a main-melody production, as in the source)—"**the patriotic theme of a main-melody subject is demoted to second place, while personal spirit and the 'spirit of drawing the sword' are placed first**"; ③ **avoid turning it into a character biography**—"**and weaken the character's specific experiences, selecting instead his key or turning-point events**." A definition to prevent misreading: "'**Drawing the sword**' refers to a spiritual realm, like a guiding strategic philosophy, not specific tactical content. As Chairman Mao famously put it, despise the enemy strategically and take him seriously tactically." Where the theme is delivered: in the graduation thesis at the very end of the series (p0278).
  - **The third highlight inherited from the novel: a positive portrayal of KMT–CCP cooperation** (p0277, as in the source): "**but political differences cannot erase their mutual admiration for each other's character and spiritual strength. The characters are not denied wholesale on the basis of political views; rather, the glory of a humanity that transcends politics is acknowledged.**"
- **Major character surgery**:
  - **The "whole-pot transplant" strategy** (连锅端, liánguōduān) (p0278; a contrast with the previous five cases, as in the source): "**apart from a slight increase or decrease in a few characters, the personality traits, identity backgrounds, fates, and relationships of protagonists such as Li Yunlong, Chu Yunfei, Zhao Gang, and Ding Wei undergo no change whatsoever. This 'whole-pot' character-selection strategy is like a translational substitution of roles.**"
  - **A sociological explanation of the rough-hewn hero** (p0279; a highly valuable passage): "On the surface, **Li Yunlong's character starting point lowers the threshold of social recognition and acceptance**. But in fact… **Li Yunlong's growth is the Chinese peasant's process of understanding the Red Revolution and war**… they are not 'gentlemanly Pattons' but '**Chinese-peasant Pattons**.'" And an explicit rejection of the opposite approach: "**If one insisted on catering to some 'overall image of senior cadres' and took the opposite approach, then this series' appeal would be at least halved.**"
  - **An honest diagnosis of the absent character arc** (p0280): "But Li Yunlong, unlike Jiang Bida in 《历史的天空》 (The Sky of History), does not have a personality rich in change; **his character is already fixed the moment he appears**… **this has to do with the series omitting the stage of his character's growth**."
  - **A trident-shaped set of character relationships** (p0284–0285; the most reusable structural diagram in this chapter): Li Yunlong is at the center, and Zhao Gang and Chu Yunfei both serve as "**counterforces**"—"this counterforce is not 'opposition' or 'confrontation' in the narrow sense, nor is it simply placing obstacles in Li Yunlong's way; rather, it creates organic interaction with him." **Four modes of connection (as in the source)**: ① Zhao Gang—(**work relationship**)—Li Yunlong—(**enemy-friend relationship**)—Chu Yunfei; ② Zhao Gang—(**clash of temperaments**)—Li Yunlong—(**clash of camps**)—Chu Yunfei; ③ Zhao Gang—(**mutual adjustment**)—Li Yunlong—(**mutual familiarity**)—Chu Yunfei; ④ Zhao Gang—(**combat cooperation**)—Li Yunlong—(**military cooperation**)—Chu Yunfei. The use of "imbalance": "Li Yunlong is the undisputed dominant figure in the army… **so Li Yunlong's 'dynamics' naturally require another character to form a 'check.'**" Zhao Gang's genre breakthrough: "**it overturns the stereotyped image of the frail political officer in our army.**" Chu Yunfei's positioning: "On the screen of Chinese television, **a positive Kuomintang officer like Chu Yunfei appears for the first time**… despite different political views, the audience can still accept this character," and he is characterized as a **witness**.
  - **Two newly added relationship scenes**: **the rewrite of Chu Yunfei's entrance** (Episode 1, scene 1-7) uses "concern for a friendly force" to close the distance first—Chu Yunfei: "**We must find a way to support them, even if it's only some artillery support.**" "**In the end, what is lost is all China's national-defense strength.**" The commentary: "**The adaptation seems deliberately to blur the boundary between the KMT and CCP camps, so that from the very start the audience's emotional distance from this Kuomintang officer is shortened.**" **The gun-giving scene** (Episode 4, scenes 4-9 and 4-16; not in the novel): Li Yunlong: "**A grown man carrying a lady's pistol—what is that? If word gets out, it will damage my face.**" Zhao Gang: "**I do not seek a jade worth cities; I seek a sword that kills.**" The commentary: "**This woven-in plot element serves as a witness to their relationship and lays groundwork for their later cooperation and combat**… **it strips away the seeming harmony and exaggerated effect of the teahouse appointment in the novel.**"
- **Structural handling**:
  - **The cut portion and the full reasoning** (p0291–0292; the most candid piece of industry record): "The series actually adapts only the earlier content of the novel—**its capacity is only about one third of the original, and it stops short at the 1955 military ranks and the reform of military thinking**." The censorship reason (as in the source): "**it involves the 'Cultural Revolution' and 'Taiwan Strait' relations**; if it did, it would probably never get past SARFT's censorship, and the series would die in the womb. **It is said that Du Liang initially disagreed and wanted a complete interpretation of the original.**… **This is not a sacrifice made because it is untrue, but a sacrifice made because it is too true.** Although it captures the feel of historical truth, it is too tragic, too unsparing, and crosses the line. **As a production team that must first consider economic interests, it cannot afford this risk.**" The audience-emotion reason: "**the audience's emotions would plunge from a high altitude… the obvious contrast in style between the two halves, if handled badly, would make part of the audience unwilling to accept it.**" Yet the author admits: "**the second half of the novel is indeed more exciting than the first; that is where the essence lies.**"
  - **Two hard data points / unconventional decisions** (p0292): ① "**the script arranges 18 battles, large and small, and every battle is different**… especially the mounted battle (not in the novel); **in script terms, this is Li Yunlong's only defeat and also the most tragic battle**. To highlight this, the script uses the cavalry commander's three charge slogans to express the spirit of Drawing Sword to the fullest"; ② "**to emphasize this feeling, the script even commits a taboo of television writing: show as few women as possible—especially in wartime. In the first 19 episodes there is only one female character, and even she soon gives her life to the brutality of war.**"—**this is the only exception the book acknowledges to its general rule that "a love line must be added," and the author calls it "committing a taboo."**
  - **Four segments (using the 30-episode version, p0290–0297, as in the source)**: "**episodes 1–18—the War of Resistance; 19–21—the War of Liberation; 22–25—Li Yunlong's marriage and romance; 26–30—the military academy.**"
    - **I. The War of Resistance (episodes 1–18)**. In Episode 1, "**Li Yunlong's backstory is not explained through narration; he is placed directly on the battlefield against the Sakata Regiment, and his character is revealed through action in this dramatic scene**"; "**the strength of the opponent invisibly raises Li Yunlong's value**"; he is demoted to head of the uniform factory, and "**this pattern of treatment runs through Li Yunlong's subsequent military career**." In episodes 2–12, "**Li Yunlong's Japanese opponents steadily escalate in strength, from the Sakata Regiment to the Yamazaki Battalion, which advances alone but badly mauls our army, and then to the elusive Yamamoto special-agents unit**"; Yamamoto "emerges in Episode 2, creating a suspense, **and does not face Li Yunlong directly until Episode 6**." **The double-climax structure** (episodes 13–14): "Great joy and great sorrow hand off to each other, but this is only the small climax of the first segment; next, the ground is prepared for the great climax of the decisive battle at Ping'an Town in Episode 14… **Li Yunlong chooses to sacrifice his wife, Xiuqin, yet resolutely fights a comeback battle that wipes out his shame**… **the spirit of drawing the sword is released in concentrated form at the moment Li Yunlong shells the gate tower of Ping'an Town.**"
    - **II. The War of Liberation (episodes 19–21)**. The transition: "**but Episode 15 already raises the curtain on the War of Liberation**… the War of Resistance segment concludes with the burial of Xiuqin, and then Chu Yunfei's stationing at Dagu Town opens the prelude to civil war." The logic of the shift in relational focus (as in the source): "**once victory in the War of Resistance is near, the KMT–CCP contradiction gradually rises to become the principal contradiction; the two men turn from friends into enemies, setting aside their bond as confidants for their respective political positions—this shift in the center of gravity of their relationship is entirely natural.**" **The "needle wrapped in silk" (绵里藏针, mián lǐ cáng zhēn) escalation sequence (usable directly as a beat sheet for an "enemy-friend switch")**: "**Chu Yunfei stations at Dagu Town — Li Yunlong trains troops at Dagu Town, Li Yunlong intercepts Chu Yunfei's military supplies — Chu Yunfei disarms the district squad, Chu Yunfei sets a Hongmen banquet — Li Yunlong attends the banquet with explosives strapped to his body.**" Length control: "Li Yunlong and Chu Yunfei's direct confrontation **actually occupies only about two and a half episodes**… **the structural arrangement strenuously avoids dragging; every exchange between the two is crisp and effective, and every move Li Yunlong makes is a killing move, each one lethal.**" The next segment is launched with "Tian Yu donating blood to save Li Yunlong's life."
    - **III. Marriage and Romance (episodes 22–25)**. Functional positioning: "**Li Yunlong's transfer to a field hospital for emergency treatment is not only a shift of scene from the front to the rear, but also marks a turning point in his life.**" Episode-by-episode functions: Episode 22 "bridges what comes before and after… **it closes out the series' battlefield spectacle**"; Episode 23 introduces **the first obstacle** (army leaders ask Director Luo of the hospital to act as matchmaker), with three functions—"**the presentation of this state actually carries a somewhat anti-heroic concept**" / it provides an opportunity to display Xiao Tian's character / "**Li Yunlong becomes the listener to Xiao Tian's troubles**… **that is, the two's emotional development is not written head-on but reflected from the side**"; Episode 24 has the proposal accepted, and **the second obstacle** = Tian Moxuan's opposition, with the principle (as in the source): "**one absolutely must not write it straight**; at this moment there can be no calm seas—the twists and turns of the characters' emotional fate must be arranged like range upon range of peaks"; Episode 25 grants the marriage and serves as a transition, "**using Li Yunlong's inability to get a combat assignment—reduced to armchair strategy—to convey his sense of loss after victory**"; the question this episode raises (as in the source): "**once a man born for war is absorbed into the trivial routines of daily life, how does he find his place, and can he draw his sword again? This is the question the episode poses and the point where the audience resonates.**"
    - **IV. The Military Academy (episodes 26–30)**. Two lines (as in the source): "**the main line is Li Yunlong's gradual integration into study and life at the military academy; the sub-line is Li Yunlong's extramarital affair. Both lines have fairly complete structures, with a beginning, development, turn, and resolution (起承转合, qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé).**" Main-line beats: reporting late and being punished with sweeping the road (beginning) → disrupting class and talking back to the instructor (development) → correcting his attitude after the commandant's criticism (turn) → the graduation thesis reading, which drives the climax → **the generals' formation at the National Day parade as the "glorious" ending**. Sub-line beats (as in the source): "Li Yunlong, asked by Tian Yu, visits Zhang Bailu as the **beginning**; Zhang Bailu falls in love with Li Yunlong and launches a series of offensives until he can no longer withstand them as the **development**; Tian Yu learns of it and telephones Feng Nan as the **sudden turn**; the candid three-way conversation among Li Yunlong, Tian Yu, and Zhang Bailu as the **climax**; Li Yunlong ultimately chooses Tian Yu and settles his family as the **resolution**." Both lines point toward "**return**" and "**finding one's way back after going astray**."
  - **The choice of the motive for change (an explicit principle against "external coercion," p0306, as in the source)**: "**The screenwriter does not design the motive for Li Yunlong's transformation and return as coercion by the authority of superior organizations, or the binding force of public opinion, or correction by laws and regulations; these rigid frameworks, which resemble the superstructure, cannot become the main driving force of Li Yunlong's return from the wrong path… the true motive and principal driving force still lie in the character's inner motivation.**"
  - **The closing theme scene: the graduation thesis defense** (p0307–0308). The divergence from the novel: "In Chapter 24 of the original novel, **only a small part of the description of the graduation thesis concerns Li Yunlong; most of the other text describes the uproar Ding Wei's thesis causes at the military academy**… Li Yunlong is placed second and merely mentioned in passing." The series changes the thesis title to "On the Fighting Will of Soldiers—The Spirit of Drawing Sword," and provides the book's only example of a "theme monologue" (script 30-12): "**A unit with fine traditions usually has the soil for cultivating heroes; the emergence of heroes or outstanding soldiers often takes a collective form rather than an individual one.**"
- **Transferable techniques**: ① **the producer's "three foundations" = an IP evaluation sheet** (Zhao Junkai, p0274): "**one is theme, a pure soldier's banner; the second is distinctive characters; the third is a great story.**" ② **read a character attribute out of a single physical description in the novel** (p0283): the novel says Li Yunlong's big head comes from practicing iron-head kung fu by butting stone tablets; the book's analysis—"**humor, self-mockery, banter, a fondness for exaggeration, and a refusal to follow the rules all became indispensable traits of Li Yunlong in later novel and television adaptations. This inheritance of a personality trait often implies future plasticity and capacity for action; in fact, it is the essence of a character attribute.**" ③ **the right way to make policy-driven cuts**: not to clash head-on, but to **distill a spiritual slogan that can run through the whole series** and move the narrative center of gravity to a period that can be written, which "not only preserves the essence of the characters but also shortens the length and concentrates the narrative" (p0275). ④ **key turning points depend on "one newly added display of ability"** (p0298–0302; the most technical passage in this chapter).
