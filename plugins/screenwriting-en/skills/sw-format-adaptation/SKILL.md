# Format, Process & Adaptation (格式·流程·改编)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

Three things: **how to write on the page** (format and action paragraphs), **how to get from an idea to a finished draft** (outline / treatment / draft chain / revision), and **what to do when adapting someone else's material**. Sources: Wendy Henson, 《编剧：步步为营》 (*Screenwriting, Step by Step*); Richard Walter, 《剧本》 (*Screenwriting: The Art, Craft, and Business of Film and Television Writing*); Neill Hicks, 《编剧的核心技巧》 (*The Essential Craft of Screenwriting*); Syd Field, 《电影剧本写作基础》 (*Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting*); Robert McKee, 《故事》 (*Story*); Julian Hoxter, 《编剧的十二条法则》 (*The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules*); Diamond & Weissman, 《好莱坞编剧的生意经》 (*Bulletproof: Writing Scripts That Don't Get Shot Down*); and Eric Bork, 《如何写出好故事》 (*The Idea: The Seven Elements of a Viable Story for Screen, Stage or Fiction*). For examples and the symbol table, see [reference.md](reference.md). For industry and sales, see `sw-industry-business`.

## I. You Are Writing a Spec Script, Not a Shooting Script

- Hicks: format comes from the outmoded studio shooting script; **what you are writing now is a spec script (selling script)**, so avoid useless abbreviations and numerical markings; **the most basic rule: make the script easy to read** — do not make the reader work, and do not make him flip back a page to check how a scene came about; "you are manipulating the reader, not the film."
- Milius: "I never write shot content, never write 'we move in on' or 'we can see' — that is extremely presumptuous. Format only needs to tell the reader day or night, interior or exterior; the rest is like a novel — clear, precise, and interesting to read."
- Henson: if the format is wrong, no one will accept it; the reader first scans the first few pages, and only if the format is right does he read the content; "do not give anyone a single reason not to read your script"; the screenplay is the "blueprint" that guides production, and the audience does not read scripts.
- Walter's Principle 32: **if you want others to treat you as a professional playwright, first hold yourself to professional standards**; "as long as it looks like something a writer wrote" (Bork); if you faithfully observe the limits of image and sound, you can almost disregard format.
- Diamond: read only scripts (good ones, bad ones, produced and unproduced); thousands of free scripts are available online; but Henson and Walter warn that most online scripts are shot-by-shot shooting scripts — do not imitate their camera terminology.

## II. Hard Format Rules (Henson / Walter / Hicks / Hoxter / Diamond combined)

- **Typeface and page count**: Courier 12 (one page ≈ one minute); 100–110 pages ideal, 90–120 acceptable, under 80 amateur, over 125 "people will think you cannot write," 130 out of control; do not change the font size to cheat the page count.
- **Title page**: only the title and the author (if you do not have an agent, you may add contact information); no date, draft number, WGA registration number, "original screenplay," or illustrations; two brass brads.
- **Page numbers** from the top right of page two; no scene numbers, no "to be continued"; "FADE IN:" only at the beginning, "FADE OUT" only at the end.
- **Scene headings**: INT./EXT. + location + DAY/NIGHT, in capitals; from general to specific ("INT. LAMBEAU FIELD – LOCKER ROOM – DAY"); details (date, time of day) go in the action paragraph; you may write "LATE NIGHT" or "DUSK" to help the reader.
- **Action paragraphs**: present tense, active voice ("Joe is led by Paul" → "Paul leads Joe"); like a list, not a description; time, season, and weather first, then the location's physical features; write only what the camera can photograph and the actor can play ("she stands there thinking" cannot be filmed); delete adjectives and adverbs; capitalize a character on first appearance and give their age; capitalize props and sound effects (to alert the production manager and the sound recordist); write only sound effects whose source is unseen (thunder, a phone ringing, a dog barking off-screen); double-space between paragraphs; "a page of script is not a wall of text."
- **Never mention the camera**: no CU/POV/cut/dissolve/"we see/we hear"; suggest shots through the text (scene heading = wide shot, a flag = medium shot, Darren walking to the microphone = close-up); the end of every scene is naturally a cut, so do not write "CUT TO" (removing it can save ten pages); FADE IN, FADE OUT, and DISSOLVE are in full capitals on the rare occasions they are needed; P.O.V. only where necessary, such as binoculars or a subjective shot.
- **Write only the master scene**: a dramatized action with continuity of thought and action may cover multiple locations (George and Martha arguing from the living room to the bedroom door to the French windows is still one scene, "INT. GEORGE AND MARTHA'S HOUSE"); a spy rendezvous at a hot-dog stand is written only as a wide shot plus the necessary details; breaking it into shots is the editor's job.
- **Dialogue**: a left-aligned narrow column starting about 2.5 inches from the left edge of the page and about 3.5 inches wide (**not centered on the page**; Henson's original "3 inches from the left margin, 3.25 inches wide" is another way of measuring the same column); see the next section for the complete layout specification; character names in capitals; do not break dialogue across pages (interrupt with action or another character's interjection; if there is no other way, "(see next page)"); a speech that runs past a page = "Hamlet syndrome"; parentheticals of ≤3 words, verbs only (smiling / nervous), 99% unnecessary, with the exception of a crowded scene where it is unclear whom the line is addressed to; no underlining for emphasis, no exclamation points, ellipses once or twice but a third time is too many; (OS) for off-screen dialogue, (VO) for narration, best avoided; no dialect transliteration; a line must be a sentence, not a groan or grunt ("S. or N.").
- **Character introductions**: give only sex and age (Walter); describe with an abstract characteristic action ("a dry, shriveled face like a dried persimmon"), not height, weight, or skin color unless the whole conception requires it; no character list, character biographies, or casting suggestions; names should indicate gender and avoid homophones or shared initials.
- **Criteria for a transition**: a change of place or time, a change in the number of characters, or a change of dramatic purpose.
- **Special effects**: write only what the audience sees. As for montage, first consider avoiding it; as a tool for simplifying a complex continuous action, you may write "CHASE" and list its salient features (thirteen pages reduced to a fifth of a page).
- **Flashbacks**: ideally do not write the word "flashback"; if you fear the reader will be confused, label "EXT. BARN – FLASHBACK" — "a device akin to cheating"; cheating (indicating that this is the boss / these two are sisters) can be done naturally and sparingly; "gentle reminders" ("Janet, the woman we saw earlier smashing the vending machine at the laundromat") are permissible.
- **Email submission**: PDF, not Word; do not mark it as written specifically for television.
- Spelling and punctuation: be an old-school pedant; use a paper dictionary; "I have never seen a script with sparkling dialogue and unforgettable characters that was a mess of format, spelling, and grammar."

## II.2 Layout Specifications, Element-Level Conventions, and Output Formats

> The figures in this section come from measurements of several professionally typeset scripts (US Letter, Courier 12) and the Final Draft default template. The element-level conventions and the Chinese and Japanese industry formats are standard industry practice and **do not come from this skill's 19 source books** (they were proposed for inclusion in issue #2). For examples, see [reference.md](reference.md), Section 10.

### Layout Specification Table (US Letter, Courier 12: 10 characters per inch, 6 lines per inch)

| Element | Starting distance from the left edge of the page | Width / right stop | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Page margins | Left 1.5", right 1.0", top 1.0", bottom 1.0" | About 55 lines per page | The wide left margin is for binding |
| Scene heading | 1.5" (flush left) | To 7.5", about 60 characters | All capitals; two blank lines before (one in some templates) |
| Action paragraph | 1.5" (flush left) | 6.0" wide, about 60 characters | One blank line between paragraphs |
| Character name | 3.7" (about character position 22) | Not centered, fixed indent | Followed by extensions (V.O.)/(O.S.)/(CONT'D) |
| Parenthetical | 3.1" | About 2.0" wide | One line, mostly verbs |
| Dialogue | 2.5" | 3.5" wide (to 6.0"), about 35 characters | Left-aligned narrow column, single-spaced within the column |
| Transition | Right-aligned to 7.5" | Starts at about 6.0" | `CUT TO:` in capitals with a colon |
| Page number | Top right, 7.0"–7.5", 0.5" from the top | In the form `2.` | From page two |

The one-page ≈ one-minute conversion rests on this table; change any one item and the page count becomes unreliable.

### How to Count a "Page" in a Chinese Draft (This Rule Has Nothing to Do with the Medium: It Is the Difference between Chinese and Western Layout, Not between Film and Series)

The table above describes a Western layout in US Letter + Courier 12. **Chinese is not a monospaced 10 characters per inch, there is no 3.7″ character-name indent, and none of the premises of that table hold**, so "one page ≈ one minute" cannot be applied directly to count a Chinese draft. Chinese scripts have no page standard, only a character-count standard, yet producers, platforms, and various writing guides all speak in "pages." Convert using the following chain, and **you must state that these are converted pages** whenever you use it:

1. First read "N pages" as "**N minutes of screen time**."
2. Chinese characters per minute: **for dialogue-heavy material, 370–400 characters per minute; for material with a lot of action and reaction (suspense, action, single-camera comedy), 270–330 characters per minute**. **The lowest band: for drafts dominated by long stretches of silent action (silent-film-style passages, long-take action scenes, Ozu-style events off-screen), use 230–260 characters per minute** — on such scripts even 270 is still too high and will systematically underestimate the actual shooting time. When using this band, **you must state the basis for the conversion in the deliverable** (which scenes are counted as silent action; character count ÷ characters per minute = converted pages).
3. Converted pages = non-blank character count ÷ characters per minute.

**Both features and series use this same chain**: a 100-minute feature at 330 characters per minute is about 33,000 characters; a 45-minute series episode is about 15,000 characters; a 28-minute half-hour episode is about 9,200 characters. **Do not borrow this chain from the series skill, and do not treat the character count for one format as the standard for another** — characters per minute varies with dialogue density, not with medium.

For sources and finer bands (measured per-episode character counts for each mainland Chinese series genre), see `sw-chinese-series-practice`; that skill gives the concrete numbers for series, while the conversion principle is here.

### Element-Level Conventions

- **(V.O.)**: the speaker is not in the scene's space — narration, the other end of a phone call, a recording, interior monologue. **(O.S.)**: in the scene's space but off-screen (the next room, outside the door). Television drafts often use (O.C.) as equivalent to O.S. Use these sparingly, but when you do, get them right.
- **(CONT'D)**: added after a character's name when that character's dialogue continues after an action paragraph interrupts it; across a page break, add `(MORE)` at the bottom of the page and `(CONT'D)` after the character's name on the next page — writing software adds these automatically; in a handwritten draft, it is better to adjust the page break.
- **(PRE-LAP)**: sound from the next scene enters at the end of the current scene. **(INTO PHONE)** / **(ON PHONE)** as parentheticals; when the person on the other end is unseen, use (V.O.), and you may add (FILTERED).
- **Dual dialogue**: two people speaking at the same time, in two side-by-side columns; in Fountain, add `^` after the second character name.
- **Montage / series of shots**: begin with `MONTAGE - TITLE`, each item on one line beginning with `--`, and end with `END MONTAGE` or `BACK TO SCENE`; `SERIES OF SHOTS` works the same way. Still observe the earlier rule: avoid it when you can.
- **Intercutting**: after `INTERCUT - LOCATION A / LOCATION B`, switch freely without rewriting scene headings.
- **Flashback**: prefix the scene heading with `FLASHBACK - ` or wrap it in `BEGIN FLASHBACK` / `END FLASHBACK`.
- **Titles**: `SUPER: "No. 18"` (inside an action paragraph).
- **Sound effects and newly appearing characters** are in all capitals within action paragraphs (English drafts); Chinese drafts use bold or no marking, consistently throughout the draft.

### Output Format Contract: Fountain

A script produced by the agent **is delivered by default as Fountain plain text** (`.fountain`), not Markdown and not a txt file with manually typed spaces. Reason: Fountain's elements correspond one-to-one with the table above, it can be version-controlled, and it can be rendered to PDF or exported to .fdx.

**Core notation** (full specification at fountain.io):
- Title page: `Title:` `Credit:` `Author:` `Draft date:` `Contact:` key-value pairs at the top of the file, followed by a blank line and then the body.
- Scene headings: lines beginning with `INT.` `EXT.` `INT./EXT.` `I/E` are recognized automatically; **force** with a leading `.` (e.g., `.内 沈越家·客厅 夜`).
- Character names: a line of all capitals preceded by a blank line is recognized automatically; **force** with a leading `@` (e.g., `@沈越`). Extensions follow directly after the name: `@小七 (V.O.)`. In dual dialogue, add `^` after the second character's name.
- Parentheticals: `(……)` on the line below the character name. Dialogue: consecutive lines after the character name, ending at a blank line.
- Transitions: an all-capitals line ending in `TO:` is recognized automatically; **force** with a leading `>` (e.g., `> 切至：`). Centering: `> 剧终 <`.
- Action: all other paragraphs; force with a leading `!`. Page break `===`; notes `[[…]]`; boneyard `/* … */`; sections `#`; synopsis `=`; lyrics `~`.

**Chinese drafts must use the forced markers**: Fountain identifies elements by capital letters and INT./EXT., and neither holds for Chinese. Add `.` to every scene heading, `@` to every character name, and `>` to every transition; otherwise everything renders as action.

**Rendering paths**: Fountain → PDF: afterwriting (command line), Beat (macOS, free), Highland, Slugline; Fountain → .fdx: Fade In and Final Draft import directly, or afterwriting exports. Chinese rendering requires a CJK monospaced font (Sarasa Mono, Noto Sans Mono CJK); Courier has no Chinese glyphs.

**Interface with `sw-workflow`**: Stage 5 deliverables follow the chosen format — Hollywood style delivers a `.fountain` file, the Chinese numbered-scene format and Japanese format deliver plain text, and Chinese opera scripts follow the format of the opera skill; the story bible records only file names and page counts.

### Chinese Film and Television Scripts: The Numbered-Scene Format (场号制, chǎnghào zhì)

The standard structure of a contemporary Chinese film/television industry draft (details vary by company; consistency within one draft is enough; for examples see reference.md):
- **Scene line**: `场号 时 内/外 地点`, e.g., `1. 日 内 沈越家·客厅`; series use `集-场` (`3-12`). Time uses 日/夜/晨/黄昏 (day/night/morning/dusk); the order is either "日 内" or "内 日," two schools — choose one.
- **Character line** (optional): `人物：沈越、小七`, listing the characters who appear in the scene.
- **Action paragraph**: begins with `△` (some use ▲ or no mark), present tense, one action unit per paragraph.
- **Dialogue**: `人名：台词`, with parentheticals after the name `沈越（低声）：`; off-screen voice marked `（OS）`/`（画外）`, narration `（VO）`/`（旁白）`.
- **Do not write transitions**; for flashbacks add `（回忆）` to the scene line; titles are written `字幕：`.
- A series episode of 40–45 minutes is about 12,000–15,000 characters; the scene-by-scene outline (one line per scene) is the precursor of the numbered-scene format and is the same thing as the step outline in `sw-story-structure`.
- The numbered-scene format **does not use Fountain**; deliver it as plain text. Before outputting, the agent should first ask the user whether they want Hollywood style or the numbered-scene format; **when no one can be asked, set the default by language: projects with all-Chinese dialogue use the numbered-scene format, English projects use Hollywood style / Fountain, and state the format at the top of the deliverable. Do not stop just because you cannot ask.** **Chinese opera scripts are the exception**: this default governs only film and series; Chinese opera (Peking opera, Henan opera, Yue opera, Kunqu, etc.) uses a format of song / spoken dialogue / stage-action cues (科介, kējiè) and 板式 (bǎnshì — the metrical pattern of a banqiang aria) or 曲牌 (qǔpái — a fixed tune pattern in the qupai system) markings, neither the numbered-scene format nor Fountain — see `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang` Section 9 and `sw-chinese-opera-qupai`.

### Japanese Scripts: 柱・ト書き・セリフ

- **柱 (hashira — the scene line)**: `○地点（时间）`, e.g., `○沈越の部屋・居間（夜）`; the scene number may be written before `○`.
- **ト書き (tosho — action description)**: the first line is indented three full-width characters; on a character's first appearance, add their age `沈越（３４）`; the passage of time is shown with `　　×　　×　　×`; a pause is written `（間）`.
- **セリフ (serifu — dialogue)**: `人物名「台词」`, with the character name followed directly by full-width quotation marks, no colon; parentheticals go after the name `小七（声）「…」`; `（Ｎ）` for narration, `（Ｍ）` for monologue, `（回想）` for recollection, added to the 柱.
- **Draft stages**: 準備稿 → 改訂稿 → 決定稿 (preparatory draft → revised draft → final draft), written on the cover; printed scripts are vertically set, but a text file may be horizontal.
- For the 企画書 (kikakusho — proposal document) format, see reference.md Section 9; it is a separate document from the screenplay format.

## III. How to Write Good Action Paragraphs

- McKee: **absolute present tense + continuous vivid movement**; specific nouns (a spike, not a big nail), action verbs (pad/amble/stroll/mosey/saunter/stagger/tiptoe/creep/shuffle/waddle/trudge/lurch/hobble, not "move slowly"); **never use is/are throughout** ("a mansion guards the edge of the village"); the Hemingway method avoids Latin abstractions and adjectives/adverbs; metaphors must pass the test "what do you see/hear on the screen?"; delete "we see/hear"; use single-spaced short paragraphs to suggest a change of shot (wide → tracking → close-up of the note → close-up of the ring → medium shot).
- Walter's symbols: **Hwk?/See/Hear** ("This truth makes Charlene remember her mother's years of abuse" — how does the audience see it?); **Ess. Det. Only / SIFYN** (leave the pastrami aroma to your novel; a Hudson Valley feast → "a lavish ball is being held on the lawn"); **Drekt/Akt** (do not write "pauses and thinks"); **Notnot** (do not write "does not answer" or "does nothing"); **Prez** ("the room is empty," not "everyone has left"); **Novry** ("very fast" → races, dashes, sprints); **Conk** (concrete); **No Tt** (not like Tarzan: "Picks up the gun. Walks to the window. Loads it."); **4 v. 6** (the ratio of needed to unneeded words; "cut at this ratio and 110 pages will shrink by 56"); **Fmpmt** (find the point, state the point, move the story forward; "Harry leaves" in two words); **NoFX** (no centering, right alignment, italics, bold, or color); **Clok** (how much screen time does this passage occupy? even one minute without drama is sluggish).
- Henson: for two weeks, keep every sentence ≤10 words; cut conjunctions, subordinate clauses, adjectives, and adverbs; keep transitive verbs; "Pam folds clothes in the laundry room. The phone rings. Pam runs quickly to the kitchen and picks up the receiver. No one answers. Pam hangs up."; learn concision from the original pages of produced scripts (compare the two versions of 《异形 II》 (*Aliens*)).
- Diamond: "He reaches into his back pocket, takes out his wallet, pulls out a $5 bill, and hands it to her" → "He takes out his wallet and hands her $5"; test: describe the scene to a friend with no more detail than you would use in telling the story; do not do the director's, production designer's, costume designer's, or composer's work.
- Hicks: write images, not pictures; "stir the inner imagination rather than describe a picture"; write actions with cause and effect, a beginning, development, and ending, to give the reader a sense of rhythm (the snowmobile chase is a counterexample); presentation = the through-line expressed in tone; the first scene gives the reader the clue for how to read the script (the most common complaint in Hollywood is that a script was misread).

## IV. The Process from Idea to Finished Draft

### Before You Write
- **Bork's 60/30/10**: success is 60% the core idea, 30% structure, and 10% expression; first write the idea as a one- or two-sentence logline plus a one-page synopsis and get objective feedback; self-check with the seven elements of PROBLEM (see `sw-premise-theme`).
- **Diamond's process**: the three C's (character / concept / context) → a list of comparable films → a one-page nine-step plot synopsis → a character action chart (tell the nine steps from each character's point of view) → plot integration + transition scenes + subplots + comic-relief character → outline → set pieces → script → revision → submission. "Do not start writing until the pitch / details / outline have won over all parties."
- **Henson**: write a theme statement (theme → basic action + purpose sentence) plus a three-sentence dramatic premise (disturbance / first-act hook / second-act hook, each with its core question) on one sheet as an outline; if the writing does not flow, the conception has a hole or you have been led astray by a subplot.
- **Walter**: an outline ≠ a synopsis (a synopsis robs the reader of pleasure, and a synopsis of an unfinished script is not taken seriously); writing an outline is "like carrying steam in a bucket," the hardest and most necessary task; the outline should contain some specific dialogue; **Principle 44: the outline is in the past tense; the script is in the present tense** — "the real problem is not deviating but staying on track"; scene cards (numbered index cards, about sixty scenes, each scene a mini-movie) make experimentation easy; if you get stuck at the two-thirds mark, go back and re-card the story.
- **McKee**: "from the inside out": for the first four of six months, write 3×5 cards as a **step outline** (one or two sentences per scene, structural position on the back; the purpose is to destroy your own work — sketch a scene a dozen ways and then throw them away) → **talk the story for ten minutes** and watch the listener's eyes → **treatment** (60–90 pages, present-tense moment-by-moment description, no dialogue, write the subtext) → **screenplay** (five to ten pages a day; dialogue is best at this point). Skipping the procedure and writing the script directly = a fake treatment and the slowest method; "premature dialogue writing suffocates creativity."
- **Field's card method**: one day for Act One, two days for Act Two, one day for Act Three; first make four sections: opening / two plot points / ending.
- **Hicks's draft chain**: couch writing → outline ("a sketch of what you see") → prose treatment (present tense) → first draft incorporating everything (at least a hundred pages, more than one draft) → revision draft (remove actor directions, remove furniture, change to simple present tense, proofread) → agent draft (show only to critics who understand scripts) → submission draft.
- Hoxter: the **outline** is for yourself (scene by scene, according to the act structure you are using); the **treatment** is for buyers (prose, written in a cinematic register; a comedy treatment should be funny; 4–30 pages); an example is John August's paragraph outline for 《大鱼》 (*Big Fish*). Diamond: a pitch document should ideally take under 20 minutes; you cannot sell a detailed narrative treatment; the mainstream only buys complete scripts.

### Writing Habits
- Tommy Thompson: "Put your clothes on every day"; Walter's Principle 41: **writer's block is the natural state of writing**; "a writer does not have the right to write only when he feels like it"; Principle 42: the deadline is the writer's friend; Principle 43: do not let perfectionism become a roadblock; "where a writer writes matters less than what he writes"; each day, revise the previous day's page before writing new pages — do not start revising from the beginning every day (the opening will be brass while the back half goes slack); Coppola turns each page face down and does not revise until the draft is finished.
- Field: two or three hours a day, four days a week, three pages a day; "if in doubt, write"; the first draft should be too long rather than too short (180–200 pages); write the three stages (6–8 weeks) → compress to 130–140 pages → polish; when stuck, ask "what happens next?", go back to the character biography, and write one scene five ways from five angles.
- Hoxter: start a new scene before stopping work each day; you need not write in order; for major changes, revise the outline first; write in one continuous burst. Diamond: "more haste, less speed"; allow yourself a day or two of procrastination before starting to clear your mind; each day, reread the previous day's pages first.
- Bork: to find ideas, make long lists and cross-pair them; 15 minutes a day; generate quantity first and select later.

### Revision
- Walter's Principle 46: **real writing, especially film writing, is revision**; answer producers' requests with "yes, but…"; Principle 47: learn to take pleasure in cutting; Principle 48: when in doubt, throw it out; experiment (confirming that something must be discarded is also a success); leave the anger in the script.
- Diamond: the first draft is "something no normal person would buy"; work from the big idea (the three C's, the argument, genre expectations) down to details; **Mrs. Thomas-style revision** (each minor character has a distinct point of view and voice; cast them in your head); cutting: trim the head and tail ("a scene truly begins only when a clear goal is introduced and then supported or obstructed"), cut scenes without a purpose, remove two or three lines per page without losing force (on 《关岛登月》 (*Guam Moon Landing*), five pages were cut over a few hours and the director could not tell), rhythm matters more than page count; do not show it to industry people before you have done your best.
- Hoxter: technical check (typos, consistent format) → creative check (tighten description, unify voice) → inspect item by item against the goal (Act Two is usually the problem); save every version; print it out to revise; mark the arc and check it against the outline.
- Henson: use a checklist for character biographies; if six people read it and five like it, it is safe; if five say it is weak, start over.
- Field: keep a "best scenes" file; "if you are not sure whether a scene works, it probably won't"; show it to two friends who will tell the truth, not four.

## V. Adaptation

- **Field**: "apples and oranges" — an adapted screenplay is an original screenplay that starts from source material; it need not be faithful to the original, only to "the integrity of the source material"; 《总统班底》 (*All the President's Men*) ends at the halfway point of the book; Coppola's word-for-word fidelity to 《盖茨比》 (*The Great Gatsby*) → "a visually magnificent failure"; the novel writes inner life, drama writes language, and the screenplay writes external situation; when adapting a stage play, visualize events mentioned in the dialogue; in a historical drama the characters need not be accurate, but the events and their outcomes must be; a biography selects only a few events (the female editor uses that one news story as the hook); journalism moves from the specific to the general, while a screenplay moves from the general to the specific; the disaster novel begins on page 278 with the glacier measurement, and Act Three is entirely new.
- **McKee**: Principle One — the purer the novel (inner conflict) and the purer the drama (personal conflict), the worse the film; choose works with conflict on all three levels and an emphasis on the personal–extra-personal (《桂河桥》 (*The Bridge on the River Kwai*)); method: read the original repeatedly without taking notes until you "smell the cologne"; reduce each event to one or two sentences; ask "is this a well-told story?" (nine times out of ten it is not); Principle Two — be willing to recreate: rearrange time, make a step outline, cut and add scenes, find visual expressions for inner conflict; Forman: "give birth to your child a second time"; "the camera cannot photograph thought"; the novelist gives the best language to the narrator rather than to scene dialogue, so adaptation must reinvent from the inside out.
- **Hoxter's four questions**: Do you have the rights? (If not, do not do it.) Can it be filmed? (Is it visual?) What is the specificity of the original medium? (The panels and pages of a comic.) Can its thematic symbolic system be borrowed? Method: first write an outline "exactly as it is," then cut.
- **Bork on adapting true events**: reality does not have story structure; it must be organized, edited, manipulated, exaggerated, and supplemented with fiction; two main problems — why the goal matters and how difficult the process is ("the hair-raising process" makes the movie); in 《逃离德黑兰》 (*Argo*) there was no actual combat at the airport; Jost's method: get familiar with the material → decide what story to tell → write in one burst → go back and check it against the research.
- **Hicks on true stories**: consult a lawyer first; have the subject sign a "Life Story Option Agreement"; "do not assume that because you are writing about your sister or uncle you automatically have the rights"; public figures have rights, and the privacy rights of non-public figures are extremely important.
- Walter: adapting a best-selling novel is often disappointing (film cannot properly handle fantasy; you can count the buttons on a shirt but cannot match the shifting ideas in the mind); "invent a sweet lie rather than the truth."
- The five Shakespearean methods of 陆军 (Lu Jun) for transforming source material (raise the intelligence of minor characters, reposition the antagonist, strengthen the protagonist's intelligence and cut his submissiveness, patch holes in plausibility while incidentally writing character, reset the ending so that all characters stand on one line and the play closes rapidly) — see `sw-premise-theme`.
- **Adapting into a series** (novel/IP/true event/foreign drama → expansion into a long-form series) and **fragment adaptation** (finished film → compression into an 8–20 minute stage fragment) are two opposite crafts; see the two adaptation sections of `sw-chinese-series-practice` (the five-source typology of 张巍 (Zhang Wei) and 卖点迁移 (màidiǎn qiānyí — sell-point transfer); the four rules of 赵彬彬 (Zhao Binbin) and master-scene consolidation); for the engine assessment of series adaptation, see `sw-series-engine-bible`.

## VI. Diagnostic Checklist (four groups by format)

**Decide the format first, then run the checklist.** The old version of this checklist was written entirely for Hollywood features; anyone writing in the Chinese numbered-scene format would fail almost every item — that does not mean the draft is bad, it means the checklist was misapplied. It is now divided into four groups: **Group A is run for all three formats; Groups B, C, and D are run only for the one you are actually using.**

### A. General (independent of format and medium; run for all three)

1. **Action paragraphs**: present tense, active voice, no is/are, specific nouns and action verbs, no camera terminology, no "we see," no unfilmable inner states such as "thinks / remembers / realizes," no negative constructions such as "does not answer," and every sentence ideally ≤10 words?
2. **How many seconds of screen time does each action paragraph occupy?** Is it worth the audience's money? Does it have a purpose and a value, and is it the best way to tell this? (3 Strikes)
3. **The logic of the scene heading** (whichever format it is written in): are all the elements present, does it move from general to specific, and are details such as time and date in the action paragraph rather than the heading?
4. **The craft of dialogue**: no dialect transliteration, no grunts or moans, no emphasis through punctuation (underlining, exclamation points, a third ellipsis), parentheticals ≤3 words, verbs only, and deleted 99% of the time?
5. **Character introductions**: only sex, age, and one characteristic action; no character list, character biography, or casting suggestions?
6. **Only the master scene**? Can the montage be avoided or compressed into "a title + several salient features"? Is the flashback kept to a minimum, and will the reader not mistake the time and place?
7. **Before writing**, is there a one-page synopsis / theme statement / dramatic premise / character action chart / step outline? Have you talked the story through with someone for ten minutes?
8. Does the **treatment or outline** contain subtext and no dialogue? Which step in the document chain was skipped, and why?
9. In **revision**, do you move from the big idea to details? Have you trimmed the head and tail? Does each minor character have a distinct voice? Is rhythm prioritized over page count?
10. **Adaptation**: at what level does the source material's conflict mainly sit? What have you reinvented? Have the rights / option been signed? Have you found the true event's "why it matters" and "hair-raising process"?
11. **Length**: which conversion are you using? Has a Chinese draft been calculated with the conversion chain in II.2 (character count ÷ characters per minute = converted pages) and stated in the deliverable?
12. **Is the format consistent throughout**? Are two formats mixed (for example, numbered-scene scene lines with Hollywood-style action paragraphs)?

### B. Hollywood Style (spec / Fountain, English drafts)

13. Does the title page contain only the title and author? No scene numbers, date, draft number, or registration number? **Courier 12**? Page count **90–120**?
14. Does the layout match the table in II.2 (left 1.5″, dialogue column starts at 2.5″ and is 3.5″ wide, character name 3.7″, transitions right-aligned)?
15. Is a character's first appearance in **ALL CAPS** with age? Are props and sound effects capitalized?
16. Is dialogue **unbroken across pages**; do page breaks have `(MORE)` / `(CONT'D)`?
17. Are **(V.O.) and (O.S.) used correctly** (not in the scene's space / in the space but off-screen)?
18. Does the whole script **never mention the camera** and never write "CUT TO" (every scene ends in a natural cut)?
19. Is the deliverable **`.fountain` plain text**, not Markdown and not Word?

### C. Chinese Numbered-Scene Format (Chinese film and series industry drafts)

20. Is the scene line written `场号 时 内/外 地点` (series write `集-场`)? Have you **chosen one** of the two schools, "日 内" or "内 日," and kept it consistent throughout?
21. Is the action paragraph consistently marked with `△` (or consistently ▲, or consistently no mark)?
22. Is dialogue written `人名：台词`, with parentheticals after the name (`沈越（低声）：`)?
23. Are off-screen voice and narration written `（OS）`/`（画外）` and `（VO）`/`（旁白）`, one style throughout?
24. **No transitions, no camera terminology**? Flashbacks marked with `（回忆）` in the scene line? Titles written `字幕：`?
25. Is the character count within range (a 40–45 minute series episode is about 12,000–15,000 characters; other lengths calculated with the conversion chain in II.2)? Does the dialogue proportion match the genre?
26. Is the deliverable **plain text** (no Fountain, no Markdown notation)?
27. Have you mistakenly applied Hollywood hard rules to your own work (Courier 12, 90–120 pages, ALL-CAPS character names) — **these do not hold for Chinese drafts**?

### D. Japanese Style (柱・ト書き・セリフ)

28. Is the 柱 written `○地点・细部（时间）`, with the scene number before `○`?
29. Is the ト書き first line indented **three full-width characters**? On a character's first appearance, is the age given (`沈越（３４）`)?
30. Is セリフ written `人物名「台词」` (full-width quotation marks directly after the name, **no colon**)? Are parentheticals written after the name?
31. Is the passage of time shown with `　　×　　×　　×`? Is a pause written `（間）`?
32. Are narration `（Ｎ）`, monologue `（Ｍ）`, and recollection `（回想）` marked in the right places?
33. Are the draft stages written on the cover (準備稿 → 改訂稿 → 決定稿)?

### What If the Format Is Not Decided

Use the default from II.2: **projects with all-Chinese dialogue use the numbered-scene format, English projects use Hollywood style / Fountain, and Japanese style is used only when explicitly requested; Chinese opera scripts are not covered and follow the opera skill's format**; and state the format and conversion method in one sentence at the top of the deliverable. **Do not stop just because there is no one to ask.**

## VII. Workflow: From Idea to Submission-Ready Draft

1. A one- or two-sentence logline + a one-page synopsis → get feedback on the idea from three people (Bork / Diamond: "I have to have this" versus "Okay, I get it").
2. The three C's + a list of comparable films + a one-page nine-step synopsis + the argument (theme).
3. Theme statement and dramatic premise (Henson); character action chart (Diamond); three-dimensional dossier (see `sw-character-conflict`).
4. Step outline / scene cards (McKee / Field / Walter): one or two sentences per scene, marked with the value turn; plot integration + transition scenes + subplots + set-piece placement.
5. Talk the story for ten minutes; treatment (no dialogue, with subtext) or prose treatment.
6. First draft: count pages daily, better too long than too short, do not go back and revise more than the previous page.
7. Revision draft: remove director's directions, furniture, adjectives and adverbs; change to present tense; cut two or three lines per page; trim the head and tail; Mrs. Thomas-style revision; technical check.
8. Agent draft: show it only to critics who understand scripts; six readers, five liking it.
9. Submission draft: PDF; minimal title page; WGA registration (not written on the cover).
