## II. Hicks's "Harem Format Template," Paragraph by Paragraph

- Scene heading: INT. ISLAMIC HAREM – DAY — quickly establishes the place and the time of day.
- The action description first gives a large, wide picture to establish the scene and introduce the harem women; the introduction of the eunuch guards need not explain how the camera moves, only let the reader meet them and remember them.
- Fatima's "dry, shriveled face like a dried persimmon" — described through an abstract but characteristic action, not fat or thin, tall or short.
- Avoid camera instructions such as MS, close-up, or pan right; "the eunuch opens the door, the wind lifts the silk garments, all eyes notice the bronze door" suggests a return to the wide shot and the introduction of a new element.
- The caliph enters quickly — important characters are given names and actions quickly; "waves his hand" — a good actor will decide the right wave.
- Parenthetical direction "(coldly)": if the scene's intention is clear enough, no parenthetical is needed; use one only when it is unclear which person in a group is being addressed.
- The sound cue "the crisp metallic sound of ankle bells" suggests that something serious is about to happen — write only sounds that change attention (traffic noise while sitting alone is unimportant; a car door closing when you expect to be killed is a life-or-death signal).
- V.O. = the speaker's voice off-screen; O.S. = an off-screen sound made to catch the attention of a character on-screen (an off-screen explosion makes the people at the dinner table jump); both are used with extreme rigor.
- "(eyes on her new rival)" — ask yourself whether it helps tell the story or is self-indulgent decoration.
- Transitions: cut (simple, immediate change), dissolve (a longer time or a larger emotional change), fade in (several years); their function on the page is to make the reader one hundred percent aware that the master scene has changed. The new master scene "EXT. BANDITS' CAMP – DAY" — Fatima's "every rose has its thorn" carries the tension into the new scene, and the rest is the writer's responsibility: make something happen.

## III. Henson's Format Specification Checklist

- Courier 10 CPI = Courier New 12; white paper, one side; left 1.5–2 inches, right ≥1, top 1, bottom 0.5–1.5, kept consistent; faking page count by changing the white space can be caught with a ruler.
- Title page: title in large type, underlined, centered 3 inches from the top; name, address, and phone in the lower right corner, single-spaced; no name on other pages; no WGA registration number or copyright.
- Single-spaced within action paragraphs, double-spaced between paragraphs; "FADE IN:" only at the beginning, "FADE OUT" only at the end.
- Scene headings: INT./EXT. + location + DAY/NIGHT; do not write "EXT. OUTSIDE THE PALACE" or "INT. INSIDE THE VILLAGE"; details go in the action paragraph.
- Action paragraphs are like a list; they contain the given circumstances (time, season, weather; environment affecting action; physical details that provide clues; details of action that reveal character and emotion); four points: visualize; decide what the audience looks at; write visual images that convey meaning without explanation; write only what the camera can photograph and the actor can play.
- Suggest shots through the text: scene heading = wide shot, a flag = medium shot, Darren walking to the microphone = close-up; the end of every scene is naturally a cut.
- Capitalize a character on first appearance; minor characters designated by attribute (TWO POLICEMEN, A WAITER) are also capitalized; "is a corpse a character? Only if it speaks."
- Dialogue starts 3 inches from the left margin and is 3.25 inches wide; do not break across pages: interrupt with action, let a second character interject a word or two, and if there is no other way, "(SEE NEXT PAGE)" + "NAME (CONT'D)" with at least two lines on each page; five or six lines or more should make you reconsider (Hamlet syndrome).
- Parentheses: one or two words, never more than three, verbs only (smiling) (nervous) (angry); "using parentheses means doing the director's job on paper."
- Transition criteria: change of place/time, change in the number of characters, change of dramatic purpose; double-space between the last line of dialogue and the next heading.
- Sound effects: bold one or two words only within an action paragraph; do not write a visible source (pouring rain, a dog barking); write an invisible source (thunder, a phone ringing, a dog barking off-screen); (OS) for off-screen dialogue, (VO) for narration only for material that can be neither seen nor heard; special effects: write only what the audience sees.
- Passive voice is a curse; "she stands there thinking" cannot be filmed; adjectives and adverbs must be cut.
- Exercises: adapt 7–10 pages of a novel into 3–5 script pages without parentheses, OS, VO, or narration; reverse-write the first 5 minutes of a favorite film into script form.

## IV. Henson's Two Versions of 《新鲜的奶酪》 (*Fresh Cheese*)

**Bad version**: INT. UNITED NATIONS – DAY. "For the past hour the delegates have been listening to Dash hold forth… holding forth in a spacesuit." Dash: "I have made an astonishing discovery analyzing rock samples on the moon. Fresh cheese." An ambassador, disdainful: "This Dash has been an eccentric rogue since the day he joined the space agency." An aide: "Perhaps we should call General Holz." — Backstory begins, past tense and passive voice, the impression of Dash comes from other people's descriptions, and the characters keep supplying information to help the audience "get into" the scene while the action stands still.
**Good version**: EXT. LUNAR BASE – DAY. Dash in a spacesuit collects the last sample, puts it in his pocket, and walks toward the survival module; INT. SURVIVAL MODULE – DAY. Dash, in a motorcycle jacket, jeans, and tattoos, puts on a record; Mozart fills the air; he pours out the sample, a pale green lump, picks it up and smells it, opens a switchblade, cuts a slice, puts it in his mouth: "My God, it's cheese!"; INT. UNITED NATIONS – DAY. Dash waves the cheese: "We can solve world hunger and bring permanent peace to the world." Low murmurs. The ambassador looks disdainful. The aide presses his phone: "General Holz? One moment." The ambassador takes the phone: "Harvey? Dash just finished. (listens) Yes, in my office. Ten minutes. Bring grenades and a bazooka." — Costume, music, and taste infer that Dash is unusual without anyone saying so; transitive verbs, present tense, and active voice reveal action and provoke reaction.

## V. McKee's Three-Step Method and a Treatment Example

- **Step-outline card**: "He enters the house expecting to see her, but finds a note saying she has left forever"; structural position on the back; the purpose is to destroy your own work — sketch one scene a dozen ways and then throw them away, destroy sequences, destroy acts; the filing cabinet holds notes on biography / world / theme / imagery.
- **Talking the story for ten minutes**: do not show the outline; an enthusiastic reaction = a soft "wow" followed by silence; "ten minutes won't do it; a hundred and ten minutes is ten times worse."
- **Treatment**: expand each scene into one or more double-spaced, present-tense, moment-by-moment paragraphs, no dialogue, write the subtext (Jack truly hopes she is not home, smashes her precious Chippendale chair, tears of relief); 60–90 pages.
- **Screenplay**: five to ten pages a day; dialogue is best at this point (the characters have been gagged so long that they are eager to speak, and each has a voice); if you discover a flaw, go back to the treatment and revise the setup and payoff.
- Description: absolute present tense; specific nouns and action verbs; never use is/are throughout; metaphors must pass the test "what do you see/hear on the screen?" (a door like a gunshot is fine; "the sun sinks slowly like a tiger's eye" is a director's trap); delete "we see/hear"; in the master scene write only the absolutely necessary angles, and use single-spaced short paragraphs to suggest changes of shot (wide → tracking → close-up of the note → close-up of the ring → medium shot).

## VI. Diamond & Weissman: The One-Page Nine Steps and the Character Action Chart

**Nine steps**: ① Opening: establish the character and the situation. ② Introduce the opportunity or challenge. ③ First act turn: the character makes an irreversible decision. ④ A turn around page 45: new information that overturns the rules and sharply raises the stakes. ⑤ Midpoint reversal: a larger, more unexpected change forces a regrouping. ⑥ Escalation around page 75. ⑦ End of Act Two: the protagonist seems to lose everything. ⑧ Then it gets worse: the final test. ⑨ Final battle or set piece — dig deep and awaken qualities you never knew you had.

**《星球大战》 (*Star Wars*) in nine steps**: Luke, stuck on the farm, wants to go to the academy → Leia's distress message in R2 → Obi-Wan asks him to come along; he refuses; his aunt and uncle are killed → he hires Han Solo and sets out for Alderaan, learns the Force on the way, and arrives to find it destroyed → drawn into the Death Star, the princess is imprisoned, and the "delivery" becomes a rescue → the princess is rescued, Obi-Wan sacrifices himself → Han takes his reward and leaves, the Empire tracks the ship and learns the location of the base → fighters attack the Death Star and are shot down one by one; Han swoops in and shoots down the wingman → the Death Star is destroyed; the hero.

**《回溯时光》 (*Turning Back Time*) in nine steps** (the author's own pitch): a physicist and his lifelong best friend test a device that goes back a few minutes in time → greedy for fame and fortune, he is framed by a rival and fired → he proposes to use science as a "problem solver" → his first client refuses; he helps a victim recover $5,000, makes the news, and then sets his price at $1 million → everything goes smoothly; he falls in love with a science reporter and tells her the core secret → the reporter betrays him and publishes it on the front page → he is unemployed again, facing lawsuits, his friendship beyond repair, "and there is an even bigger problem" → a secret Defense Department operation creates a sinkhole that will expand into a black hole, and they are needed → they reunite to complete one last mission, arriving in 1977 to regain friendship and fortune.

**Character action chart** (Han Solo): does not appear in Act One; at minute 45 he appears in the cantina, purely for money, owing Jabba a debt; Alderaan is destroyed and the princess is imprisoned — for Luke the delivery becomes a rescue, an escalation; Han does not care, "She's rich" — "How rich?" — and joins again for money; escaping the Death Star, Obi-Wan sacrifices himself — Luke is at his lowest while Han is at his highest (paid double, exulting in the escape), and Leia sees his ability but is repelled by his cynicism; they find the Death Star's weakness and Han takes his money and leaves, "completely understandable but disappointing"; the climax is "the Han Solo move"; he and Chewie are honored. "Can you tell the story from every character's point of view?"

**Transition scene**: the Millennium Falcon from Tatooine to Alderaan — you cannot cut immediately to arrival, nor can you just sit there eating peanuts; the answer is in the character chart: a teacher / a student eager to learn / a miser forced to stay together → Obi-Wan teaches the lightsaber and the Force while Han sneers at the Jedi.

**The breast-pump set piece from 《陪产假》 (*Paternity Leave*)** (an example of a trailer moment): Jack looks for milk and finds only a bottle of breast milk, about to pour it into his coffee → "Do you know how hard I worked to pump that one bottle?" → they go shopping → they open a cupboard and see a double breast pump → they plug it in, in the living room, "whirr, whirr, whirr," and find it amusing → he takes off his shirt and attaches it to his chest → Dodgers game with beer → three hours later he falls asleep still pumping → Peg's mother walks in: "No…" → he jumps up and is pulled down by the weight of the machine → he removes the cups, two bottles with three and a half ounces of milk each → emergency room: "Male lactation… actually fairly common in POW camps" → "I'll make you a protective device" → "You're about a… B cup?"

## VII. Hoxter: Outline and Treatment

- John August's paragraph outline for 《大鱼》 (*Big Fish*): "Paragraph 01, pages 1–6, Catfish: all his life Will's father has told the story of the big fish; he is still telling it at the wedding; father and son argue" … "Paragraph 04: Will receives a phone call."
- Terry Rossio's opening for the 《佐罗的面具》 (*The Mask of Zorro*) treatment: "Zorro sails in and robs the execution ground… a black ghost in the moonlight; Alejandro and Joaquín watch, dumbstruck."
- The four adaptation questions: Do you have the rights? Can it be filmed (is it visual)? What is the specificity of the original medium (the panels and pages of a comic)? Can its thematic symbolic system be borrowed? First write an outline "exactly as it is," then cut.
- 《爱乐之城》 (*La La Land*) opening: white space and section breaks = individual shots; "the camera drifts, we see more cars" suggests a tracking shot; use sound to mark the image (progressive rock / opera / hip-hop / French folk → an original song).
- 《谍影重重》 (*The Bourne Identity*) fishing-boat surgery: "Now — Giancarlo holds the flashlight in his teeth — ting — ting — ting — shrapnel drops into the olive can" — double hyphens = jump cut; onomatopoeia expresses genre; "do not copy the style; ask what it is trying to achieve."
- 《迷失东京》 (*Lost in Translation*): "Bob is distracted" (behavior, not emotion); "They do not know what to say. For some reason breakfast is awkward. Charlotte notices her own every movement as she eats."

## VIII. Field: Format Elements and "the Subject of the Shot"

- `EXT. ARIZONA DESERT – DAY`; two blank lines; new characters in bold; speaker names centered; dialogue centered; sound and music in bold; `CUT TO:` (fades and dissolves are decided by the director/editor).
- Rule: identify the subject of the shot (Bill walks out of the apartment: the subject is Bill; Bill drives down the street: the subject is the car).
- Twelve terms to replace "camera": angle on / favoring / another angle / wider angle / new angle / POV / reverse angle / over-the-shoulder / moving shot / two-shot / close shot (use sparingly) / insert.
- Do not number scenes; the title page contains only the title, author, address, and phone.
- Adaptation cases: the disaster novel begins on page 278 with the glacier measurement; the Halloween blizzard = Plot Point I; the ship frozen in the ice = Plot Point II; Act Three is entirely new — nine people living like Eskimos, ending with a birth. The biography of the female editor-in-chief: 26 pages of chronology → changed to use the news story that earned her the editor-in-chief position as the "hook."
- The three stages of collaboration: ground rules / preparation / writing (thirty pages as a unit, editing each other); four roles: author, researcher, recorder, editor.

## IX. Proposal Structure by 大宫艾丽 (Omiya Ellie) for 《木下部长和我》 (*Section Chief Kinoshita and Me*) (an Original 企画書 Template)

1 Story synopsis; 2 Characters and important roles (catchphrases, mottos, viewpoint character and contrast character); 3 Synopses of episodes 1 and 2, and titles up to episode 12; 4 Purpose and selling points of the proposal (identify the main appeal and give comparable works). "If it is original, it must be even more accessible and easy to understand; write down the key points and goals."

### 10.3 Example of the Chinese Film and Television Numbered-Scene Format

```
1. 夜 内 沈越家·客厅
人物：沈越、小七

△ 雨打在窗上。客厅中央立着一只人形大小的白色运输箱。
△ 沈越（34岁，胡子三天没刮）蹲在箱前，用指甲划开封条。箱盖打开。一个女人躺在软槽里，闭着眼。
△ 沈越按住她锁骨下方的凹点，数三秒。女人睁开眼。

小七：请说出您的名字。
沈越：沈越。
小七：沈越。绑定完成。请问怎么称呼您？
沈越（不看她）：随便。
小七：请指定一个称呼。
沈越：……就叫我沈越。

△ 她坐起来，转头看这间屋子，一处一处地看。

2. 日 内 沈越家·客厅
人物：沈越、小七

△ 窗外雨停了。她还站在原地，姿势和昨夜一模一样。
沈越（OS）：你一夜没……
```

Variants: scene lines may also be written `第1场 内 夜 沈越家客厅` or `1-1 沈越家·客厅 内 夜`; for the action mark, some companies use `▲`, or no mark at all, instead of `△`; series drafts write the scene number as `集-场`. Consistency within one draft is all that matters.

### 10.4 Japanese Script Example (柱・ト書き・セリフ)

```
　１　○沈越の部屋・居間（夜）

　　　雨が窓を打つ。
　　　部屋の中央に、人の大きさの白い輸送ケース。
　　　沈越（３４）、無精ひげ、ケースの前にしゃがみ、爪で封を切る。
　　　蓋が開く。女が目を閉じて横たわっている。
　　　沈越、彼女の鎖骨の下のくぼみを押さえ、三つ数える。
　　　女、目を開ける。

小七「お名前をおっしゃってください」
沈越「沈越」
小七「沈越。登録完了。何とお呼びすればよろしいですか」
沈越（見ずに）「何でもいい」
小七「呼び名を指定してください」
沈越「……沈越でいい」

　　　彼女、起き上がり、部屋を一つ一つ見回す。

　　　　　　×　　　×　　　×

　２　○同・居間（朝）

　　　雨は上がっている。彼女、昨夜と同じ姿勢で立っている。
沈越（声）「一晩中……」
```

Key points: `○` before the 柱 (hashira — the scene line), with the time in parentheses after the location; ト書き (tosho — action description) indented three full-width characters at the start of the paragraph, with age given on a character's first appearance; セリフ (serifu — dialogue) written `人名「…」`, with parentheticals after the name; `×　×　×` indicates the passage of time; `（声）` for off-screen voice, `（Ｎ）` for narration, `（Ｍ）` for monologue; consecutive scenes in the same location are written `○同・居間`. Mark 準備稿／改訂稿／決定稿 (preparatory draft / revised draft / final draft) on the cover.
