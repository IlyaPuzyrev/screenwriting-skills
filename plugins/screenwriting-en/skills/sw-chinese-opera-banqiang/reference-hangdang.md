# Chinese Opera Playwriting · Banqiang (板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ — the paired-line opera system) Reference: Role Types (行当, hángdang) and Lead-Performer Profiles

> Use alongside SKILL.md.

This file covers one thing only: **how role types and performer conditions constrain the words and scenes the playwright writes**. Vocal technique, movement training, and performer anecdotes are excluded; every entry lands on "how the playwright writes from this." Each table gives its source (notebook directory + original page number); missing cells are filled with "—"; where the notes are silent, the cell says "not recorded in the notes."

## §1 The Definitional Layer of Role Types: Zhang Geng and Guo Hancheng's Five Factors

Source: zhang-guo/06, Chapter 7, pp. 0341–0347; also cited: zhang-guo/02 pp. 0150–0151, zhang-guo/04 pp. 0217–0218, zhang-guo/03 p0175, zhang-guo/05 pp. 0250–0251.

### 1.1 Definition

> "Role type is a concept with a double meaning... in short, **it is both an image system and a convention system**." (程式, chéngshì; p0341) Two companion statements: fixing the role type "**breaks the limitation of portraying character by literary language alone**" (pp. 0150–0151); such a character image "**has already been fully portrayed in the literary script**" (p0218).

**How the playwright writes from this:** writing "laosheng (lǎoshēng — mature male role) / qingyi (qīngyī — dignified singing heroine) / jiazi hualian (jiàzi huāliǎn — 'frame' painted-face role)" in the character table is not a classification label but **a switch that summons a whole sequence of conventions at once**; if the role type is not fixed, you are left with literary language as your only means.

### 1.2 The Five Constituent Factors (pp. 0342–0344)

| # | Factor | Key quotation | How the playwright writes from this |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Natural attributes** | Age, sex | Write age as a playable number or form of address ("in her fifties," "old sister-in-law"), not merely "middle-aged"; change the sex or age and the whole set of conventions changes |
| 2 | **Social attributes** (**core**) | Status, occupation, personality, temperament. Shenyin Jiangu Lu (《审音鉴古录》 — A Mirror of Sound and Antiquity) notes on Qian Yulian (钱玉莲): "**In movement she uses only four-inch steps... if she lets her heels slip into a stride, it is a rustic walk.**" On Wang Shipeng's mother (王十朋's mother, a laodan): "**Though the lady is old, she was born a young miss; her dress may be worn, but her bearing and sense of propriety remain.**" | **This is the cell that determines the role type.** Answer three questions first: What is the status? How much of it remains? Which part of the past governs the bearing? "Her dress may be worn, but her bearing and sense of propriety remain" can go straight into a stage direction |
| 3 | **Comic or serious style** | The chou (chǒu — comic role) splits into wen chou (wénchǒu — civil comic) and wu chou (wǔchǒu — martial comic); wen chou further splits into paodai chou (páodàichǒu — official-robe comic), fangjin chou (fāngjīnchǒu — square-cap comic), xizi chou (xìzichǒu — robe comic, also called gongzi chou), chayi chou (cháyīchǒu — tea-cloth comic), and lao chou (lǎochǒu — old comic) — "**though they differ in civil or martial, good or evil, high or low status, all are comic figures**"; sheng (shēng — male role), dan (dàn — female role), and jing (jìng — painted-face role), "apart from huadan (huādàn — vivacious young woman) and certain erhualian (èrhuāliǎn — secondary painted-face) characters who carry comic color, are generally serious-role types" | The right to carry comedy is concentrated in the chou role and in certain huadan and erhualian characters. **Assigning a laugh to a serious role type is as good as writing it dead.** **Self-check:** mark every laugh and see whom it lands on |
| 4 | **Aesthetic evaluation** | In The Peach Blossom Fan (《桃花扇》), the "proper color" (zhèngsè — orthodox character) Hou Chaozong (侯朝宗) and Li Xiangjun (李香君) are played by sheng and dan; the "malignant qi" (lìqì — violent, corrupt aura) Ruan Dacheng (阮大铖), Ma Shiying (马士英), and the Hongguang Emperor (弘光帝) are played by jing and fujing with white-painted faces; Liu Jingting (柳敬亭), Cai Yisuo (蔡益所, chou), and Su Kunsheng (苏昆生, jing) are "insufficient proper color" borrowings — "**whenever a proper-color role borrows from chou or jing... the powder and ink are temporarily washed off at entrance**"; the "intermediate color" (jiānsè) Yang Longyou (杨龙友) is played by **mo** (mò — supporting male role). Kong Shangren (孔尚任): "**Role types (脚色, juésè) are what distinguish gentlemen from villains.**" | To fix a role type is to **make a judgment**; refusing to make it does not help — the audience has already read it from the costume and makeup. To write a good person of "insufficient proper color," the old method is to **borrow chou or jing and temporarily wash off the powder and ink**, not to give him a speech in self-defense |
| 5 | **Technical specialty** | The laosheng, qingyi, and dahualian (dàhuāliǎn — "great painted face") "all have solid singing skills"; huadan "stresses acting and spirit... yet must sing with grace and agility"; changkai wusheng (chángkào wǔshēng — armored martial male) "stresses the grandeur of posture and stance"; duanda wusheng (duǎndǎ wǔshēng — short-clad martial male) "excels in agile, light movement and in falls, tumbles, flips, and fights"; Xiang opera's "big-footed old-woman dan" has "more than a dozen" stage walks | This determines **how the four skills — song, speech, acting, and martial action (唱念做打, chàng niàn zuò dǎ) — are allocated**. Writing a long manban passage for a huadan, or a full set of weeping tunes for a changkai wusheng, leaves the specialty unused and forces the performer to do work he does not do |

### 1.3 Three Functions, Three Terms (pp. 0345–0346)

| Item | Key quotation | How the playwright writes from this |
|---|---|---|
| **Function one · immediacy** | Zhuge Liang (诸葛亮), Guan Yu (关羽), Zhou Yu (周瑜), White Snake (白娘子), Zhang Fei (张飞), Cao Cao (曹操), Bao Zheng (包拯), and Jiang Gan (蒋干) "**need almost no introduction once they enter**" | **Do not write introductory dialogue for a character whose role type is already fixed** |
| **Function two · no abstract convention** | "The same qiba (qǐbà — martial entrance-and-posturing sequence) is performed with different relish by sheng, dan, jing, and chou; the same tangma (tàngmǎ — horse-riding mime)... each has its own style" | It is legitimate to write "qiba" or "tangma," but the length and breath points differ entirely depending on which role type performs it; first confirm whose it is |
| **Function three** | Role types are the professional division of labor among performers | See §3 |
| **Home type (本工, běngōng)** | A character in one's own role type (Zhuge Liang in The Empty City Stratagem [《空城计》] is laosheng home type) | Default approach: whatever the character table marks, write according to that role type's syntax, proportions, and specialty |
| **Also-performed type (应工, yìnggōng)** | Not one's home type but conventionally also performed (in the Kunqu play The Iron Crown [《铁冠图》], Wang Chengen [王承恩] is also played by a laodan) | When a troupe lacks a star of a certain role type, first check whether a conventional undertaking already exists |
| **Two-door (两门抱, liǎngménbào)** | Can be played by either of two role types (Xiang Yu [项羽] in Farewell My Concubine [《霸王别姬》] can be taken by the painted-face or the martial-male route) | **The script must still lean toward one door** (song-heavy or acting-heavy), or neither performer knows where to begin. See §2.3 |

### 1.4 Three Flexibilities

| Item | Basis | How the playwright writes from this |
|---|---|---|
| **Creating a new type across role boundaries** | In Iron Cage Mountain (《铁笼山》), Jiang Wei (姜维); in Bright Sun Tower (《艳阳楼》), Gao Deng (高登); in The Scholar's Seal (《状元印》), Chang Yuchun (常遇春) — shifted from wujing to changkai wusheng while keeping the painted face = "**painted-face wusheng** (勾脸武生, gōuliǎn wǔshēng)"; Wang Yaoqing (王瑶卿) fused qingyi, huadan, and daomadan = "**daomadan huadan** (刀马花旦, dāomǎ huādàn)" (p0347). The test case for breaking the rule (p0155) is Mu Guiying (穆桂英): "**the role-type norm came into conflict with the character's personality**"; the solution was "postures larger than a qingyi's but more lettered than a daomadan's" | When a character needs "a qingyi's weight plus a daomadan's physical prowess," the solution is **to create a new route**, not to split the difference |
| **Changing role type mid-play** | In the Sichuan opera Questioning the Illness and Storming the Palace (《问病逼宫》), Yang Guang (杨广): **xiaosheng** (xiǎoshēng — young male role; respectable appearance) → **hualian** (huāliǎn — painted face; after poisoning his mother) → **xiaochou** (xiǎochǒu — young comic; after seizing the imperial seal, a monkey decked out in a hat; zhang-guo/03 p0175) | **The moment of changing role type must be pinned to a definite event**, not merely a shift of mood. For a softer version, see §3, Zhou Xinfang: "changing to a white beard = changing role-type route" |
| **For transplants, fix the role type first** | In The Fisherman's Revenge (《打渔杀家》), Xiao En (萧恩): played by a Peking-opera laosheng, "the language is vigorous, seasoned, and sharp"; played by a clapper-opera jing, "the language is rough, bold, and unrestrained" (zhang-guo/05 p0250) | The first step in transplanting across genres is **fixing the role type**, not translating words; change the role type and the whole language style changes with it — not one original line can be carried over directly |

### 1.5 The Bottom Line: Seek Individuality Within the Type

> "**The performing art of any role type cannot replace the character's individuality**... one must seek individuality within the type." (p0347) The Sichuan-opera maxim "**one set of conventions, ten thousand characters**" (pp. 0217–0218). Li Yu's (李渔) qualification (zhang-guo/05 pp. 0250–0251): "Role-type language is, after all, not individualized language but typified language. **If one relies solely on role-type language to shape character, it is very hard to create a host of vividly individualized figures.**" Example: the three dan roles in The Romance of the Western Chamber (《西厢记》) — Yingying (莺莺), Hongniang (红娘, a tiedan [tiēdàn — secondary dan role]), and the old lady (laodan).

**Self-check:** if you remove the names from the arias of two or three characters of the same role type in one play, swap them, and cannot tell the difference, you have written only the role type and not the people.

### 2.1 The Six Routes of the Xiaosheng

Supplemented by: the "Five Sons Pass the Examination" (wǔzǐ dēngkē) skill set (weng-lunwenji/04 p0210), one performer across four routes (same volume, pp. 0229–0230), The Complete Zhou Yu (《全部周瑜》; weng-bianju/03 p0197), and the xiaosheng subdivisions (liu/02 pp. 0148–0150).

| Route | Typical character / plays | Proportion of song and speech | Line-level approach when writing him | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pheasant-feather sheng / lingzi sheng** (雉尾生／翎子生, zhìwěishēng / língzisheng; home type) | Zhou Yu in Gathering of Heroes (《群英会》), The Feast at the River (《临江会》), Phoenix Pavilion (《凤仪亭》), Flooding Xiapi (《水淹下邳》), Shooting the Halberd at the Camp Gate (《辕门射戟》), Yellow Crane Tower (《黄鹤楼》) | Song, speech, acting, and martial action are equally weighted, **with acting and posture depending on pheasant-feather work**; audiences "felt that Zhou Yu seemed somehow deficient without pheasant feathers" | Leave **gaps in the dialogue where the feathers can answer** — "the feathers take a stand"; do not chain every line together: repeated words and long melismas are the openings left for the feathers and for the other performer | weng-bianju/05 p0260; weng-lunwenji/04 p0210, pp. 0229–0230 |
| **Singing xiaosheng** (唱工小生, chànggōng xiǎoshēng) | Luo Cheng (罗成) in Luo Cheng (《罗成》) and The Wine-Inspection Order (《监酒令》) | **Primarily song** | The xiaosheng voice is "a composite of the dragon, tiger, and phoenix tones: **the dragon tone is high and soaring, the tiger tone broad and rich, the phoenix tone soft and graceful**"; the lyrics must leave room for all three — **a high, soaring opening line (dragon), a graceful transition (phoenix), a broad, rich closing line (tiger)**. All high lines or all soft lines force the performer into a weird, affected delivery | weng-bianju/05 p0260; weng-lunwenji/04 pp. 0211–0212 |
| **Fan sheng** (扇子生, shànzisheng) | A Fortunate Match (《得意缘》), Winning the Flower Queen Alone (《独占花魁》), the Kunqu plays The Interrupted Dream (《惊梦》) and Testing the Lute (《琴挑》); Zhou Yu in the "Stealing the Letter" episode of Gathering of Heroes (martial-sheng headdress + stiff robe) | Song and acting are equally weighted, **with the acting hanging on the fan and the water sleeves of the stiff robe** | "The fan conveys feeling": **repeat the same fan movement three times, changing one measurable parameter each time** (amplitude / height / force); that is an emotional curve without dialogue. | **Official-robe sheng** (官衣生, guānyīshēng) | The Strange Double Meeting (《奇双会》), The Jade Hall in Spring; Zhou Yu in the "Matching the Fire Characters" episode of Gathering of Heroes (scholar's cap + paired robe) | Primarily speech and acting, with emphasis on "presence" | Write **the tone of official business**: words that acknowledge duty, observe measure, and can be held accountable | weng-bianju/05 p0260; weng-lunwenji/04 pp. 0229–0230 |
| **Martial xiaosheng** (武小生, wǔxiǎoshēng) | Lu Wenlong (陆文龙) in The Eight Great Hammers (《八大锤》); Battle of Puyang (《战濮阳》), Elegant View Tower (《雅观楼》), Borrowing Zhao Yun (《借赵云》); Zhou Yu before and after "Burning the War Boats" (in full armor) | **Martial action comes first**, with pheasant-feather work and armor work ("the weapons speak") | A maximum of six to eight sung lines, preferably sanban or yaoban; **song must be inserted between one fight and the next** (even one or two sanban lines), or the audience cannot tell the layers of the battle apart | weng-bianju/05 p0260; weng-lunwenji/04 p0210; weng-lunwenji/09 p0033, p0038 |
| **Poor sheng** (穷生, qióngshēng) | Chen Daguan (陈大官) in The Top Scholar's Register (《状元谱》), An Daoquan (安道全) in The Qinhuai River (《秦淮河》), Wang Mingfang (王明芳) in The Rapid-Promotion Inn (《连升店》) — none are leads; Zhou Ren (周仁) in Tears of the Mandarin Ducks (《鸳鸯泪》) / Zhou Ren Gives Up His Wife (《周仁献嫂》); Kunqu's "straw-sandal sheng"; clapper opera's "upper eight plays" | Song and acting are equally weighted, **with the acting hanging on the soft robe (a plain blue robe, in the same family as the "wealth-and-honor garment") and on straw-sandal work** ("the robe confides") | The poor sheng **has no play in the traditional repertoire capable of carrying the final slot of a bill**; this is a ready-made gap for newly written plays. The technical checklist for Zhou Ren can serve as a model for building a full poor-sheng play: gauze-cap wings / water sleeves / eye work / footwork / slipping the sleeve and shedding the robe / kicking off the shoe and changing the face / the diàomáo tumble, qiǎngbèi shoulder roll, and shuǎifà hair swing — **one skill per scene** | weng-bianju/05 p0260, p0264; weng-lunwenji/04 p0210, p0214, p0220 |

Note to the table: ① **Wawasheng** (娃娃生, wáwáshēng — child role; Xue Yige [薛倚哥] in The Third Lady Teaches Her Son [《三娘教子》]) belongs to a separate category (liu/02 pp. 0148–0150). ② **One performer can cross several routes, and route changes are marked by costume changes** (weng-lunwenji/04 pp. 0229–0230): Zhou Yu = fan sheng (martial-sheng headdress + stiff robe) → official-robe sheng (scholar's cap + paired robe) → martial xiaosheng (full armor) → lingzi sheng (home type), "**each assigned to Zhou Yu as needed**... the so-called **civil skin, martial bones**." → For a character who runs through the whole play, **list scene by scene which route he belongs to**; this table is his "role-route design" and is far more useful to the performer than a character biography.

Note to the table · **Cutting a play into a role-type sequence (a positive example):** A Hundred Birds Pay Homage to the Phoenix has Song Dezhu (宋德珠) alone threading through four dan role types — huadan + assassin dan → ghost dan → caidan → wudan weapon work (weng-bianju/02 pp. 0116–0127). → However many routes the lead can perform, cut the play into that many segments, **changing the technical route in each**; pin the segments together with plot events (death, return of the ghost, change of costume).

### 2.3 The Jing Role: Three Branches, Four Great Branches, Three Singing Styles

**(a) Three branches** (liu/02 pp. 0148–0150):

| Branch | Other name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 正净 (zhèngjìng — primary painted face) | — | Xu Yanzhao (徐延昭) in Entering the Palace a Second Time (《二进宫》), Yao Qi (姚期) in Caogiao Pass (《草桥关》) |
| 副净 (fùjìng — secondary painted face) | 架子花脸 (jiàzi huāliǎn — "frame" painted face) | Ma Wu (马武) in Taking Luoyang (《取洛阳》), Ma Su (马谡) in Losing Jieting (《失街亭》) |
| 武净 (wǔjìng — martial painted face) | 武花脸 (wǔhuāliǎn — martial painted face) | Xiahou Yuan (夏侯渊) in Mount Dingjun (《定军山》), Bao Zi'an (鲍自安) in Jiaxing Prefecture (《嘉兴府》) |

**(b) Four great branches** (weng-lunwenji/06 pp. 0272–0274): **tongchui (铜锤 — "bronze hammer," the singing jing), jiazi (架子 — "frame," the acting jing), wuhua (武花 — martial painted face), and shuaida (摔打 — "throw-and-fall" jing)**; there is also an "erhualian" [二花脸] branch, with few characters and no prominent art, now merged into jiazi. The eighteen schools are classified by this scheme, plus a group of **dual specialists**: Mu Fengshan (穆凤山, tongchui + jiazi + wuhua), He Guishan (何桂山) and the New Jin school's Jin Shaoshan (金少山, tongchui + jiazi), Qian Jinfu (钱金福, wuhua plus jiazi), and Fan Baoting (范宝亭, shuaida plus wuhua).

**The jing's "two-door" roles** (p0274): Cao Cao, Meng Liang (孟良), and Dou Erdun (窦尔敦) belong to jiazi but can also be taken by tongchui; Bao Zheng belongs to tongchui, yet in The Double Nail Record (《双钉记》) Bao Zheng is undertaken by jiazi, "allowing more scope in performance"; Pu Tiantian (濮天雕) in Fierce Tiger Village (《恶虎村》), Fei Degong (费德功) in Wax Temple (《蜡庙》), Chi Fushou (赤福寿) in Taking Jinling (《取金陵》), and Huang Longji (黄龙基) in Tyrant's Manor (《霸王庄》) can all be played by either jiazi or wuhua. Weng: "'Two-door'... **allows the performer to combine his own conditions and give broad play to his artistic talent**."

**How the playwright writes from this:** there is no fixed branch for writing a jing, but **the script must lean toward one branch** — "**tongchui stresses song, speech, and acting; jiazi stresses acting, speech, and song**" — that is, tongchui puts song first and jiazi puts acting first (pp. 0284, 0348). Correspondingly, tongchui singing uses "standing tone" and "smooth tone," while jiazi uses "horizontal tone" and "explosive tone" (p0301); the trade saying goes, "**tongchui fears black, jiazi fears white**" (p0284).
**Self-check:** make one list of the jing's arias and one of his acting scenes; whichever is longer indicates the branch. If that does not match the role type marked in the script, the role type is wrong.

**(c) Three styles of the singing painted-face role** (weng-lunwenji/06 pp. 0341, 0361) — the most finely divided table in the jing role:

| Subdivision | Representative figures | Singing style | Weng's explanation | How the playwright writes from this |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Laolian** (老脸 — "old face"; the original sense of "tongchui," "the hammer-bearer") | Xu Yanzhao, Yao Qi, Li Keyong (李克用), Grand Preceptor Wen (闻太师) | **Stresses bearing** | "It must be sung steady and balanced, dignified and solemn, grave and disciplined, grand and generous. Even in arias depicting passion, **there must be no fire of a gale or a crossbow bolt**." | The lyrics should be steady and dignified; **do not write violent invective** |
| **Heitou** (黑头 — "black head") | Yuchi Jingde (尉迟敬德), Bao Zheng, Yang Qilang (杨七郎) | **Stresses power** | The Imperial Orchard (《御果园》) belongs here | Write with weight and a sense of pressure, but that does not mean soaring high all the way through |
| **Dahualian** (大花脸 — "great painted face") | Shan Xiongxin (单雄信), Meng Liang, Huyan Zan (呼延赞) | **Stresses momentum** | Locking Up the Five Dragons (《锁五龙》) belongs here | **Only this route's lyrics may soar high all the way through** |

Origin of the name (p0341): the first play every singing painted-face performer must learn is Entering the Palace a Second Time; Xu Yanzhao paints a purple six-part old-face mask and cradles a bronze hammer, hence the term "hammer-bearer," and "tongchui" became the name for the singing painted-face role. **Failure condition:** write these three categories wrong and the performer sings "not that flavor," while the audience feels uncomfortable without being able to say why.

**(d) Three arias to three different opponents in one act: Locking Up the Five Dragons** (pp. 0343–0345) — after Shan Xiongxin is captured, he denounces three men in succession:

| Passage | Opponent | Intensity | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Li Shimin (李世民) | **Highest** ("A mouthful of rage rises beyond the sky" is lifted with the dragon tone; the last two lines tighten the metrical groove) | Enemies meet, and the eyes redden with particular fury |
| II | Xu Maogong (徐茂公) | **Relaxes** ("comparatively calm," with inserted short lines only at the close) | Shan Xiongxin values martial prowess and scorns letters; "**he utterly despises the clever schemes of scholars and strategists**" |
| III | Luo Cheng | **The climax of the whole play** (the gong and drum cue "Gazing Toward Home" [望家乡, Wàng Jiāxiāng] starts him, and he soars high twice) | The closest relationship, the deepest betrayal |

**How the playwright writes from this:** when one scene denounces three opponents in succession, **the three passages cannot escalate all the way; the middle one must come down according to the character's attitude**, and the reason for the drop must be found in his personality, so that the third passage can rise. **Self-check:** if all three are equally violent, the audience is exhausted by the second passage.

**(e) Binding the shoulders (扎膀子, zhā bǎngzi)** (weng-bianju/03 p0175): "'Binding the shoulders' is a special costume-and-posture convention in the painted-face role: **the shoulders are bound, the chest is thrust out, the buttocks are padded, the whole body is transformed, and every gesture and step follows another door**." → Changing a character to bound shoulders mid-play = changing his appearance, and the script must prepare a bridging scene (see the Jin Shaoshan entry in §3).

### 2.4 The Laodan's Inserted and Overlapping Lines

Source: weng-lunwenji/09 (location of the essay and writing instructions); main text weng-lunwenji/02 p0169, p0172. "These syntactic forms are **a distinctive form of laodan singing**, and at the same time **the hardest metrical discipline to master** in the laodan vocal style." (p0172)

**What inserted lines are:** within the seven- or ten-character skeleton, **insert groups of phrases, allusions, or parallel clauses**, stretching the line longer while the paired-line skeleton stays fixed. Examples:

- Fishing the Golden Turtle: "Ding Lang carved wood, Lai Zi wore colorful clothes, Meng Kang wept for bamboo, Yang Xiang fought the tiger" (丁郎刻木、莱子斑衣、孟康哭竹、杨香打虎; four allusions inserted in parallel); "It is because the son is young and the mother is in mid-life, and what she fears is entering the nine springs a hundred years from now" (皆因是，儿年少，娘在中年，怕的是百年之后身入九泉; three-character pause + three-character pause + four-character pause + long line)
- Beating the Imperial Robe, liushui: "For my sake, Mother was pushed outside the Meridian Gate to be beheaded" (将为娘我推出了那午门以外问斩刑) / "For my sake, Mother was beaten in the cold palace and cold courtyard and could not see the emperor" (将为娘我打至在那寒宫冷院不能够去见君) / "For my sake, Mother was rescued in that broken-tile cold kiln and kept alive" (将为娘我救至在那破瓦寒窑把身存; the same line opening restarted three times); "I nearly beheaded by mistake the sea-spanning golden beam, the one pillar holding up heaven" (险些儿错斩了那架海金梁擎天柱一根)
- Chisang Town: "He is loyal and upright, impartial and selfless, and only thus can be called matchless loyal and good" (他忠心秉正、公而忘私、方算得盖世忠良; four characters + four characters + seven characters)
- **Overlapping lines**: "Thinking of me, Mother's eyes blur **again and again**" (想得为娘一阵一阵眼不明) / "That dragon carriage and phoenix palanquin **step by step by step** come to welcome Mother into the imperial city" (那龙车凤辇一步一步一步迎接为娘进入皇城)

**Where to write them:** **in the laodan's long narrative passages, especially liushui** — do not stay with seven- and ten-character lines only; use inserted lines to fit in allusions, parallel clauses, and pauses of varying length, and use overlapping lines to build emotional accumulation. The laodan's language is by nature "tallying and recounting," with many inserted short lines (垛句, duǒjù; liu/02 p0214).
**Failure condition:** inserted lines test breath points and the metrical groove — "some less capable performers... deliberately stretch them out and sing them apart; others... sing faster and faster and make them frantic" (p0172). **Writing for a performer without the skill is the same as writing the aria badly**; before writing, ask for whom this script is intended.

Three accompanying metrical-pattern maxims (p0170, p0172, p0173), which are also **length norms for writing lyrics**: **sanban must be precise** (it cannot be held up by length alone) / **kuaiban must be steady** (liushui can run to 28 lines, but the line patterns must be regular enough to "hold steady") / **manban must be tight** (lines must bite into one another, not stand as an independent four-line poem).

### 3.1 Three General Rules

1. **To add a role for a lead, first look at the gaps in his repertoire, not only at the subject.** Cheng Yanqiu shelved Spring in the Jar because of "too many tragedies" and asked for a comedy by name; later, lacking the "martial and dance combined, civil and martial equally weighted" route, he wanted A Daughter's Heart; Ye Shenglan, lacking a "full-length poor-sheng play," wanted Tears of the Mandarin Ducks. **However well a script suits the performer's skills, if it conflicts with the genre distribution of his existing repertoire, it still will not get staged.** (weng-bianju/02 p0137, p0144; weng-bianju/05 p0260)
2. **Failure condition: after a play is a great success, the performer "turns his horse loose on the southern hills," and the next script becomes impossible to stage.** His words: "A failed performance naturally kills the appetite; but a successful performance can also produce a side effect... the performer, like one 'feasting before recording merit,' **turns the horse loose on the southern hills and puts the swords and spears into storage**." Examples: Little Ao Mountain was blocked by The General and the Minister Make Peace; Spring in the Jar was blocked by The Jewelry Purse. Another version: submit two scripts at once, and **the lighter one eats the heavier one** (Jin Dali ate The Legend of Zhong Kui). (weng-bianju/05 p0278; weng-bianju/03 pp. 0186–0192)
3. **Distribute arias with an eye to the stamina curve.** Cheng Yanqiu: "In creating a new play's arias and vocal music, one needs **a whole picture in mind**... **select the key passages and arrange them precisely**... at the same time **take one's own energy into account, and arrange and use it properly**." The Jewelry Purse has four key passages, four sets of metrical combinations, none repeated: sheltering from the rain and giving the purse [erliu] [kuaiban] / finding the ball and recognizing the purse [erhuang manban] [yuanban] [sanban] / reliving the past [xipi manban] [yuanban] [liushui] / reunion after the disaster [erliu] plus a "kuitou" cry and a final [liushui]. **Self-check:** draw the length of the four key arias as a curve — can the lead sing them in sequence over one evening? Is the longest passage placed where he still has strength? See also Li Shaochun's rejection of the Qilin Pavilion climax (physical-energy budget). (weng-lunwenji/03 p0187; weng-bianju/06 p0333)

### 3.2 A Warning on Using the Profiles

> **The profile table records the division of labor at the height of a career; Luo Huaizhen warns that the period preserved on film is often the compensatory period.** (luo-v6/03 pp. 0103–0104)

Quotations from Luo Huaizhen: "When I watch their late-life performances, I am doubtful — on what grounds did these four become famous?" — "It turns out that when Xun Huisheng (荀慧生) played the Thirteenth Sister, he wore only a single flower in his hair... but by Xun Huisheng's later years, he dared not point with his hand, dared not open his mouth too wide; the layers and hangings on his body weighed more than ten catties... because he was old, and a man, and had grown stout... he had to cover up and could no longer display his physique." — "In the decline of the Four Great Dan, **they dared not let their voices out, so they polished the vocal music and played with flavor**. But in the early years it was not like this."

**Self-check (Luo's own words, same page):** "The costume and makeup / vocal method you are learning as a paradigm — was it the choice of the artist at his peak or a compensation of his old age?"
**How the playwright writes from this:** when building a profile from recordings, first distinguish which element is **division of labor** (the role type's inherent technical boundary) and which is **compensation** (a practice the artist developed in old age to conceal physical conditions). Writing compensation into a script as a norm imposes on a young performer a set of limits he does not need. The counterpart on Weng Ouhong's side is "two-door... allows the performer to combine his own conditions" (§2.3).

> Material gap: in the "Gaps in the repertoire" column, only Cheng Yanqiu, Ye Shenglan, Jin Shaoshan, Zhou Xinfang, Mei Lanfang, and Li Shaochun have explicit records; the rest are "not recorded in the notes." The dan and jing profiles are far denser than those for the chou role (for chou there are only the physical requirements for Ye Shengzhang's [叶盛章] "Five Poison Plays," weng-lunwenji/04 pp. 0202–0204, which never became a profile).

### 4.1 There Are Only Two Vocal Types

> **There are only two vocal types**: the **large-voice type** (dàsǎng — natural chest voice; represented by the laosheng, **with the painted face, chou, and laodan belonging to it**) and the **small-voice type** (xiǎosǎng — high head voice; represented by the dan, **with the xiaosheng belonging to it**, though there are also xiaosheng who use the laosheng type). Vocal structures within a type are basically the same; the differences lie only in voice production, singing method, register, and ornamentation and phrasing. (liu/02 p0015)

**How the playwright writes from this:** **when writing lyrics for the jing, laodan, or chou, follow laosheng syntax; when writing for the xiaosheng, follow the dan.** This rule compresses a role-type table of a dozen categories into two.

Confirmed role by role (same structure, distinguished only in style): the jing's "vocal structure is broadly the same as the laosheng's, only rougher and more expansive in delivery" (liu/02 p0211), and xipi yuanban "is also structurally identical to the laosheng's" (liu/03 p0343); the laodan "uses the large voice, and the structure is basically the same as the laosheng's," **except that it has many inserted short lines** (liu/02 p0214; liu/03 p0341); the dan "is about the same as the laosheng, with some differences in range and phrasing" (liu/02 p0199; liu/03 p0338: the dan's yuanban "is somewhat slower than the laosheng's"); the xiaosheng's erliu "basically uses the laosheng's line patterns and closing notes, but the phrasing lies in a higher register" (liu/03 p0399).

**Boundary:** the two-type theory governs **syntactic structure** (paired lines, character positions, closing notes), not **character requirements**. Character is still differentiated by role type (liu/02 pp. 0150–0151): laosheng — high, vigorous, seasoned, composed; wusheng — hard, strong, forceful, and **the tune should not twist too much**; xiaosheng — bright and robust, or slow and delicate; qingyi — round, polished, graceful, restrained, and refined; laodan — broad, bright, and soft, with a note of aged vigor and strength; jing — "the amplitude must be large, majestic and sonorous" ("ten sheng are easy to get, one jing is hardest to find"); chou — "mostly humorous... it must be exaggerated and grotesque."
**Self-check:** write syntax by the two types and tone by the ten categories. Swap them and you go wrong — writing a jing's lyrics in dan syntax, or a xiaosheng's lyrics with a laosheng's character, will not stand.

### 5.3 How to Read These Two Tables

**One: check the law of staging alternation (排场交替律, páichǎng jiāotì lǜ).** Read through the "one-line action" column and mark each scene hot or cold — **hot = ensemble scene, fight, broad-voice display, ritual, laugh scene; cold = two-person confrontation, solo scene, complete aria, lyric passage** — and see whether they alternate. Two proxy indicators: ① **song/speech ratio**: more song tends cold (Chaoyang Village scene 8, 73:27 = mother-daughter confrontation in a complete aria), more speech tends hot (scene 5, 8:92 = the crowd laughing). The accompanying criterion: "**ensemble scenes use speech; two-person confrontations use song**," but "if the ensemble scene is ritual (welcome, chorus, grand finale), the opposite holds: use unison singing." ② **"Big scenes" marked in the notes** (City of Blood and Tears scenes 3 and 5) = hot; the single-line advancement scenes wedged between them (scenes 4 and 6) = cold.

**Two: check for "three cold or three hot scenes in a row."** **Three consecutive scenes with the same mark mean the order must be rearranged.** In City of Blood and Tears, scenes 10–13 are four consecutive scenes of plot advancement plus character betrayal (no ensemble, no long arias), the easiest place to run cold; in Chaoyang Village, scenes 4 and 5 are both speech-dominated (69%, 92%), and scene 6 returns to 47:53.

**Three: two connected rules.** ① **Hot/cold balance is also a stamina schedule** — read the main singing role-type column together with §3.1, rule 3, and place the longest passage where the lead still has strength. ② **Action intensity must be modulated within a single scene**: the three units of "Havoc in the Imperial Stables" in Havoc in Heaven — inspecting the stables and testing the horses (strong) → mocking and fighting Tianxi (weak) → overawing the Horse King (extremely strong); "prose dislikes flatness, as a mountain view does, and at the same time this **modulates the performer's exertion and rest**" (weng-bianju/06 p0315). **Self-check:** graph a scene's action intensity as a line; if it is flat, it needs revision.

**Four: a sample's conception does not travel with its technique.** In City of Blood and Tears, scene 11 turns "in a rage for a beautiful woman" into an accomplished fact, and scene 14 has Chen Yuanyuan come out to stop Wu and take her own life; both are **products of the play's conception (the protagonists are two bondservants; Chen and Wu are only background)**, not the default treatment of this subject. The "walking into the countryside" conception of Chaoyang Village is the same (SKILL.md, last paragraph of 12c). When writing the same subject, first cover the "one-line action" column and look only at hot/cold and alternation.

> Material gap: neither set of notes **marks hot/cold scene by scene, nor records song/speech ratios for any play other than Chaoyang Village**; the "main singing role type" column for City of Blood and Tears is not recorded in the original book (the main text says only that Tian Shou is 'skilled in combat, much like Yan Qing in Daming Prefecture,' and that Li Shaochun played two roles in the final scene), so the whole column is left blank rather than filled by inference. Chaoyang Village marks no role types at all; that column records only the functional positions explicitly stated in the notes.

## §6 Modern and Contemporary Plays: Role Types Recede, Functional Positions Remain

### 6.1 Chaoyang Village: No Role Types Marked, Yet Functional Positions Still Follow Role Types

Source: yang/01 character table p0004 and the accompanying observations (= yang suggestion 6); for aria and convention examples, see yang/03.

> **No role types are marked.** Of the eleven named characters, not one is labeled "dan," "sheng," "jing," or "chou" — the character table of a modern play uses **age + identity** in place of role type. Yet traces of role type remain in performance... **A modern play need not mark role types, but a character's functional position is still assigned by role type.**

Note to the table · **How the playwright writes from this:**

1. **The character table may omit role types, but you must have a functional-position table in your head.** Self-check: fill in the third column for your own script — **is the "comic bearer" cell empty? Has the "only carries comic business" cell been wrongly assigned to the protagonist?**
2. **The form of address is the relationship**: the four middle-aged and elderly characters **have no names at all, only relational forms of address**. "Mother-in-law, sit down" works in scene 9 precisely because these two have only the single identity of "mother-in-law" from beginning to end.
3. **What modern plays cut and what they keep** (yang/03, section 4): **cut** self-introduction (自报家门, zìbào jiāmén), entrance poem (定场诗, dìngchǎngshī), and exit poem (下场诗, xiàchǎngshī) — "they were cut, not rewritten"; **keep** the entrance-and-exit conventions (scene 1 has four entrances and exits just to complete a four-line rhymed passage), the aside (背供, bèigòng; marked "(sings aside)" / "(speaks aside)"), and offstage singing (内唱, nèichàng); **add** offstage chorus / accompanying song as a 合头 (hétóu — the sung section at the end of a tune), with the whole company singing in the final scene in place of the exit poem. **Self-check:** does the script contain "(sings aside)" or "(sings offstage)"? If there is not one, the conventions have been thrown away entirely.
4. **Criterion for dividing song and speech**: for the same character and the same content, **prospective or teasing observations are written as spoken dialogue; judgments and demands that the other change are written as song** — Er Daniang's comments on the city students are speech in scene 3, p0026, and made into song in scene 6, p0052.

### 6.2 Yue-Opera Female Xiaosheng: Create Subject Matter from the Performer's Plasticity

Note to the table (the notes' instruction no. 14): **When writing for a Yue-opera female xiaosheng, let him cry, show weakness, and sing children's songs, and give priority to one-performer-two-or-more-roles structures.**
**Failure condition:** **not applicable to male xiaosheng in Peking opera and Kunqu, or to genres whose xiaosheng roles are divided into "pheasant-feather / fan / poor-sheng" routes.**
**Self-check:** does the script give the xiaosheng one complete "show weakness" aria? If not, you have wasted the strongest register of the Yue-opera female xiaosheng. Also ask: does the subject leave room for the same performer to wear two faces? If so, write toward one performer in multiple roles.

### 6.3 Two Parallel Paths, No Verdict

| | Weng Ouhong's path | Luo Huaizhen's path |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | **Write according to an existing role-type route**: first check the lead's home type and repertoire gaps, then arrange scenes according to that route's technical checklist (§2, §3) | **Create subject matter from the performer's plasticity**: the subject grows out of "how many roles this performer can play" (D2) |
| How to write the xiaosheng | Divide into six routes; mark route changes with costume changes; Zhou Ren's "coquetry" had to be argued into "beauty" before it could be kept | No route divisions; **do not avoid softness, self-pity, or childishness** (D3) |
| Mark role types or not | Mark them. "In writing a play, every character who appears is marked with one of the role types sheng, dan, jing, mo, chou" (zhang-guo/04 p0217) | **Never mark them**, though functional positions remain (D5, §6.1) |
| Duets | One complete passage each, passages joined end to end; a duet between the two role types with the greatest difference in vocal color is the strongest musical scene (the Li Duokui entry in §3) | **Mainly single-line alternation**, one seven-character line each, one rhyme to the end (D4) |

**The two directions are opposite and both valid; do not merge them into one rule.** To decide which to use, look at three things: the genre (role-type divisions are hard in Peking opera and Kunqu, soft in Yue opera), the performer (does he have a home type, and how solid is it?), and the source of the subject (subject first, then star, or star first, then subject).

> Material gap: the evidence for the Yue-opera path comes entirely from one volume by one person, Luo Huaizhen (seven plays plus the afterword); D6 is an inference marked as such in the notes, not the original book's words; this section has no comparative material from a second contemporary regional-opera playwright. For the chou's functional position in modern plays, there is only one recorded sentence about the two figures in Chaoyang Village ("Old Child" and Xiao Heidan), not enough to make a table.
