The two digits after the slash give the notebook volume (e.g. liu/02); the number after p is the scanned page in the original book.
**General rules** (stated before the tables and not repeated in them): Peking-opera lyrics are mainly **seven-character lines (2·2·3)** and **ten-character lines (3·3·4)**; the division into sub-phrases depends only on character count, not on banshi (liu/02 p0176–0177). Vocal passages are always paired first and second lines; **an odd number of lines is not permitted**; the only exit is to delete the final line deliberately and fill it with the percussion pattern 【扫头】 (sǎotóu — "sweeping head") (liu/02 p0175–0176; liu/04 p0070). **First lines mostly end on an oblique tone and need not rhyme; second lines end on a level tone and must rhyme** (liu/02 p0016, p0176). The tables below record only each banshi's **exceptions and added constraints** to these rules.

## §1 Peking-Opera Banshi: Character Count and Line Shape

### 1.1 The Erhuang Family

Source: liu/01 (master table) + liu/02 (item-by-item outline).

| Banshi (板式, bǎnshì — metrical pattern) | Character count | Line shape | Beat structure (only the constraints that bear on the wording) | First/second-line handling | Duoju (垛句, duǒjù — inserted short lines) / padding | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 二黄原板 (èrhuáng yuánbǎn) | 7 / 10 characters | Three sub-phrases; the third sub-phrase divides again into two commas | One beat, one eye (一板一眼, yī bǎn yī yǎn — one strong beat and one weak beat); **opens on the beat and closes on the beat** (both lines begin on the beat and their last character lands on the beat); the long bridge after the second sub-phrase of the first line may be omitted when "the line needs to be tight" | Even number; no limit on the number of lines | Duoju lines may be added, mostly in the second line | liu/01, liu/02 p0178–0181 |
| 二黄慢板 (mànbǎn; = 三眼 sānyǎn / 正板 zhèngbǎn) | 7 / 10 characters | Same as yuanban; words sparse, melody long | One beat, three eyes (一板三眼, yī bǎn sān yǎn); = the yuanban "expanded and slowed to twice its length"; still opens and closes on the beat | Even number | Possible, but seldom used where the melody is long | liu/01, liu/02 p0218–0219 |
| 二黄中三眼 (zhōng sānyǎn) / 快三眼 (kuài sānyǎn) | 7 / 10 characters | Same as yuanban | One beat, three eyes, tighter than the manban; internal structure roughly the same as yuanban | Even number | Possible | liu/01 p0218–0219, p0229, p0235 |
| 二黄碰板 (pèngbǎn — "touching the beat") | Not fixed (follows the banshi it touches) | Same as the banshi it touches | **Not an independent banshi, only a way of starting to sing**: no long bridge; **the first character comes out together with the beat** | Governs only the first line | Often begins with a duoju phrase | liu/02 p0248–0249 |
| 二黄顶板 (dǐngbǎn — "topping the beat") | Not fixed | Same as the banshi it tops | As above: **no bridge before singing**; **spoken lines may replace the first and second sub-phrases of the sung line** | Governs only the first line | — | liu/02 p0255–0256 |
| 二黄导板 (dǎobǎn — "leading board") | 7 / 10 characters | A first line in three sub-phrases, broader than an ordinary first line | A sanban (散板, sǎnbǎn — free metre) form with no eyes; once freed into sanban, the cadence notes vary greatly | **Only a first line**; the following huilong or yuanban must supply the second line | — | liu/02 p0256–0257 |
| 二黄回龙 (huílóng — "returning dragon") | 7 / 10 characters + duoju | Opens with duoju lines, enters on the beat, then carries a long melody | Entered on the beat (pengban) | **Supplies the second line missing after a daoban**; a single line | **Duoju lines are standard** | liu/02 p0257 |
| 二黄散板 (sǎnbǎn — "free board") | 7 / 10 characters, may be irregular | Cadence structure same as yuanban | **Sanban has no eyes; slow bowing, slow singing**, free rhythm | Even number | Possible | liu/02 p0263 |
| 二黄摇板 (yáobǎn — "shaking board") | 7 / 10 characters, may be irregular | Same as sanban | **Tight beating, slow singing** (tight bridge, free vocal line) | Even number | Possible | liu/02 p0266 |
| 二黄滚板 (gǔnbǎn) / 哭板 (kūbǎn — "crying board") | **Not fixed, and must not be regular** | **The lyrics cannot be regular lines**; they must create the condition for "a cataloguing, crying-out lament" | A sanban type, one character chasing the next | Even number | Duoju pattern is the norm | liu/01, liu/02 p0267, p0271 |
| 反二黄 (fǎn èrhuáng) | 7 / 10 characters | Same as standard erhuang; **the sub-phrase cadence structure is identical** | Bantou (板头, bǎntóu — the set of opening patterns available in a mode): daoban, huilong, yuanban, manban, fast sanyan, sanban, yaoban | Even number | Yes (in 《碰碑》 (*Peng Bei*) one second line carries a duoju of more than fifty characters) | liu/02 p0272, p0292; liu/01 p0325 |

**Bantou gap**: fan erhuang **has no traditional kuaiban**; the kuaiban was newly developed after 1949 (liu/01 p0325).

## §3 The Thirteen Rhyme Categories and Three Differences from Northern Pronunciation

"辙" (zhé) is the colloquial name for "rhyme". **The Thirteen Rhyme Categories are the thirteen rhyme classes on which Peking-opera lyrics rhyme**, and also the basis for articulating and rounding off sounds in singing (liu/04 p0169).

**Mandatory version note**: Peking opera uses the **old Thirteen Rhyme Categories** — "the thirteen categories referred to here are the **old** thirteen, not today's new thirteen organized wholly according to Standard Mandarin" (liu/04 p0170). They descend in a direct line from the nineteen rhyme classes of 《中原音韵》 (*Zhongyuan Yinyun*, Rhymes of the Central Plains).

### 3.2 Three Differences from Northern Pronunciation (liu/04 p0169–0171)

1. **Characters in the gengqing rhyme do not go to the zhongdong category but to the renchen category.** The book's examples: 恒 read hén (痕), 成 chén (沉), 生 shén (身), 宁 nín (您), 曾 cén (岑), 兵 bīn (宾), 登 dēn. Corpus confirmation: in *Hongyang Cave*, 曾 goes to renchen and is read cén, while 平 goes to renchen and is read pín (liu/04 p0175); in *The Jewelry Purse*, the passage "收余恨" rhymes 定, 明, 性, 命 and 声 with 尽, 襟, 尘 and 因 (suo/01 p0057).
   → **Self-check**: if a rhyme word contains -ing or -eng, first ask whether it should go to renchen. This is the opposite of Mandarin intuition and the easiest place to go wrong.
2. **Entering-tone characters still keep their original sound and rhyme as stage-diction characters (上口字, shàngkǒuzì).** Northern opera and storytelling have already assigned 学 xué, 约 yuē and 却 què to the yexie rhyme, and 北 běi, 贼 zéi and 黑 hēi to the huidui rhyme; **Peking opera does not.**
3. **"Juyu rhyme" characters (珠 zhū, 书 shū, 如 rú) do not go to the gusu category; by the original rhyme they merge into the yiqi category.**
4. **The most easily confused group (measured in a controlled experiment, not from the book)**: **-ei / -ui go to huidui, not yiqi** — 碑 bēi, 悲 bēi, 杯 bēi, 辈 bèi, 泪 lèi, 贵 guì, 内 nèi, 类 lèi, 水 shuǐ, 谁 shéi; yiqi takes only -i / -ü / -er. In the experiment, the last line and key line of a yiqi-rhyme passage landed on 碑, and the whole passage therefore left its rhyme. **Self-check**: check character by character against [reference-shisanzhe.md](reference-shisanzhe.md).

### 3.3 How to Read Entering-Tone Characters as First- and Second-Line Rhymes

**"As a rule, entering-tone characters used as first-line rhymes mostly go to the departing tone; entering-tone characters used as second-line rhymes mostly go to the yangping tone."** (liu/04 p0165, transcribed verbatim; example: in the erniu of 《浣纱记》 (*The Washerwoman's Tale*), the first-line 国 guó goes to the departing tone, and the second-line 合 hé to yangping.)
→ When using entering-tone characters (说 shuō, 合 hé, 学 xué, 北 běi, 贼 zéi, 白 bái, 活 huó, 石 shí, 铁 tiě, 杀 shā, 血 xuè, 发 fā, 月 yuè...) as rhymes, the first and second lines are read differently; use this rule to determine which rhyme category they fall into.

### 3.4 Stage-Diction Characters and Pointed/Rounded Characters: How Far the Playwright Must Manage Them

**Stage-diction characters** (上口字, liu/04 p0168–0169) = "characters far from northern pronunciation, or requiring a special fanqie (反切, traditional two-character spelling)". Four classes: entering-tone characters (说, 合, 学, 北, 贼); juyu-rhyme characters (珠, 书, 如) and geluo-rhyme characters (个 read guò, 何); jiwei-rhyme characters (知, 痴, 非, 飞); ancient and southern pronunciations (脸 read jiǎn, 喊 read xiǎn, 我).
**How far the playwright manages it**: only as far as "determining the rhyme" (see 3.2 item 2 and 3.3), not how to pronounce it. And it is **time-sensitive** — "in Peking-opera **modern plays, stage-diction characters are almost never used**" (same page); when writing modern plays one may use erhua and the small Beijing rhyme categories (liu/03 p0547–0548).

**Pointed and rounded characters** (尖团字, jiāntuánzì) (liu/04 p0166–0168): the strict concept requires **a final containing i or ü, or beginning with i or ü** — pointed = z/c/s + i/ü (精 zing, 心 sin, 须 su), rounded = j/q/x + i/ü (经 jing, 欣 xin, 虚 xu); **zh/ch/sh are not involved** (so 使, 重, 身 and 睡 are not rounded characters; 塞 has initial s but no i/ü in the final, so it is not pointed either, p0175).
**How far the playwright manages it**: homophones with different meanings (相 / 乡, 剑 / 箭) are distinguished by pointed or rounded articulation; when writing lyrics you **need not avoid homophones**; conversely, **two characters that differ in pointed or rounded articulation cannot be heard as a pun**.

**One hard rule about the four tones and character positions** (liu/04 p0158–0159, transcribed verbatim): "Singing yangping high is permitted in Peking opera, but **yinping must not be sung low; if yinping comes out low, the character is sung upside down**... 'Yangping may be sung high; only yinping may not come out low'!" (阳平可高唱，唯阴平不可低出)
→ **This is the only clear criterion for a "fallen character" (倒字, dǎozì).** Self-check: read aloud the four tones of each line's final character and of each melismatic character; for a yinping final character, confirm that the melodic position there is not a low one.

> Material gaps: ① the thirteen categories lack any corpus for "yexie" and "gusu", and "huidui" has only one character; ② the book explicitly declines to give a character-by-character rhyme table, so a minimal usable version has been generated from the 402-syllable table plus this batch of corpus (reference-shisanzhe.md), still without a rhyme book as textual authority; ③ the correspondence between old and new rhyme names (this batch of notes mixes "衣期" and "一七") cannot be documented, so they are listed in parallel here without asserting equivalence.

## §4 Two Classes of Percussion: Entrance/Exit Percussion and the Percussion that Opens or Closes a Banshi

**Three corpus examples of 【扫头】**: ① 《赤壁之战》 (*The Battle of Red Cliff*): Zhou Yu (周瑜) sings only "Heaven will not make things easy for men" and collapses (liu/04 p0070); ② *Farewell My Concubine*: after the erniu of Consort Yu (虞姬) comes the sword dance, which ought to be completed by a sanban second line; in performance it is usually not sung and **【扫头】 supplies it** (liu/03 p0392–0393); ③ *A Hundred Battles to Restore the Tang*: Nan Jiyun's three lines were changed by Li Shaochun (李少春) to one line, "At a time like this I have no leisure for more talk", and 【扫头】 starts at once; Zhang Xun (张巡) and Xu Yuan (许远) add "Take care!" within the 【扫头】 and send him off (weng-bianju/04 p0226).

**Four percussion patterns attached to song and speech** (they fall on the words, so the playwright can mark them directly, liu/04 p0044–0053): **【哭头】 (kūtóu)** = crying within spoken lines and crying inserted into an aria; **【叫头】 (jiàotóu)** = a lament or cry in extremity, with 【双叫头】 and 【三叫头】 variants; **【扑灯蛾】 (pūdēng'é)** = metred speech inserted after 【抽头】, "mostly used when emotion is unusually agitated"; **数板 (shùbǎn)** = a passage of spoken verse accompanied by percussion. Example: in *The Jewelry Purse* scene 8, the flood is entirely given to the 【扑灯蛾】 spoken passage of Hu Jie (胡杰), taking no singing (suo/01 p0034–0035).

**A "shoulder" must be given before singing** (weng-bianju/01 p0087, a correction by Hu Zijun (胡子钧)): Li Yuanba (李元霸) enters to 【急急风】 → strikes a pose to 【四击头】 (sì jī tóu) and starts singing at once; "too rushed, **no shoulder given to the audience**"; it should be changed so that after the pose he clashes the twin hammers against the iron staff, turns and starts 【帽儿头】, strikes a high-low pose, and then sings. Second item on the same page: at a complete emotional turn there is dedicated percussion (【望家乡】 should start at the Meng Jiangni (孟姜女) moment), **one cannot simply cover everything with 【急急风】**.

> Material gaps: in Table One, the four rows "lively young maidservant", "old man", "entering in grief and pain" and "exit with stage business" say only "use a pattern of such-and-such a nature" without giving pattern names; Table Two lacks the opening percussion for uncommon banshi such as 【垛板】 and 【清板】.

## §5 The Thirteen Functional Categories of Scene Qupai

> **Note (read this first)**: Peking-opera "qupai" are **instrumental tunes (plus a small amount of choral "big-character" singing)**; they **share a name with, but are not the same thing as, qupai in the qupai system** (曲牌体, qǔpáitǐ — the tune-pattern system), and **must not be handled according to qupai-system metre** (the boundary drawn in liu/04 p0071). For the few qupai with singing, "**fitting words to the tune**" is the playwright's job: in earlier years performers "paid attention only to the tune of the qupai and ignored the content of its words... one should, according to the specific plot of each play, **re-determine the original words of the chosen qupai, or forge them anew and fit words to the tune**" (weng-lunwenji/07 p0482–0483).

Terminology: **"big characters" (大字, dàzì) = the sung text**; **"mixed tune" (混牌子, hùn páizi) = a qupai with percussion accompaniment; without percussion it is a "plain tune" (清牌子, qīng páizi)** (liu/04 p0071, p0088 footnote). The author points out that the traditional sixfold division (sacred, banquet, dance, military, joyful, mournful music) is unreliable, because changing instruments or tempo can cross categories; but "**even if flexible use on stage is possible, there must be a limit, otherwise... qupai will be used indiscriminately**" (same, p0072).

## §8 Baseline Ratios and the Data Behind the Four Rules of Banshi Layout in *The Jewelry Purse*

### 8.1 Historical Baseline of Ratios in Old Texts

**Scope = the period of the scene-table system (the old "knocked-together scripts" of the 1920s–30s), not a target for today.**

> "Now if we take their scripts and read them again, we see that these scripts could only be realized through performance: a few small pages, or twenty or thirty lines, and a great mass of transition scenes. **If there are twenty scenes, at least fifteen are transition scenes; then there are three or five core aria scenes, with a little confrontation between opponents.**" (Luo Huaizhen, *Collected Works, Volume 6: Theory and Lectures*, luo-v6/04 p0141)

That is, **15 transition scenes : 3–5 core aria scenes**. The accompanying law of correspondence (same, p0140–0141): "**The shape a period's texts take is always correspondent to the performing art of its time**... so we should decode and imagine the performance site of the time from the script."

**A near-at-hand comparison (for calibration)**: in the twelve scenes of the performed *Jewelry Purse*, **four have no singing** (scenes 1, 4, 6 and 8), and **the metrical passages are concentrated in four scenes** (see rule 3) — **the ratio in the 12-scene system and the "20 scenes, 15 transitions" ratio are not on the same order of magnitude; do not swap them** (suo/02 §6).

### 8.2 The Four Rules of Banshi Layout in *The Jewelry Purse*

> **Sample limitation**: the four rules come from **one 12-scene singing play with a comic ending**, and there is no second play for cross-checking. When applying them to another number of scenes, convert by **proportion** — rule 3's "4 metrical scenes" is one third of 12 scenes; for a 9-scene play, 4 metrical scenes is 44 per cent, and copying the absolute number would actually be looser.

**Mandatory version note: all the following figures come from the revised score** — edited by Wang Yinqiu (王吟秋), notated by Gu Yongxiang (顾永湘), Shanghai Literature and Art Publishing House, 1983 — which is the **performance text** of Cheng Yanqiu's 1940 premiere, continuously revised after 1949 and arranged by Wang Yinqiu for performance (the preface says Cheng Yanqiu "until the eve of his death laboured tirelessly at innovation", suo/01 p0003, p0011). Wherever it disagrees with Weng Ouhong's own account, **the text is authoritative** (suo/02, §7 comparison table, 15 items: 11 agree, 3 partly agree, 1 disagrees). **Totals**: the whole play has **65 arias, about 196 lines** (suo/02 §6).

**Rule 1: Mode divides people, not degrees of emotion.** The rich side (scene 2, the Xue mansion) is all erhuang; the poor side (scene 3, the Zhao house) all xipi; **from scene 5, when the two bridal sedans meet, the rest of the play is xipi only**, and erhuang returns once in scene 12, "recognizing the purse". **Failure condition**: it holds only in plays where "two groups of people are long separated on stage". Sources: suo/02 §6 item 1, §9 item 11 (score p0008–0020, p0053–0068).

**Rule 2: 〔二六〕 is used only twice in the whole play, symmetrically placed, both times immediately after 〔流水〕.** Source: suo/02, §6 item 2 (arias #16–#17, #58–#65).

(*original*: "生就一副热心肠，十八岁开始做红娘，搭鹊桥配鸳鸯串村走户心欢畅……人也吃得像蹄膀，呀蹄膀娘三字名气响"); Xiao Ai (小艾, 衣溪 yīxī rhyme) — "Everyone says I, Xiao Ai, am lucky: my husband does design work in the engineering brigade... and I feel that family joys are always joys, and the family gathered together is happiness." (*original*: "都说我小艾好福气，丈夫在工程队里搞设计……也觉得天伦之乐总是乐合家团聚便是喜").

For whom did I suppress twenty years of hate and rage? (*original*: "既然是十全十美人间无九十元收入我也知足八面玲珑虽则好七尺男子重不惑六亲断了可以续五谷不分从头学四肢发达多刚健三班倒总得有人去工作二厢情愿最重要一锤定音请答复").

Had I given in to you then, Wen Hui (雯慧) would have died and you would have been ruined... the reproach of conscience torments you, the whip of morality lashes you, the shadow on the soul covers you, and for a lifetime there is no peaceful you — tell me, should I have stopped you?" (*original*: "原谅老夫骗了你，骗你其实是爱护你，倘若当初依了你，死了雯慧害了你……良心的谴责折磨你，道德的鞭子抽打你，心灵的阴影笼罩你，一辈子没有一个安宁的你，你说要不要阻止你？"); Aunt Wu (吴妈) — "The wedding wine is drunk, the wedding sweets handed out, the wedding money in hand, the wedding done; the guests have all gone, it's getting late; the second young master had better come back — the bride wants to sleep." (*original*: "喜酒吃好了喜糖发过了喜钿到手了喜事办好了，客人走光了辰光勿早了，二少爷好回来了新娘子要困觉了").
- **Variation in line shape** — 《桃园曲》 (*Song of the Peach Garden*), carrying the sedan: Taohua (桃花) — "Has that peach tree any disease, any damage, any injury, any death?"; Liu (柳) — "This treasure — every branch green, every leaf tender, every root thick, every tree sturdy"; "I shouldn't think a hen would break out in boils—"; "What do you mean?"; "The trouble is in the feathers—" (毛里有病, a pun on 毛病, "a defect"); "I don't deserve that"; all — "Lift that sedan, eyes brimming — one foot already across the coffin's side." (*original*: "那桃树可有病可有残可有伤来可有亡？" / "这宝贝枝枝绿叶叶嫩根根粗来棵棵壮" / "想不会母鸡生疮——" / "此话怎讲？" / "毛里有病——" / "不敢当" / "抬起那个轿子泪汪汪一只脚跨在棺材旁").
- **Palindrome** — 《女贞观》 (*The Nüzhen Convent*): "Regret that too much feeling passed a spring; once spring has passed, much feeling turns to regret. Listless, with no heart, I wrong others; others wrong me, and my heart is free of listlessness. True becomes false, false becomes true; kindness breeds resentment, resentment breeds kindness." (*original*: "恨多情过一春，春一过情多恨。闷无心我负人，人负我心无闷。真成假假成真，恩生怨怨生恩。")

**How to read them**: the six groups correspond to the three rules of SKILL.md 6c — the first four groups are "choose the rhyme well" (character sets the rhyme / key line sets the rhyme / risky rhyme / same-character rhyme); the last two are "weave the structure well" (variation in line shape / palindrome). "Lay it out well" has no separate model, because it is a question of allocation across the whole play — it looks at which scene an aria falls in, not at any single passage.

## §10 The Three-Column Checklist for Adapting *The White Snake* (moved from §11 of SKILL.md)

> A template for editing traditional texts. Source `tian-baishezhuan` §2. **Mandatory version note: the 1955 text, not the revised versions circulating online.**

**The White Snake three-column checklist** (template, tian §2): **keep** the qupai, entrance poem, self-introduction, kutou, jiaotou, circular walk (圆场, yuánchǎng) and spoken couplets; **weaken** the protagonist's self-introduction, the four-line exit poem (changed to a two-person couplet) and the whole scene 《煎药》 (*Decocting the Medicine*) — **but move its entire 〔二黄慢板〕 duet into 《释疑》 (*Dispelling Doubt*)**; **add** the wind god escorting Xu Xian (许仙), borrowed from a Guangxi Gui-opera text, and Xu Xian's long 〔反西皮〕 passage at Broken Bridge. **Guard against false inference**: the protagonist not introducing herself is a choice of the adaptation, not a general rule of Peking opera. **Knock-on consequence**: moving an aria to another scene changes the keynote of the new scene as well — **either accept it or rewrite the melody**.

## §11 The Cross-Time Pattern of *Pan Jinlian* (moved from SKILL.md 12b)

> Source `wei-panjinlian`. **Mandatory version note: the notes have missing pages, marked 〔残〕 where they are missing.** Scope = the absurd and cross-time writing of the Sichuan-opera tanxi huqin line (弹戏胡琴, tánxì húqín), not a general rule of banqiang opera (板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ — opera organized by banshi rather than fixed tune patterns).

The pattern of *Pan Jinlian*: there are nine cross-time characters; "they are a **commentary bench**; they can converse and intervene, but they cannot change the plot"; **self-check**: if you delete any one cross-time character, does the story line still run? If not, you have written him as a plot function. Three accompanying rules: cross-time duets rhyme on a single category; outside characters introduce themselves in their own idiom and a local character points it out on the spot; **the most unbearable place is given no words** (only marked "wordless song"; more than three such places is abuse). The author does not claim "absurdist" but "magical realism"; at the time critics attacked from both sides — **both sides are recorded side by side, without adjudication** (pan p0086).

## §12 Sequential Plays (连台本戏, liántái běnxì): Two Contradictory Episode-Division Templates (moved from §13 of SKILL.md)

> Source `weng-bianju-shengya`. **Scope = the commercial production system of Republican-era Shanghai sequential plays**, not a target for episode division today; both are kept side by side, and Weng himself had reservations about both.

- **Shanghai spectacle play in three sections** (彩头戏, cǎitóuxì; weng-bianju p0036, **recorded as a vice**): **opening pepper** (开门辣子, kāimén làzi — a big spectacle at the start to seize the audience) → **clever suspense** (巧卖关子, qiǎo mài guānzi — a long middle stretch of plot, the story **not brought to a close**, and near the end another big spectacle; the suspense must connect to the main through-line of the play) → **knocking in the wedge** (敲入崭子, qiāo rù zhǎnzi — the final martial fight closed inside a stage-machinery spectacle). **Failure conditions and price**: structural authority is handed to the spectacle master, the playwright can only fill in blanks; Weng judged 《火烧红莲寺》 (*The Burning of Red Lotus Temple*) "the blemish of my life".
- **《十二金钱镖》 (*The Twelve Gold Darts*), one four-hour instalment** (p0240, his improvement): each instalment must have a complete story while connecting to the whole sequential play; "it seems also to be the '**phoenix head, pig belly, leopard tail**' pattern" (凤头猪肚豹尾, fèng tóu zhū dù bào wěi — a three-part opening, body and ending shape); after the leopard tail, one hour of mainly martial action **leaving a hook**. Self-assessment: "I could not avoid vulgarity."

## §13 Two Disagreements over *The Jewelry Purse* (moved from §14 of SKILL.md)

> Sources `weng-bianju-shengya`, `weng-xiqu-lunwenji`, `weng-suolinnang-score`, `fan-xiqu-bianju-lunji`. **Mandatory version note: the figures and the status of the definitive text all come from the revised score.**

**Two disagreements kept side by side**: ① **Weng vs. Fan Junhong (范钧宏)** — Fan Junhong criticised the ending passage in which the Xue family grows suspicious as "lower in tone... outside the theme"; **the definitive text keeps it intact, and the preface by He Man (何慢) does not answer this point**. ② **Weng vs. Cheng Yanqiu (the grand close)** — Weng wanted Princess Baihua (百花公主) to cut her own throat and called the reunion a "tumour"; Cheng Yanqiu wanted to keep the reunion and add a political thread; Weng accepted (weng-lunwenji p0189). The other direction: the last scene of *The White Snake* has no singing or speech at all — **a reunion can be given in a single picture**; **failure condition**: in this play the last scene has already given all its singing to Xiaoqing (小青).

## §15 Evidence of Cross-System Practice within a Single Sichuan Opera Script (moved from SKILL.md 0.1)

> Sources `wei-bashan-xiucai`, `wei-panjinlian`. This is evidence for the rule that "marking the vocal system in Sichuan opera is itself the playwright's job", not a general rule of Sichuan opera.

Evidence: Wei Minglun's 《巴山秀才》 (*The Scholar of Bashan*) = high-pitched opera (高腔, gāoqiāng) = qupai system (with bangqiang — 帮腔, bāngqiāng, an offstage answering chorus); *Pan Jinlian* = tanxi huqin = banqiang system (marks only "sing / bangqiang"). In the former, within one play, high-pitched opera, Kunqu and the Peking-opera 〔西皮二六〕 appear together; **vocal system is used as a character label** (wei-bashan p0026).

**How to read it**: the same author, the same genre, two plays years apart, belonging to two vocal systems — so when a Sichuan-opera script arrives, first ask "which vocal system does this play use?", then decide whether to read `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang` or `sw-chinese-opera-qupai`; the qupai-system side of *The Scholar of Bashan* is in `sw-chinese-opera-qupai` and `sw-chinese-opera-qupai-cases`.
