# Chinese Opera Screenwriting · 板腔体 (bǎnqiāngtǐ — banqiang system) Reference: Yuju (豫剧, Henan Opera) and Regional Opera Genres

> Use together with SKILL.md.

This file covers only material that **lands on the words themselves**: the constraints that 板式 (bǎnshì — the metrical pattern of a banqiang aria) impose on aria lyrics, the correspondence between bǎnshì and emotion/position, 韵辙 (yùnzhé — rhyme categories) and the four tones, and aria texts annotated with bǎnshì. It does not cover melody, modes, 过门 (guòmén — instrumental bridge) figure types, accompanying instruments, or 行腔 (xíngqiāng — vocal phrasing) technique. Where the notes are silent, the text says "not recorded in the notes"; where a cell is missing, it is filled with —.

**Source editions and page numbers**: yuju = Fan Shanglin (樊尚林), *A Compendium of Xiangfu-School Yuju Aria, Bǎnshì, and Instrumental Tune Excerpts* (《豫剧祥符调流派唱腔、板式暨器乐曲牌集萃》); page numbers are **scanned pages** pNNNN (for the bǎnshì section, printed page = scanned page − 25). yang = Yang Lanchun (杨兰春), performance script of *Chaoyanggou* (《朝阳沟》, "Chaoyang Valley") by the Henan Provincial Yuju Troupe No. 1, 1978. luo-v2 / luo-v3 / luo-v6 = Luo Huaizhen (罗怀臻), *Collected Plays* (《戏剧文集》), volumes 2, 3, and 6 (printed page = scanned page − 5). pan / wei-bashan = Wei Minglun (魏明伦), *Pan Jinlian* (《潘金莲》) and *The Scholar of Bashan* (《巴山秀才》). When the source column gives only a page number, the note's table-of-contents name appears on the line before the table.

### 1.1 The Four Major Classes and Their Row Counts

**The inventory is inconsistent in two places; they are transcribed as found, without adjustment**: (1) the list of 14 items in the èrbā bǎn class **does not include "èrbā bǎn" itself** (p0207 is the class introduction); mànbǎn, liúshuǐbǎn, and fēibǎn each occupy one cell. (2) In the fēibǎn class, the single "zāibǎn" cell contains two entries, dà zāibǎn and xiǎo zāibǎn.

### 1.3 Three Ways to Read the Table (Supported by the Source Book, Not Inferences)

1. **There are only three hángdang restrictions**: wǔyīn (dàn only, p0197), guǎitóudīng (jìng / wài bājiǎo, p0201), and tījiǎokào (wài bājiǎo, p0215). The rest "apply not only to dàn roles but can equally be adopted by shēng, jìng, chǒu, and mò" (yuju/01 p0015) (shēng 生, jìng 净, chǒu 丑, mò 末 — the male and comic role types).
2. **The amount of guòmén = the distance between lines, but the length of the guòmén does not change the character count**: eight bāng (mànbǎn) → four bāng (liánhuánkòu) → none (jīngōuguà, èrbā liánbǎn, duǒzibǎn, liúshuǐ liánbǎn). Examples: liánhuánkòu still uses ten-character lines (yuju/02 p0195); yíngfēngbǎn and mànbǎn have exactly the same line pattern (p0191).
3. **Only one hard rule governs line count**: fēibǎn generally turns to another metre after four, six, or eight lines (p0233); everything else is unrestricted.

### 1.5 Fan's Rules vs. the Actual Score of Chaoyanggou—Side by Side, Neither Merged Nor Adjudicated

The main text of *Chaoyanggou* does not mark bǎnshì anywhere; the scores in the "Selected Aria Passages" appendix are the only place in the whole play where bǎnshì are marked (yang/02). **The two bodies of material differ in opera branch, period, and genre** (Fan's book = a collection of traditional Xiangfu-mode examples; *Chaoyanggou* = a modern play written in 1958 and restaged in 1977). Where they disagree, both are transcribed; no judgment is made about which is right.

### 2.2 Functional Positions of Bǎnshì Names (Xiangfu Yuju ↔ Peking Opera)

**What is mapped is functional position (bǎn–yǎn + tempo + emotion + place within a full aria suite), not musical descent**—Yuju belongs to the bāngziqiang (梆子腔 — clapper-opera) family, Peking opera to the píhuáng (皮黄 — the xīpí and èrhuáng vocal systems) family. **Every Peking-opera entry is marked "to be verified"**: the yuju notes state that this table awaits checking against Liu Jidian's (刘吉典) *Introduction to Peking Opera Music*. **All entries come from the final section of yuju/02; for the bǎn–yǎn and line constraints of each Yuju bǎnshì, see §1.2 and do not repeat them here.**

**Correspondences (Yuju → Peking opera; confidence in parentheses)**: mànbǎn → mànbǎn / xīpí (西皮) or èrhuáng (二黄) mànbǎn (high: name, bǎn–yǎn, and function all identical) | èrbā bǎn → yuánbǎn (原板) (high: both are the medium-tempo narrative backbone with no line limit) | duǒzibǎn → duǒbǎn (垛板) / fast yuánbǎn (high) | liúshuǐbǎn → liúshuǐ (medium: Peking-opera liúshuǐ is faster and exists only in xīpí, while Yuju divides it into fast, medium, and slow and counts it among the four major classes) | jǐn èrbā → kuàibǎn (快板) (medium-high) | jǐn dǎ màn chàng → yáobǎn (摇板) / jǐn lā màn chàng (high) | fēibǎn → sǎnbǎn (high; Yuju mostly "turns after four, six, or eight lines") | gǔnbái → fǎn èrhuáng sǎnbǎn (反二黄散板) / kūtóu (哭头) (low, to be verified) | dà zāibǎn · xiǎo zāibǎn → dǎobǎn (导板) (medium-high: both are "one free-metre line leading into the main-metre lower line") | dàqǐbǎn → huílóng (回龙) / [dǎobǎn] continuation (medium: Yuju specifies that "the first upper-rhyme line is divided into four sub-phrases") | jiàobǎn → jiàotóu (叫头) / jiàobǎn (high).

**Four categories with no Peking-opera counterpart (unique to Yuju)**: (1) jīngōuguà / liánhuánkòu / èrbā liánbǎn / liúshuǐ liánbǎn—Peking opera usually treats these as "joined directly" or "without guòmén" and **has no separate bǎnshì names**; **Yuju turns "the presence or absence of guòmén" into an independent bǎnshì, the biggest systematic difference from the Peking-opera table**. (2) tóujùqiāng / liǎngjùqiāng / sānjùqiāng / the six guòbǎn types—**Peking opera has no corresponding bǎnshì level** (see §3). (3) guādāzuǐ—five-character lines occur sporadically in Peking opera, **but there is no dedicated bǎnshì**. (4) èrbā liántán / gǒusīyǎo / liúshuǐ liúbǎn—Peking-opera duets mostly use yuánbǎn, kuàibǎn, or liúshuǐ, **with no special names** (see §5).

> **Gaps in the material:** the entire Peking-opera column is "to be verified"; the yuju notes have not yet been checked item by item against Liu Jidian's Peking-opera table. Until then, **this table can only locate corresponding positions; it cannot be used to replace bǎnshì names**.

## §3 The Nine Types of Line-Head Phrase

In the Yuju mànbǎn class, 9 bǎnshì deal specifically with **the first three characters** of the upper or lower rhyme (the "sānzìtóu", 三字头 — three-character head): **the first three characters of an upper or lower line are an independent unit that must be written substantively**; if they cannot stand as a unit, these 9 phrase types have nothing to hang on. **The source for this entire table is yuju/01.**

| # | Name | Character count | Where used | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 头句腔 (tóujùqiāng) | First three characters of the upper rhyme | Opening phrase of the first upper-rhyme line in mànbǎn (start of a section) | p0203 |
| 2 | 两句腔 (liǎngjùqiāng) | First three characters of the upper rhyme | Start of a section; the phrase on the first three characters divides into two sub-phrases | p0203 |
| 3 | 三句腔 (sānjùqiāng) | First three characters of the upper rhyme | Start of a section; the phrase on the first three characters divides into three sub-phrases | p0204 |
| 4 | 双过板 (shuāng guòbǎn) | First three characters of the lower rhyme | Where the lower line begins; four bāng are struck (even number) | p0204 |
| 5 | 单过板 (dān guòbǎn) | First three characters of the lower rhyme | Where the lower line begins; three bāng are struck (odd number) | p0204 |
| 6 | 简过板 (jiǎn guòbǎn) | First three characters of the lower rhyme | Where the lower line begins; only one bāng is struck, the simplest form | p0205 |
| 7 | 花腔过板 (huāqiāng guòbǎn) | First three characters of the lower rhyme + all that follows fully padded | Where the lower line begins; **most often on the lower rhyme of the second line**; paired with cheerfulness (dàn) | p0205 |
| 8 | 大过板 (dà guòbǎn) | First three characters of the lower rhyme | Where the lower line begins; five bāng are struck, longer than any other bridge | p0205 |
| 9 | 高音过板 (gāoyīn guòbǎn) | First three characters of the lower rhyme | Where the lower line begins; the three-character phrase is already in the high register; paired with grief, indignation, and generosity | p0206 |

**Division of labor**: 1–3 handle the first three characters of the **upper rhyme** (three grades of opening phrase at the start of a section); 4–9 handle the first three characters of the **lower rhyme** (six grades of guòbǎn, differing in how many bāng are struck and in the vocal register). **Linkage**: guǎitóudīng specifies that "**the third character of tóujùqiāng** is sung an octave above èrběnqiāng" (p0201).

> **Gaps in the material:** Fan writes "how many bāng are struck" as a guòmén rule; **the differences among shuāng guòbǎn / dān guòbǎn / jiǎn guòbǎn / dà guòbǎn lie in the guòmén, not in the words** (yuju/01 §8, item 2, lists them as "no constraint whatsoever on the aria text"). The only general rule available to the librettist is: both upper and lower lines need a three-character head that can form a phrase on its own; which grade of guòbǎn is chosen is not determined by the words.

> Rhyme categories left blank: this book supports only reliance on 中州音韵 (Zhōngzhōu yīnyùn — Zhongzhou pronunciation); there is no division table.

**The point of this section is precisely the blank.** Fan's book contains **no rhyme-category division table at all**; the eighteen-rhyme / 十三辙 (shísān zhé — thirteen rhyme categories) tables are not in this book (for the exact wording and page, see §4.1; for the gap, see yuju/01 §8, item 3). - **This skill does not support rhyme-category lookup for Yuju**; rhyme words must be checked against separate Zhongzhou-rhyme material or learned by ear in the dialect. The librettist has only two rules: **judge rhyme compatibility by Zhongzhou pronunciation (the Bianliang–Heluo dialect)**, and **the lower line must rhyme**.

### 4.2 A Measured Test of Rhyme in One Script of Chaoyanggou (yang/03 §3)

**This is the actual state of one modern-play script (written 1958 / restaged 1977), not a rhyme standard.**

- **The lower line must rhyme; the upper line is free** is the only strictly enforced rule; the upper line rhymes about fifty percent of the time, and the more parallel the couplet, the more likely it rhymes.
- **Dialect-based rhyme assignment is allowed**: in Mandarin, 飞 (fēi) belongs to the 灰堆 (huīduī) rhyme category, **but in Yuju singing and speech it is assigned to 一七 (yīqī)**; this is dialect rhyme assignment, not a rhyme error.
- **Changing rhyme within a section is legitimate**; the trigger is a turn in emotion (in the passage of Yin Huan (银环), "If anything happens to my mother", the first two lines are 一七 (yīqī) and the last eight 怀来 (huáilái)).
- **Adjacent passages often join on the same rhyme**, creating tonal unity within a scene (scene 2, 9→10 言前 (yánqián); scene 4, 22→24 中东 (zhōngdōng); scene 9, 47→48 梭波 (suōbō)).
- **Broad rhyme categories are used heavily**: nine categories—中东 (zhōngdōng), 言前 (yánqián), 人辰 (rénchén), 梭波 (suōbō), 发花 (fāhuā), 一七 (yīqī), 由求 (yóuqiú), 怀来 (huáilái), and 江阳 (jiāngyáng)—cover all 50 passages of the play, while **the risky categories (乜斜 miēxié, 遥条 yáotiáo, 灰堆 huīduī, 姑苏 gūsū) are not used once**; colloquial scripts default to broad rhymes.
- **In a duet, a question line may go unrhymed, with the other singer's lower line picking up the rhyme** (scene 9, "what's laid underneath, what's on top" breaks the rhyme, and the next singer brings it back); **this is not allowed in a solo**.
- The longest passage uses the strictest rhyme: Yin Huan's long passage in scene 7 runs **30 lines in the 由求 (yóuqiú) rhyme from beginning to end, without a single departure**.

Another Yuju sample (luo-v2/04, *Journey to Fengshan* [《凤山行》]): **yánqián is the most frequent, jiāngyáng second**; but the note declares that the rhyme names are **summarized by Mandarin pronunciation, not dialect rhyme categories**—"before using the lyrics in this volume as rhyme examples, one must first go through the dialect rhyme table of that opera genre".

> **Gaps in the material:** a Yuju rhyme-category division table is absent from all existing notes. The two sets of rhyme names in §4.2 come from two different methods (yang judges by Zhongzhou rhyme categories and adds "?" where confidence is low; luo-v2 summarizes by Mandarin), and **they cannot be substituted for each other or merged into one table**. The requirements of the four tones and level-oblique patterns on Yuju aria text: not recorded in the notes.

| Bǎnshì name | Rule of line-length decrease | Scenes used in | How to write the close | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **二八连弹 (èrbā liántán; also 二八乱弹 èrbā luàntán)** | **No decrease** (for the three hard requirements, see §1.2). In the aria text, **the upper and lower lines are split among different characters**, each taking one line or half a line and alternating to the end | Reunion and victory scenes, several role types onstage | Close with **the same line pattern repeated by a different singer** ("he urges me… / she urges me…") | yuju/01 p0212; example yuju/02 p0212–13, three-person duet in *Returning in Triumph After Defeating the Enemy* [《克敌荣归》] |

### 5.1 Aria-Text Example of Gǒusīyǎo (the Decrease Is Structural, Not Rhetorical)

yuju/02 p0214–p0215, Bao Zheng and the Imperial Aunt in *Qin Xianglian* [jìng vs. dàn]:

```
（皇姑）吓住胆大小包拯，你把皇姑看得轻。          两句对唱（7＋7）
(Imperial Aunt) You would frighten this bold little Bao Zheng—you take the Imperial Aunt lightly.  two lines of duet (7 + 7)
（皇姑）你向秦氏是何理？（包拯）状状告的驸马宫。  一句对一句
(Imperial Aunt) What justice is this toward Lady Qin? (Bao Zheng) The complaint names the Prince Consort's palace.  one line against one line
```
The remaining rounds are in note yuju/02 p0214–p0215.

**When writing a courtroom face-off, design the aria text so that the line count decreases from long to short.**

**Principle for distributing it across a whole play**: decreasing all the way to half a line is a one-time resource in the play; save it for the biggest confrontation, and in earlier scenes take the decrease only as far as one line.

### 5.2 Cross-Check Against the Mother–Daughter Quarrel in Scene 8 of Chaoyanggou

yang/02 §3; yang/03 example 10. Scene 8 is **the only two-character scene in the whole play**; its song-to-speech ratio is **73:27 (the highest in the play)**, and the mother–daughter duet runs about **40 lines in the suōbō rhyme from beginning to end**. The actual decrease: **four lines each → two lines → one line or even half a line**.

```
银环：依我说——      银环妈：你说什么？
Yin Huan: If you ask me—            Mother: What did you say?
银环：好妈妈！        银环妈：你气死我。
Yin Huan: Dear mother!              Mother: You'll be the death of me.
银环：说走我就马上走！  银环妈：你走你哥养活我，／离了你丫头我也能活。
Yin Huan: Say the word and I'll go at once!  Mother: Go—your brother will support me, / and without you, girl, I can still live.
```
The remaining rounds are in note yang/03 example 10 (script p0075–0076).

**Two points of cross-confirmation and one divergence**:

1. **The rule of decrease is the same**—Fan's gǒusīyǎo ("two lines → one line → half a line") and *Chaoyanggou* ("four lines → two lines → one line → half a line") follow the same rule; *Chaoyanggou* adds an initial "four lines" grade.
2. **The closing method differs; both are retained.** Fan speaks only of a metrical close (developing into beat-start, beat-end) and **does not say how the words close**; *Chaoyanggou* uses an asymmetrical close in which **one side adds one more line to settle the passage**—yang/03 instruction 12: "after the decrease reaches half a line, one side must add one more line to settle the passage, otherwise it hangs in midair."
3. **Divergence: this passage has no bǎnshì annotation.** Not one of the four aria passages in scene 8 is included in the score collection, so **one cannot say it "is gǒusīyǎo"**—yang/03 instruction 16: "do not fill in a bǎnshì for a passage whose score does not mark one." This file merely sets the line-length shapes side by side.

> **Gaps in the material:** (1) **Liúshuǐ liúbǎn has no aria-text example** (yuju/02 does not transcribe the words under that score); what its "final line is not sent off" looks like on the level of words is not recorded in the notes. (2) Scene 8 of *Chaoyanggou* has no bǎnshì annotation, so its correspondence with gǒusīyǎo extends only to "decreasing line length".

## §6 Three-Column Comparison: Bāngqiāng—Hétóu—Offstage Singing

Chuanju (川剧) 高腔 (gāoqiāng — high-pitched vocal system) **帮腔 (bāngqiāng — supporting chorus)**, Yuju modern-play **合头 (hétóu — chorus tag; offstage chorus / accompanying chorus)**, and Yueju (越剧) **幕内唱 (mùnèichàng — offstage singing)** are three forms occupying the same functional position: **a layer of voice belonging to no character**, responsible for exposition, translating inner life, and commentary.

| Opera genre | Textual form (where it is written in the script, and in what shape) | Functions it performs | Can it be transplanted? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **川剧高腔·帮腔 (Chuanju gāoqiāng · bāngqiāng)** | **The word "bāngqiāng" is written at the head of the line, occupying the same position as a character name**—it is treated as a character in the script. Extremely short: in *The Scholar of Bashan*, 6 of 9 instances are only 3–7 characters; in *Li Yaxian* [《李亚仙》], about 20 instances. **It can change qǔpái**. **It can be embedded inside a sung line and interrupt a line of song** | Four functions: (1) **narration** (stating facts the character cannot conveniently or cannot say); (2) **commentary** (judging, exposing, settling accounts from outside the character); (3) **speaking for / participating** (speaking on the character's behalf, even making decisions for him); (4) **emotional amplification** (expanding one person into a crowd). Before transplanting it, first ask the orchestra whether it has a bāngqiāng chorus | wei-bashan/02 §2 (p0013 / p0025 / p0026 / p0030 / p0038 / p0048 / p0065); luo-v2/04 1.5; on non-transplantability, see **pan/02 instruction 5** |

### 6.1 One Point Shared by All Three Columns: the Chorus as a Whole Replaces the Opening and Exit Poems

luo-v2/04 commonality 2: all seven plays after 1992 are equipped with a chorus layer (歌队, gēduì)—Huaiju (淮剧) offstage solo / accompanying chorus, Yongju (甬剧) offstage singing, Huangmei opera (黄梅戏) chorus, Chuanju bāngqiāng + 莲花落 (liánhuālào — lotus-fall song genre), Qinqiang (秦腔) offstage chorus. **In the eight plays the exit poem (下场诗, xiàchǎngshī) does not appear even once**, and the opening poem (定场诗, dìngchǎngshī) appears only once each in *The Death of Han Xin* (《韩信之死》) and *Li Yaxian*; the only play without a chorus, *The Death of Han Xin* (1981), is also the only one that keeps the opening poem and the "(recite)" marker. **The appearance of the chorus and the disappearance of the opening poem are two sides of one thing.**
**Conditions under which this fails** (instruction 4): replacement is not cancellation—if you delete the opening poem and also provide no chorus, no one carries the expository function. **Self-check**: delete the chorus lines and reread; if no one accounts for the time, place, and commentary between scenes, the chorus is carrying a structural function and cannot be deleted as decoration.

### 6.2 Three Formal Rules of Bāngqiāng (Unique to This Genre; Source: wei-bashan/02 §2)

1. **Extremely short; it never carries extended lyrical expression**—extended lyricism is always left to the characters.
2. **It is always placed where "the character cannot keep singing"**: the sentence is cut off, the upper line hangs waiting for its lower, the character loses consciousness or judgment.
3. **Bāngqiāng can be heard by the characters**: in scene 3 the bāngqiāng makes a decision for husband and wife ("Take your wife to Chengdu!"), and Meng Dengke (孟登科) answers in speech, "Hey, that bāngqiāng helped well!"—**the bāngqiāng turns from "the voice of the crowd" into "the voice of fate" and brings a comic beat** (p0026, the only case where bāngqiāng is written as **a present participant**).

**The boundary with accompanying chorus / chorus**: **layout differs**—bāngqiāng is listed alongside character names, while accompanying chorus is marked "offstage" or "behind the curtain"; **function differs**—accompanying chorus / chorus handles announcements and theme, while bāngqiāng clings to the aria passage and performs the four functions above.

> **Gaps in the material:** (1) All evidence in the Yuju "hétóu" column comes from **a single script of *Chaoyanggou*** and is characterized as "an invention of the modern play"; **whether traditional Yuju plays have hétóu is not recorded in the notes**. (2) The evidence in the Chuanju bāngqiāng column (two plays by Wei Minglun + one by Luo Huaizhen) **all comes from newly written plays**; the notes do not record how bāngqiāng is written in traditional gāoqiāng scripts.

### 7.2 Huaiju

> Induced from scripts, not from a theoretical treatise.

Source: luo-v2/04 1.1. Sample: *The Death of Han Xin* (1981, p0006–0052), *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* (《金龙与蜉蝣》, 1992, p0054–0085), *The Hegemon of Western Chu* (《西楚霸王》, p0086–0118). **Huaiju is the only genre in this volume with three plays, spanning 1981, 1992, and 1994, so its reliability is higher than that of the other five; but *The Hegemon of Western Chu* was originally a Peking-opera script and later adapted for Huaiju (afterword 5), so its value as a Huaiju sample must be discounted.**

1. **The foundation is neat seven- and ten-character upper/lower couplets, in pairs** (*The Death of Han Xin* is like this almost throughout)—four lines to a group, with the second and fourth rhyming (p0007).
2. **The basic unit of a duet is "one seven-character line per person"**, single lines alternating back and forth, capable of running a dozen or more exchanges (p0014). **This is the default format for writing confrontation in Huaiju.**
3. **Eleven years later (1992), the variance in line length was completely opened up**: within one passage the character count rises from 3 to 14, with three-character duǒ, four-character chains, and five-character nursery-rhyme form (p0063, p0076). **The upper/lower line pair and the rhyme words did not move; only the waist of the line moved—this is the entire substance of "urban new Huaiju" as it lands on the aria text.**
4. **The main rhymes are jiāngyáng and rénchén**, with one rhyme running through a section without change (p0042, p0076 jiāngyáng; p0014 rénchén mixed with yóuqiú).
5. **Spoken dialogue is extremely colloquial; aria text leans literary**: the speech carries self-corrections and stumbles ("take Zhongli Mei (钟离昧)—no, take this brave man into the back hall", p0012), while the aria text does not.
6. **Duǒ lines stack lists of events, not adjectives**: "Cross the Wu River, resist the Qin army, / enter Xianyang, destroy Ying Zheng (赢政)" (p0112), p0081.
7. **Offstage solo / accompanying chorus is a fixed arrangement**, and its line pattern is not bound by upper/lower couplets (the theme song is a six-line folk-style irregular verse, p0056). The 1981 play is the only one in the volume without a chorus, and the only one that keeps the opening poem and the "(recite)" marker.

### 7.3 Yongju

> Induced from scripts, not from a theoretical treatise.

Source: luo-v2/04 1.2. Sample: *The Wife for Hire* (《典妻》, p0120–0150, premiered by the Ningbo Municipal Yongju Troupe), **one play per genre**.

1. **The most colloquial genre in this volume.** A whole aria can go without a single literary allusion or four-six parallel line ("when cold, I ask only for a garment, / when hungry, I ask only for grain", p0129). Its nouns are all rice, firewood, oil, water, umbrellas, sedan chairs, silver dollars.
2. **The only classical Chinese is used to mark status**: after the xiùcai quotes *Classic of Poetry · Shuo Ren* (《诗经·硕人》), his wife answers in speech, "I don't understand a single line" (p0132). **Classical Chinese is a property of the character, not the register of the whole play.**
3. **Seven-character upper/lower lines predominate, ten-character lines second**; transition songs ([Mùnèichàng]) are always **one poem of four seven-character lines**, six in the whole play (6 instances across p0124–p0146).
4. **The main rhetoric is parallelism and refrain**: "for my son, so he won't go hungry / have clothes / be cured" (p0125); "I am wronged, so wronged, / I regret, so much regret…" (p0148); eight groups of reduplicated characters in a row (p0147).
5. **Rhyme**: jiāngyáng overwhelmingly dominates (the longest passage, p0147–0148, runs jiāngyáng to the end), followed by rénchén and yóuqiú (p0144).
6. **Yongju allows "speech inserted into song"**: a passage stops at the third line, and the other character's one line of speech asks out the final line (p0138). **This is a song–speech interlock peculiar to life-like genres**; it does not occur even once in *The Death of Han Xin* or *Yang Guifei* (《杨贵妃》).

### 7.4 Huangmei Opera

> Induced from scripts, not from a theoretical treatise.

Source: luo-v2/04 1.3. Sample: *Southeast Flies the Peacock* (《孔雀东南飞》, p0152–0186, written with Wang Chang'an [王长安]), **one play per genre**.

1. **Five- and seven-character lines carry equal weight; three-character lines are used for fast cuts**: "this height / this girth / this step / this raised hand", one three-character phrase passed from person to person (p0161–0162).
2. **Reduplication, er-suffixation, and modal particles are frequent; the aria text is light** ("this lute sound, now bright now fading, / this lute sound, drifting and floating…", p0154–0155).
3. **The chorus sings original lines from the Han yuefu poem *Southeast Flies the Peacock***, six times (p0154, p0164, p0166, p0172, p0180, p0187). **The original poem belongs to the chorus, the new lyrics to the characters; the two registers are never mixed.**
4. **Speech in the fùzibǎn (赋子板 — narrative metre) style**: Lan Zhi's (兰芝) two sets of one-to-ten ("first, I am not greedy for food… tenth, I am not harsh to my husband", p0177–0178) are **not marked (sings), but their rhythm is the rhythm of song**. Numbered parallelism also appears in Yuju and Yongju; **it is a format shared across banqiang genres, not a Huangmei-opera patent**.
5. **Rhyme**: Lan Zhi's long "why" passage rhymes in yánqián / rénchén (p0173), broad rhymes.
6. **Three-person round singing creates comic rhythm**: in scene 3 the three each sit up, sing four lines, and lie down again, for three rounds (p0169–0170)—**same line pattern, same rhyme, same length; the laughter comes from repeated physical action**.

### 7.5 Yuju (the Luo Script Side)

> Induced from scripts, not from a theoretical treatise.

Source: luo-v2/04 1.4. Sample: *Journey to Fengshan* (p0188–0225, written with Yao Jincheng [姚金成]), **one play per genre**. This section differs in kind from §1 (Fan Shanglin) and §4.2 (Yang Lanchun) and is **not merged with them**.

1. **Ten-character lines overwhelmingly dominate, with a "3-3-4" rhythm** ("I cannot bear to see the fields cracked and the drought year after year", p0190–0191). **This is the genre with the longest lines and the widest breath points (qìkǒu) in the volume**—consistent with §1, where Fan's book makes "ten-character lines (3+3+4)" the skeleton.
2. **One person can carry a passage of thirty or forty lines without a change of singer** (about 40 lines, p0213–0214); **full-length solo suites are the norm**.
3. **Duǒ lines are grouped under "ask" and "do not ask"**: four asks (ask the great house / the gold plaque / your excellency / the magistrate) + six do-not-asks (do not ask about disaster / hardship / mulberry and hemp / fields / the common people / the locality) (p0213–0214); in one passage Lady Cao (曹夫人) has six "fear that he" + four "do not seek" (p0195–0196). **In the latter half of the duǒ the character count decreases, with three- and four-character lines coming in succession; this is the only means of acceleration**—consistent with §1, where duǒzibǎn says "the lines must grow shorter".
4. **Colloquial speech and Mandarin classical are stratified**: officials speak in a classical register ("to hear the Way in the morning is enough to die content at night", p0190), while mountain folk speak plainly (p0210). **Two registers are distributed by social class within one play.**
5. **Rhyme**: yánqián is the most frequent (nan / guan / shan / duan / tian / chan / chuan), jiāngyáng second. **Summarized by Mandarin, not by Zhongzhou rhyme categories.**
6. **Duets fall into two grades**: the passage between Hai Mei (海妹) and Rang Hao (让号) (p0209) is a **folk-song-style duet** of four seven-character lines per round, in sharp contrast with the old shēng's long passage—**small shēng–dàn scenes use short folk-style lines; large official scenes use ten-character suites**.

### 7.6 Chuanju

> Induced from scripts, not from a theoretical treatise.

Source: luo-v2/04 1.5. Sample: *Li Yaxian* (p0226–0255, premiered by the Chongqing Municipal Chuanju Troupe), **one play per genre**. See also §7.8 and §6.

1. **The only script in the volume with [Bāngqiāng]** (a gāoqiāng feature). Its bāngqiāng lines are extremely short (from one five-character line to two seven-character lines), about 20 instances, with three functions: **voicing inner thought** (p0228, p0231), **naming the action** (p0229), and **prompting a change of breath in the song** ("change, change, change—", p0251–0252).
2. **A duet can close down to one character per line**: "I want wealth / I want him / wealth / him / wealth! / him!" (p0236–0237), **a four-stage decrease from five characters → three → two → one**, the most intense closing method in the volume.
3. **Er-suffixed padding syllables pile up** (in liánhuālào style, "call out to grandpa, grandma, and sister…", p0240). **The padding syllables carry identity (beggar) and rhythm, not rhyme.**
4. **The liánhuālào chorus ("Eight Immortals") sings four five-character lines + one shouted line "ha ha liánhuālào ye"**, once at the head and once at the tail of each scene, also serving as announcement, commentary, and time caption.
5. **Rhyme**: mostly rénchén, zhōngdōng, yóuqiú; **bāngqiāng lines often share the rhyme of the preceding sung line** ("the silk whip falls in the dust" answered by "it makes me dizzy on horseback", both rénchén).

**One caution** (same note): among the "most miscellaneous" elements, **liánhuālào belongs to quyi (曲艺 — Chinese popular performance arts), not to operatic vocal systems**; with liánhuālào removed, Chuanju's line patterns come close to Huaiju's.

### 7.7 Qinqiang

> Induced from scripts, not from a theoretical treatise.

Source: luo-v2/04 1.6. Sample: *Yang Guifei* (p0256–0284), **one play per genre**. **The play grew out of the author's musical play *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* (《长恨歌》) and was further adapted into a Qinqiang script from a Huangmei-opera version (afterword 9); its line patterns bear traces of the source genres, so its value as a Qinqiang sample must be discounted.**

1. **Four- and five-character lines are the most numerous, the shortest in the volume**: "The royal hunting ground, / the sun of a golden age. / In hunting dress, / summon the favored general, / draw the bow to the full moon and shoot the river deer" (p0258).
2. **Emotion is piled up by repeating the same lead line**: "because I have you…" four times (p0272); "watch the morning star / drink wine / play the jade lute / bathe in the hot spring / comb the cloud-like hair / ask after the illness in detail" seven times (p0278); "boundless hate, endless regret" four times (p0277). **The lead line stays fixed while the final character changes rhyme—this is the main texture of Qinqiang in this play.**
3. **Short one-line-against-one-line duets, linked by anadiplosis**: the last two characters of the previous line become the first two of the next (p0283–0284), and the final section closes down to three characters (always together / forever following / entwined together / infinitely beautiful / flying wing to wing).
4. **Nine passages of Bai Juyi's (白居易) original *Song of Everlasting Sorrow* (《长恨歌》) are used as offstage chorus** (9 instances across p0257–p0284), the same method as Huangmei opera's use of Han yuefu.
5. **Rhyme**: jiāngyáng (Yang Yuhuan's long passage in act 3; 杨玉环), rénchén (act 4). **Summarized by Mandarin, not by Guanzhong dialect rhyme categories.**
6. The only "**ballad-style villain's confession**" in the volume: An Lushan's (安禄山) "wolf / sheep" passage (p0270), beginning with seven-character couplets, turning in the middle to a three-character lead, and closing with four four-character lines in thesis–antithesis–synthesis.

### 7.8 Chuanju: Rhyme Comes First

Chuanju gets a section of its own: it has two things other banqiang genres lack—**a rhyme system different from Peking opera's**, and **a bāngqiāng that can be embedded inside a sung line**.

#### 7.8.1 The Rhymes of Sichuan Mandarin: the Entering Tone Is Assigned to Yangping, Hence Rhyme Words Differ from Peking Opera's

Source: pan/02 instruction 11 (based on p0040–p0041, p0043–p0044).

- **Do not force the Mandarin thirteen rhyme categories onto Chuanju rhymes.**
- Two phonological features: **① front and back nasals are not distinguished, so rénchén and zhōngdōng rhyme interchangeably** (p0040–p0041 rhyme together: 紧 jǐn / 门 mén / 行 xíng / 昏 hūn / 腾 téng / 滚 gǔn / 沉 chén / 恐 kǒng / 纷 fēn / 定 dìng / 层 céng / 僧 sēng); **② the entering tone is assigned to yangping, so "se, ke, re, jie" can enter the miēxié category** (p0043–p0044 rhyme together: 雪 xuě / 别 bié / 折 zhé / 色 sè / 客 kè / 热 rè / 洁 jié / 蝶 dié).
- **Self-check**: wherever reading in Mandarin makes you think "that doesn't rhyme", read it in Sichuan dialect first, then judge.

Two accompanying rhyme techniques (same note, instructions 9 and 10): **a change of rhyme is a change of face**—Wu Song (武松) switches from the yīqī category to yóuqiú at the same moment he flies into a rage (pan/02 p0047); self-check: at every turning point, does the rhyme change with it? If not, you have thrown away a tool for nothing. **Save the risky rhyme for the longest passage**—the longest aria in the play uses miēxié (pan/02 p0043–p0044); condition for failure: risky categories have few characters, and if you cannot assemble apt words you must retreat to a broad rhyme; **if even one word in the rhyme position is forced in just to rhyme, retreat**.

There is another rhyme technique for cross-time duets (same note, instruction 3): **cross-time duets should share one rhyme** (p0036 both in yánqián; p0052 both in yóuqiú). **Condition for failure**: if the two are supposed to be "unable to talk to each other", then you should instead change tune and rhyme.

#### 7.8.2 How the Five-Voice Combination Is Notated: the Tune-Title Column Is Actually a "Singing-Method Instruction Column"

Source: wei-bashan/02 §3, question 4. *The Scholar of Bashan* **contains not a single newly composed tune title** (all 11 gāoqiāng qǔpái are existing Chuanju tunes), but in the parentheses where tune titles should go, four kinds of thing that are not qǔpái appear:

| Type | How written | Where it appears | Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Vocal system as tune title** | 「（唱"昆曲"）」 [sings "Kunqu"] | p0029, p0030, p0050, p0074 | The Chuanju notation of "**五腔并用 (wǔqiāng bìngyòng — five vocal systems used together)**"—the tune-title column says "sing this passage in Kunqu style" (昆腔, kūnqiāng), and **which Kunqu qǔpái to use is left to the composer** |
| **Another genre's bǎnshì as tune title** | 「（唱京戏"西皮二六"）」 [sings Peking opera "xīpí èrliù"] | p0054 | **Vocal system is a character label**: Sichuan characters sing gāoqiāng; the prince from Beijing speaks Beijing colloquial and sings Peking-opera bǎnshì |
| **Recitation method as tune title** | 「（念"韵白"）」 [recites "rhymed speech"] | p0049 | A letter is recited in rhymed speech instead of sung |
| **Vocal-form category / loose tune** | [笛引] [Díyǐn], [七句半] [Qījùbàn], [流水腔] [Liúshuǐqiāng] | throughout the play | Less qǔpái than categories of gāoqiāng vocal form |

**Entry for the librettist**: in modern newly written Chuanju scripts, **the qǔpái-name column is in practice a "singing-method instruction column"**, which may be filled with a qǔpái name, a vocal-system name, another genre's bǎnshì name, or a recitation-method name. The same method appears in pan/02 instruction 7: **borrow another genre's vocal system for comedy and a sense of otherness** (p0049: the Sesame Official (芝麻官) "sings in Henan bangzi (河南梆子) style"; Anna (安娜) sings an aria). **Condition for failure**: He Kaisan (何开三) records that the play also wove in Yueju and pop songs (p0115), **but these are not marked in the script**—any vocal system not marked in the script is decided by the music design, and **the librettist cannot rely on it to carry structural function**.

**The full list of the five vocal systems in "five vocal systems used together": not recorded in the notes.** wei-bashan gives only the phrase "five vocal systems used together", together with textual traces of **gāoqiāng** (the main body of the play) and **Kunqu** (marked 4 times), plus 1 instance of Peking-opera [Xīpí èrliù]. The set of names gāoqiāng / Kunqu / huqin (húqín — two-stringed fiddle) / tanxi (tánxì — plucked-string opera) / dengdiao (dēngdiào — lantern tune) has no source in the existing notes, so this file does not list it.

#### 7.8.3 Bāngqiāng Can Be Embedded Inside a Sung Line—the Hardest Textual Dividing Line Between Gāoqiāng and the Banqiang System

luo-v2/04 1.8's original judgment: **only Chuanju bāngqiāng is embedded inside a sung line (it can interrupt a line of song); the choruses of other genres stay at the head or tail of a scene or between scenes and never enter the interior of an aria passage—this is the hardest textual dividing line between gāoqiāng and other banqiang genres.**

Three pieces of textual evidence (wei-bashan/02):

1. **Cutting off a sung line** (p0030): Nichang (霓裳) sings "Nichang the woman, singing softly—" / bāngqiāng: "Put on a smiling face!" / Nichang turns her back and sings. Three characters cut off the sung line and rewrite "singing softly" as "put on a smiling face"; **it is the switch from "performance" to "inner life"**.
2. **Taking up the antithetical lower line and magnifying the subject** (p0026): Meng Dengke sings "I sigh that tonight, on the road to the Yellow Springs, there is no inn—" / bāngqiāng ([刮骨令] [Guāgǔlìng]): "Where will they lodge, the three thousand wronged souls of Bashan?" **The antithesis is complete, and the subject shifts from "I" to "three thousand souls".**
3. **The bāngqiāng executes the change of tune title** (p0026): the bāngqiāng starts [Guāgǔlìng] anew and "turns to the [孝顺歌] [Xiàoshùngē] 合同 (hétóng — chorus join)"—**the change of tune is done by the bāngqiāng, and the characters follow**, a structural power unique to bāngqiāng.

Accompanying prohibition (luo-v2/04 instruction 5): **when writing for a non-gāoqiāng genre, do not stuff commentary lines into the middle of a character's aria passage.**

> **Gaps in the material (common to all subsections of §7):** (1) luo-v2 lists four limitations itself: **no bǎnshì** (the eight scripts contain not a single bǎnshì marker, so it cannot answer "how many characters does [二六 Èrliù] require" or "what emotion does [慢板 Mànbǎn] go with"); **no rhyme-book-level rhyme judgment** (rhyme names are summarized by Mandarin pronunciation; Yongju uses Ningbo dialect, Chuanju Sichuan dialect, Qinqiang Guanzhong dialect, and the actual rhyme categories do not match Mandarin—**whenever these lyrics are used as rhyme examples, one must first go through the dialect rhyme table of that genre**); **the sample is one author** (how much of the genre difference is the genre and how much is Luo Huaizhen personally cannot be distinguished in this volume; for example, "one chorus layer per play" is obviously a personal technique); **two plays are adapted from other genres / other forms** (see 7.2, 7.7). (2) Apart from Huaiju (3 plays), Yongju, Huangmei opera, Yuju, Chuanju, and Qinqiang each have only 1 sample; **each genre needs at least one more script by someone else for comparison before conclusions can be written as genre constraints**. (3) The correspondence between **bǎnshì / vocal-tune names and line patterns** in Huaiju, Yongju, Huangmei opera, Chuanju, and Qinqiang is **entirely absent from the existing notes**.

## §8 Aria Capacity and Genre Resources

### 8.1 Aria Capacity: Calculated by Genre, and Recalculated for Performances Outside the Region

Sources: luo-v6/05 12.3 (p0182–p0183) + luo-v6/04 9.2 (p0131).

| Genre | Lines of aria text in a whole play | Maximum for one passage / one singer | Evidence and source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conventional genres (Luo's baseline)** | **Two or three hundred lines are enough** | — | "For an ordinary genre, two or three hundred lines of aria text in a play are quite enough" (luo-v6/05 p0182) |
| **Huaiju** | — | **A single aria can run 180 lines**; when the transplanted play *Guan Yu Executes His Son* (《关羽斩子》) added aria text, **Lord Guan (关公) sang more than 200 lines in one stretch** | "Huaiju is a genre that can really sing… a single aria can run 180 lines, which is why Huaiju adaptations of scripts from other genres always add aria text"; *Guan Yu Executes His Son* "let Lord Guan sing more than 200 lines in one stretch; the actors sang and the audience listened, and both cried 'deeply satisfying'" (luo-v6/05 p0182) |
| **Yuju** | — | **One person can carry a passage of thirty or forty lines without a change of singer** (*Journey to Fengshan*, about 40 lines); the longest passage in *Chaoyanggou* is about 30 lines (one rhyme to the end) | luo-v2/04 1.4 (p0213–14); yang/02 #38 (score p0142) |
| **Yueju** | 496 aria markers across 7 plays; density per play 1.29–2.63 per page | The longest single-rhyme passage runs about 40 lines (p0172) | luo-v3/04 sample table, C1 |
| Peking opera / Yongju / Huangmei opera / Chuanju / Qinqiang | — | — | — |

**Contrasting negative data**: *Golden Dragon and Mayfly* toured more than a dozen cities on the mainland over four years, playing a hundred performances and winning 43 awards; its three performances at Taipei's National Theater "were not the sensation expected" (luo-v6/04 p0131):

> "The audience generally found **the protagonist's several long arias drawn from ancient Huaiju vocal styles 'long and dragging', blocking the normal development of the plot**; and the lighting, praised on the mainland as 'superb', left people 'dazzled and thoroughly annoyed'."

The book's explanation: "This had to do with Taipei audiences' unfamiliarity with Huaiju vocal style and **the different pacing expectations for new-style theater on the two sides of the strait**."

**Two parallel readings**: **capacity is capacity within the genre's home region**—the same aria is "deeply satisfying" inside the dialect area and "blocks the plot" outside it. **Self-check**: if this script is performed outside the genre's home region, should the longest aria be cut?

**Hard constraint (quoted from the source)**: "But **however much aria text is added, it must not affect the development of the plot or the character's personality**, so there is a limit." (luo-v6/05 p0182) Another line in the same place (p0183): "**I have a thousand lines of aria text in my belly**; **the aria text of Chinese opera is just that one little mechanism turned this way and that; it is not hard to master**."

### 8.2 Genre Resources = the Dialect Area

Source: luo-v6/03 7.2 (p0104–p0105). Diagnosis and prescription for Yangju (扬剧); omitted passages are left in the notes:

> "Yangju… under the squeeze of Peking opera, Huaiju, Yueju, and Xiju (锡剧), **did not have time to develop fully**… every excerpt play bears the shadow of a major genre. **Which one exists only in Yangju?**"

> "**All of Yangzhou's culture is her resource: Yangzhou pronunciation and the melodies that extend from those sounds; Yangzhou's little tunes, Yangzhou's folk songs, the greater Yangzhou region once administered by Yangzhou Prefecture under the broad concept—wherever Yangzhou dialect is spoken, it is her resource**; and in this way our tradition opens up at once."

**Template: a genre's resource boundary = the boundary of its dialect area, not the current troupe's repertory.**

**How to use this to judge "whether a subject can be given to this genre"**:

1. **Draw the dialect area first, not the play list.** A subject's region, character accents, available little tunes and folk songs, place names, and objects—anything inside the genre's dialect area (including its historical greater region) is a resource; **what lies outside is not impossible to write, but the genre has no ready linguistic or musical material to receive it**.
2. **Turn the criterion around and ask**: "Which play exists only in this genre?"—if the subject would be the same in another genre, the genre's resources are not being used, and the result is "the shadow of a major genre".
3. **Read together with §8.1**: subject inside the dialect area → aria capacity can be written to the genre's upper limit (Huaiju can go up to 180 lines); outside the dialect area → cut the longest aria first.
4. **Read together with §7**: the line-pattern test must also be passed—Yuju favors ten-character lines (3-3-4), Qinqiang four- and five-character lines, Yongju and Huangmei opera seven-character lines, Huaiju takes seven-character lines as its base, and Yueju mainly seven-character with ten-character secondary. **If the register a subject demands fights the genre's dominant line pattern, changing genres is less trouble than changing line patterns.**

> **Gaps in the material:** (1) Only **Huaiju** has definite figures for aria capacity (180 / 200+); conventional genres have only the rough figure "two or three hundred lines", and **the notes do not record the upper limit on total lines per play for Peking opera, Yueju, Yongju, Huangmei opera, Chuanju, or Qinqiang**; the two figures in the Yuju row are passage lengths counted from scripts, not genre regulations. (2) The Taipei feedback is a reception record of **one run, three performances**, not statistics. (3) "Genre resources = dialect area" comes from a single lecture, and its only original example is Yangju; **applying it to other genres is this file's extension, not the source book's claim**.
