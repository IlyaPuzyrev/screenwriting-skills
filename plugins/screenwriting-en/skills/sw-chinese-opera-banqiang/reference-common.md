# Chinese Opera Screenwriting Shared-Layer reference: 三性 (sān xìng — the Three Qualities), Two Measures, the Rehearsal Layer, and the Lower Limit of Adaptation

> This file is shared by sw-chinese-opera-banqiang and sw-chinese-opera-qupai.

It covers only the layer that both vocal systems must observe. Matters that hold only for 板腔体 (bǎnqiāngtǐ — the banqiang system, which builds arias from rhymed couplets and varies the metrical pattern) — the sequence of 板式 (bǎnshì — the metrical pattern of a banqiang aria), 十三辙 (shísān zhé — the thirteen rhyme categories of Peking opera), and 锣鼓经 (luógǔjīng — percussion patterns) — belong to `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang`; matters that hold only for 曲牌体 (qǔpáitǐ — the qupai system, which fits lyrics to fixed tune models) — 宫调 (gōngdiào — the modal system), 套数 (tàoshù — suites of qupai in one mode), 格律 (gélǜ — prosodic rules), and 衬字 (chènzì — filler syllables beyond the prescribed count) — belong to `sw-chinese-opera-qupai`.

**Sources and page numbers**: zhang-guo = the printed-book page of Zhang Geng (张庚) and Guo Hancheng (郭汉城), *A General Survey of Chinese Opera* (《中国戏曲通论》) | li-yu = Li Yu (李渔), *Casual Expressions of Idle Feeling* (《闲情偶寄》) (**these notes follow the chapter pages on Wikisource; the entire set has no printed page numbers**, so citations give chapter · item, and the page field reads “not in the notes”) | luo-v6 = the scanned page of Luo Huaizhen (罗怀臻), volume 6 (printed page = scanned page − 6) | wang-jilie = Wang Jilie (王季烈), *Talks on Opera from the Yǐnlú Studio* (《螾庐曲谈》), volume + page | gu = the PDF page of Gu Zhongyi (顾仲彝), *Dramaturgy: Theory and Technique* (《编剧理论与技巧》) (printed page = pNNNN − 24) | wu-mei = the page of Wu Mei (吴梅), *Random Talks on Opera* (《顾曲麈谈》) | tang-mudanting (《牡丹亭》 *The Peony Pavilion*) / kong-taohuashan (《桃花扇》 *The Peach Blossom Fan*) / yang / luo-v3 = the original book page of the respective full-text notes.

## §1 The Three Qualities and 意象说 (Yìxiàngshuō — the Theory of Yixiang)

Source: zhang-guo/02 — Chapter 3, by Shen Daren (沈达人). **The original text on yixiang theory is in Chapter 4** (zhang-guo/03); it is merged here as the task requires, with page numbers following Chapter 4.

### 1.1 The Central Proposition

The conclusion of the whole chapter (p0135): “**Chinese opera is in fact a formally regulated kind of song-and-dance dramatic poetry.**” The 三性 (sān xìng — three qualities) are derived from this. The bottom-line statement (p0120): “Abolish this unique art form in which singing, recitation, acting, and fighting are highly integrated, and you abolish Chinese opera.” The same page names the playwright's means: “The opera playwright, too, must employ **aria lyrics, spoken dialogue, and 科介 (kējiè — the action and business cues written into the script)**.”

**The first textual constraint**: kējiè stands **alongside** aria lyrics and spoken dialogue as a parallel mode of the script, not as a parenthetical note appended to dialogue. Kējiè gets its own line and states clearly what is done. **Self-check**: If you copy out every kējiè in the play, does it read as one continuous line of action? If not, they have been written as annotations.

### 1.2 Rhythmicity (节奏性, jiézòuxìng; p0135–0141)

Definition (p0135): operatic performance “is as a rule **never without music**.” Overall function (p0139): “**Stage rhythm has only one through-line, and it is determined by dramatic conflict and dramatic action.**”

|Point|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|Word count is set by the beat|Slow beat = **little verbal feeling, much vocal feeling** (lyric expression); fast beat (including jǐn dǎ màn chàng, fast accompaniment under slow singing) = **much verbal feeling, little vocal feeling** (narrative, question and answer); 散板 (sǎnbǎn — free tempo) = agitation. p0136|**Write little in lyric passages and much in narrative passages.** The same table in Chapter 6 covers both 曲牌 (qǔpái — fixed tune models) and bǎnshì — “**all fall into these three types**” p0283|
|A long aria is written as a sequence of beats|The only complete example in the book: Su San (苏三) in 《三堂会审》 (*The Joint Hearing of the Three Courts*): 【散板】→【慢板 (mànbǎn — slow tempo)】→【原板 (yuánbǎn — primary tempo)】→【流水 (liúshuǐ — flowing tempo)】→【二六 (èrliù — two-six tempo)】→【流水】→【散板】. p0136–0137|Do not write arias that are “as long as you like”; do not stuff narrative into mànbǎn; do not put a lyric climax in liúshuǐ or fast bǎnshì|
|Spoken dialogue has four levels|散白 (sǎnbái — colloquial speech) → 韵白 (yùnbái — heightened, rhymed speech) → 引子 (yǐnzi — entrance verse) → 数板 (shùbǎn — rhythmic patter); yùnbái is “**written in prose**” p0137; Li Yu: “the study of 宾白 (bīnbái — the spoken passages of opera) puts sonority first” p0138|For each passage of spoken dialogue, fix its level first, then its sentence pattern|
|Even scenes without song or speech go into the script|In 《乌龙院》 (*The Black Dragon Court*), Song Jiang (宋江) searches for the letter-case in what is “close to pantomime,” organized by the percussion patterns 【四击头 (sìjītóu)】, 【乱锤 (luànchuí)】, and 【撕边 (sībiān)】. p0138|A silent scene must write out its percussion and its sequence of action|
|**Self-check**|Failure diagnosis: “a succession of uniformly lyrical tunes… monotonous and dull” p0281–0282|Label each aria with one word — lyric / narrative / free — and see whether long stretches come out the same color|

### 1.3 Virtuality (虚拟性, xūnǐxìng; p0141–0149)

Definition (p0141): under conditions where no concrete object or environment exists, opera “**engages the audience's imagination** through bodily movement.” The operating rule (p0144–0145, the passage that constrains script convention most severely): “**When no character is on stage, the stage signifies no time and no place. Only when a character enters is a specific place defined; when the character exits, that specific place disappears again; and even without the character's exit, the place may change.**”

|Point|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|A scene is a unit of entrances and exits, not a location|Derived from the Song dynasty practices of calling the troupe on and dismissing it (勾队／放队, gōuduì/fàngduì), and from Zhu Quan's (朱权) “ghost gate” (鬼门道, guǐméndào); entrances and exits “become the basic unit of operatic scene structure” p0143–0144|**“Scene heading = location” does not hold**; slugline and 场号制 (chǎng hào zhì — the scene-number system) cannot be copied over unchanged|
|The scene is a variable map|Liu Chengji's (刘成基) 《赠绨袍》 (*The Gift of the Silk Robe*): “the bedroom is in the inner stage, the main hall in the back half of the stage, the courtyard in the front half”; after Fan Sui (范睢) enters the hall, “the main gate, side gate, alley, and courtyard all vanish at once” p0184|Write “wherever someone walks, that becomes the place,” not a floor plan|
|Verisimilitude is psychological|“not truth in the physical sense… but the truth of emotion, feeling, and atmosphere in the psychological sense” p0146; feeding the chickens in 《拾玉镯》 (*Picking Up the Jade Bracelet*) stays close to life / the 趟马 (tàmǎ — horse-riding mime) in 《昭君出塞》 (*Zhaojun Goes Beyond the Frontier*) escapes mere imitation p0147|When choosing a virtual movement, ask whether **the emotion at this moment needs magnification**, not whether the movement looks lifelike|
|Time can be compressed or extended|“One 圆场 (yuánchǎng — a circuit of the stage representing a long journey) is a trek of dozens of miles”; it is “a kind of **psychological time and psychological space**” p0148|Write duration by psychological time, not clock time|
|**What must not be written**|A Jia (阿甲): “**Place the virtual stage movement inside a dramatic environment fixed by realistic scenery, and operatic stage movement will lose all its virtual power.**” p0195|Do not write realistic scene description or build literal scenery|
|**Self-check / failure**|—|Ask of each scene: Who enters and who exits to cut this scene? Which line of aria or which movement tells us where we are? If you cannot answer the second question, the location lives only in the stage direction. **Failure condition**: once realistic scenery is used, the entire virtual apparatus fails — the script must choose a side, not compromise|

### 1.4 Conventionality (程式性, chéngshìxìng; p0149–0158)

**Master table of 程式 (chéngshì — the codified conventions of performance and text)** (p0150, transcribed as-is):

|Department|List of conventions|
|-|-|
|Literature|Aria lyrics: paired seven-character or ten-character lines; long-and-short lines with prescribed syllable and line counts. Spoken dialogue: yǐnzi, couplets (对子, duìzi), poems, and shùbǎn. Special forms: 帮腔 (bāngqiāng — offstage chorus), 背供 (bèigōng — an aside to the audience, unheard by the other characters), and 自报家门 (zìbào jiāmén — self-introduction).|
|Music|The qupai of every mode in the qupai-suite system; the principal and reverse-mode bǎnshì (正调/反调, zhèngdiào/fǎndiào) of the bǎnshì-variation system; percussion scores and 牌子 (páizi — titled instrumental pieces) for suona, huqin, and banhu|
|Performance|Conventions of singing (chàng) and recitation (niàn); conventions of acting (zuò) and fighting (dǎ); conventions for entrances, exits, and stage blocking; conventions for expressing emotion|
|Stage design|Character styling (makeup and costume above all, supported by fans, handkerchiefs, ceremonial processional insignia, and weapons); scenic styling (city cloths, carriage flags, water flags, boat oars, horse whips, and the different arrangements and uses of tables and chairs)|
|行当 (hángdang — role type)|“Role type is precisely the combination and concentration of the various operatic conventions” — the conventions of each department form different **convention sequences** according to role type|

|Point|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|**Role type is the master switch**|“**Once the question of a character's role-type affiliation is settled, the limitation of portraying character through literary language alone is broken**” p0150–0151|Assigning the role type = calling up an entire convention sequence at once. **Self-check**: Does every character in the cast list have a role type?|
|A role type may change mid-play|《问病逼宫》 (*Asking about the Illness and Storming the Palace*): Yang Guang (杨广) goes from 小生 (xiǎoshēng — young male) → 花脸 (huāliǎn — painted face) → 小丑 (xiǎochǒu — comic). p0175|**Self-check**: A mid-play change of role type needs an event to hinge on, not merely a change of mood|
|俊扮 (jùnbàn — handsome makeup) and 丑扮 (chǒubàn — comic makeup) do not equal praise and blame|“Playing a role as 丑 (chǒu — comic) does not equal… criticism; it is only a **variant of beauty**” p0157|Do not let makeup substitute for value judgment|
|The three properties|Comprehensiveness (the table above) / normativity = “the artistic standardization of material drawn from life” (a full red face implies loyalty and courage; 富贵衣, fùguìyī — the “wealth-and-honor robe” of patches, signals destitution) p0151–0152 / general applicability = “**general applicability is the extension of normativity**” p0153|Using old conventions is not laziness|
|Law one: reform conventions from life|In 《穆桂英挂帅》 (*Mu Guiying Takes Command*), a movement passage and the percussion pattern 【九锤半 (jiǔchuíbàn)】 are inserted between two sung lines; the criterion is “**a contradiction has arisen between role-type norms and the character's personality**” p0155; “**the 心象 (xīnxiàng — inner image) always appears in rhythm with operatic music**” p0156|Before breaking a rule, state exactly where the contradiction lies|
|Law two: conventions themselves have no bias|“Only when connected with… an aesthetic attitude is it actively called into play” p0156|Praise and blame are carried by blocking, not built into the convention|
|Law three: the formula for creating new conventions|Poeticization, musicalization, choreographization, and ornamentation; “**what matters… is the grasp of the principles for refining operatic conventions**” p0157–0158|Refine new conventions through those four transformations|
|What to write and what not to write|Composite convention sets carry content direction; single technical conventions do not. p0338–0339|Write qǐbà (起霸 — the opening martial pose sequence), tàmǎ, and zǒubiān (走边 — the stealth travel sequence); do not write xiàyāo (下腰 — backbend) or shuànlíng (涮翎 — circular pheasant-feather flourish)|
|**Failure condition**|If conventions do not resolve their relation to life and aesthetics, “**they will become a historical inertia**”; once resolved, “**one can also gain freedom within strict norms**” p0158|**Self-check**: Is there any convention here that exists only to look good and does not serve the emotion of this moment?|

### 1.5 The Theory of Yixiang (Chapter 4, p0160–0198)

The three-part division (p0161): “**摹象 (móxiàng — mimetic), 喻象 (yùxiàng — metaphorical), and 意象 (yìxiàng — yixiang)** — three main types… **Mimetic drama dominates the Western stage; yixiang drama commands the Eastern stage.**”

||Mimetic drama|Metaphorical drama|Yixiang drama|
|-|-|-|-|
|Representative form|European realist drama|The Theatre of the Absurd|Chinese opera|
|Theoretical source|Aristotle's mimēsis|—|The “stimulus-and-response” theory (物感说, wùgǎnshuō) of the *Record of Music* (《乐记》)|
|Object–self relation|Object foregrounded, self concealed|Self foregrounded, object departed from|**Object and self fused**|
|Nature of the image|Representation|Expression|**Expression grounded in representation**|

Definition (p0165): the yixiang method creates an image in which “**the object contains the self, and the self contains the object**.” Overall characterization (p0191): “**A combination of representational stage action and expressive stage movement.**”

|Point|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|The author need not exit|Mimetic drama “**deeply conceals… its tendentiousness**” p0163; Chinese opera has three licensed exits for the author: **the 副末开场 (fùmò kāichǎng — opening prologue delivered by the fumo role) / the accompanying chorus and bāngqiāng / the cartoon-like zìbào jiāmén** p0203–0204|To take a position, use these three exits; the general rule that “the author must disappear” holds only partly in opera|
|Adaptation = adding a “meaning”|Dou E (窦娥) has more rebellious spirit than her prototype — “Guan Hanqing (关汉卿) infused… his aesthetic judgment” p0173–0174|**Self-check**: What does the protagonist have here that the prototype did not? If you cannot say, this adaptation is mere transcription|
|Write the spirit, not the form|Mimetic drama “transmits spirit through form” (Ibsen's “last button”); opera “**seeks spiritual likeness, attains likeness by departing from form, and possesses both spirit and form**” p0176; Sikong Tu (司空图): “attain likeness by departing from form” p0180|Describe appearance only where it bears on spiritual likeness|
|Anchor the term back to the source word|The Western term *image* is limited to simile and metaphor; “inner visual image” (内心视象) is an image within the mind; “**neither is the yixiang of this book**” p0169–0170|Do not translate it as image/imagery, and do not conflate it with “inner visual image”|
|**Guard against misappropriation**|The spoken-drama examples in Chapter 4 (the entrance of Luo Binwang (骆宾王) at p0190; Ibsen on character at p0176) describe laws shared by both kinds of drama|These passages are not opera technique and must not be adopted directly|

## §2 Two Measures for Artistic Method: Virtual/Real and Abstract/Concrete

Source: zhang-guo/03 (Chapter 4, by Shen Daren). The first asks “whether this scene is written as real or as virtual”; the second asks “whether this passage gives more or less ink than life does.”

### 2.1 Measure One: Virtual / Real

**What it measures**: whether this scene should be carried by literature or by performance. **Theoretical root** (p0191): “**A combination of representational stage action and expressive stage movement**… grounded in representational stage action.”

|Point|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|The representational side (real)|Stage action must “conform to the logic of life… be governed by causality” p0191–0192. The four-part pattern of 《蝴蝶杯·投县》 (*The Butterfly Cup · Taking the Case to the County*): kneeling to plead → “stand up to answer” → “sit down to speak” → producing the butterfly cup to claim kinship p0192–0193|The book's comment: “**Let the relationships among the characters remain temporarily hidden from those onstage together, then rely on the characters' active action… to reveal them.**”|
|The expressive side (virtual)|Blocking “is built on **large-scale curvilinear movement**”; A Jia: “the step does not leave the stage” p0194–0195|The virtual half must have a performer to carry it|
|**Criterion (practical test, p0225)**|“**Scenes dominated by song and recitation… use real writing: make the literary texture rich and write them through; scenes dominated by acting and dance… use virtual writing: keep the text ethereal and concise, stopping as soon as the meaning arrives.**”|Evidence: *The White Snake* (《白蛇传》) — “A Walk by the Lake” (《游湖》) and “Broken Bridge” (《断桥》) are written real; “Stealing the Herb” (《盗草》) says only “Bai Suzhen (白素贞) fights the Deer Boy (鹿童), wounds him, and hastily picks the magic lingzhi”; “The Water Battle” (《水斗》) says only “the water creatures and the gods fight”|
|Combining the hidden and the shown in offstage scenes|The onstage singing of Guiying (桂英) in 《打渔杀家》 (*The Fisherman's Revenge*) is real writing; the offstage calls “Ten! Twenty! Thirty! Forty!” are virtual writing p0226|An offstage scene needs a real-written line onstage to support it|
|Characters, too, are divided into real and virtual|《西厢记》 (*The Romance of the Western Chamber*) writes Yingying (莺莺) frontally only in “Listening to the Lute,” “Peeking at the Letter,” and “Farewell at the Long Pavilion,” and writes her obliquely elsewhere; Li Zhi (李贽): “nothing but groping for the effect” p0226|The protagonist need not be written frontally in every scene|
|General principle|“Scenes that are primary should be written real; scenes that are secondary should be written virtual”; if the real is not written real, it is not written through; if the virtual is not written virtual, it becomes long and slack p0225|Decide primary versus secondary first, then real versus virtual|
|**Failure conditions**|① Leaving a blank is an active technique — the climax of 《三岔口》 (*At the Crossroads*) is only a stage direction, yet it “was performed for a full half-hour” p0202; ② once realistic scenery enters, this measure fails p0195|① **Self-check**: For every blank you leave, can you say whom it is left for (acting? fight choreography? bāngqiāng?); if not, it is an omission; ② realistic scenery pins down the environment, and virtual writing is left with nothing but “fewer words”|

### 2.2 Measure Two: Artistic Abstraction / Artistic Concreteness

**What it measures**: **whether a passage is related to the characters' emotional experience**. What is related is written slower than life; what is unrelated, faster than life.

|Point|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|Definitions of the two methods|**Artistic abstraction** = “the meaning arrives but the brush does not” for inconsequential stretches of event; **artistic concreteness** = making the emotional activity that must be highlighted specific, enlarged, and finely written p0197|Judge relevance first, then allocate words|
|Example|A Jia on 《乌龙院》 (*The Black Dragon Court*): “**To find the letter-case, he wanders the streets and alleys… the meaning can only arrive while the brush does not**”; **recitation and acting both use the concrete method; the search through the streets is dispatched in a single stroke by the abstract method** p0197|Use both methods in the same scene|
|Principle of selection|“**It depicts only the surrounding things related to the characters' emotional experience; everything unrelated is discarded wholesale**” p0196|It **replaces the general rule of “enter late, leave early”**: that rule cuts by scene duration; this measure cuts by emotional relevance|
|Characterization|“**It does not cling to… the truth of fact, but seeks… the truth of feeling**” = “the truth of psychological logic” p0196; Fan Xiwen (范晞文): “**turn scenery into feeling**” p0198|Judge truth by psychological logic, not by factual plausibility|
|**Failure condition**|Abstraction can be used only on **the process of events**, never on **changes in character relationships**|If the relationship is abstracted away, concreteness has nowhere to land (in 《投县》 *Taking the Case to the County*, none of the four levels can be omitted)|
|**Self-check action**|—|List the three passages with the most ink and the three with the least; ask of each, “Is this related to the protagonist's emotional experience?” If one of the most-written passages answers “no,” it should be abstracted; if one of the least-written answers “yes,” it is an omission|

### 2.3 Using the Two Measures Together

||Measure One: Virtual / Real|Measure Two: Abstract / Concrete|
|-|-|-|
|What it measures|Whether literature or performance carries the scene|How much ink is given|
|Criterion|Whether the scene is dominated by song and recitation or by acting and dance|Whether it is related to emotional experience|
|Result|Text written full, or text kept ethereal|Slower than life, or faster than life|
|Failure|Fails when realistic scenery is present|Cannot be used on changes in character relationships|

A scene dominated by acting and dance can be both “virtually written” (few words) and “concrete” (lasting longer than life) — *Stealing the Herb* and *The Water Battle* are examples. **Only by using both measures at once will you avoid mistaking “few words” for “little drama.”**

## §3 Key Points from the General Theory of Opera Literature

Source: zhang-guo/04 and zhang-guo/05 — Chapter 5 in six sections, by Su Guorong (苏国荣); Chapter 6, by He Wei (何为). **Chapter 5 is the only chapter in the book written directly for playwrights.** Its definition (p0200): “Opera literature is the written dramatic form composed according to **the requirements of operatic stage performance**.”

### 3.1 Dramatic Poetry (p0200–0207)

|Point|Quotation or summary|Playwright's move|
|-|-|-|
|No form is missing|“Though chuanqi is a minor art… **no form is missing**” p0201|Let multiple literary forms coexist; do not seek uniformity|
|**Use the pen as a tongue**|“**You must write literature as music**” p0202|Read the draft aloud when finished; rewrite wherever the tongue catches|
|**Use the real to make the virtual**|“**Do not fill the text too full**… **leave broad space for the stage**” p0202|Every blank must be assignable to someone (use with §2.1)|
|**Use wholeness as change**|“‘**Drama**’ hides within change” p0202|Ask of each scene: What has changed since the previous scene?|
|Three breaches in 代言体 (dàiyántǐ — the dramatic mode in which the author speaks through characters)|The fùmò prologue / the accompanying chorus and bāngqiāng / naked evaluation through a character's mouth p0203–0204|The author takes a position through these three exits, not by stuffing it into interior monologue|
|The lyric scene is the climax scene|“The most brilliant scenes… are often lyric scenes” p0205–0206|Check whether the longest lyric passage falls at the climax|
|Antiphonal song = each sings their own inner life|“**Antiphonal singing is almost always each person singing his or her own inner activity**” p0206|Write the two inner lines separately first, then intercut them; do not write it as question and answer|

### 3.2 Character (p0207–0218)

|Point|Quotation or summary|Playwright's move|
|-|-|-|
|Against “single-trait character”|Bao Zheng's (包拯) uprightness and timidity “merge naturally together” p0209|Do not flatten characters just because this is opera|
|**Subjectivity commands richness**|“**Find the dominant aspect of the character and render it in thick, heavy color**”; the non-dominant aspects serve only as another color p0210|**Self-check**: Does the secondary color enrich the dominant color, or fight it?|
|Harboring feeling and conveying it|**Spiritual likeness = emotional likeness**; grasp the emotion and the action that issues from it p0211–0213|Give each character a one-word definition (Wang Siren, 王思任: “Du Liniang's (杜丽娘) enchantment, Liu Mengmei's (柳梦梅) infatuation, the old lady's softness, Du Anfu's (杜安抚 — Du Bao, the Pacification Commissioner) stubbornness, Chen Zuiliang's (陈最良) pedantry” p0212)|
|Meeting the scene, the heart stirs|“‘**The scenery before the eyes cannot be seen**’… **the natural environment is all spoken and sung through the characters' mouths**”; the law is “**write scenery according to feeling**” p0213–0214|**Self-check action**: Extract the landscape language and read it alone; ask whose it is and whether that character has the leisure for it at this moment|
|Counterexample of landscape language|In 《中山狼》 (*The Wolf of Zhongshan*), after escaping, the character sings of “the idle world of the upper eight grottoes” — “**it is dead scenery, not living scenery**” p0214–0215|The only named failure in the book; use it as a negative template|
|Landscape must be written within narrative|“The wonder is to paint scene within narrative”; in 《潇湘夜雨》 (*Night Rain over the Xiao and Xiang*), the “strange wind” and “sudden rain” are Cui Tong's (崔通) **accomplices** p0215|What happens if you do not: landscape language stops the play|
|Grasp the object and obtain its spirit|“The horse goes slowly, the carriage follows quickly” = “**the incarnation of a human soul**”; the play's title is the prop (《拾玉镯》, 《红灯记》 *The Red Lantern*) p0216–0217|**Self-check**: How many times does this object appear, and does its meaning change? Appearing once does not make it a through-line|
|Gathering into a type (role type)|“**One set of conventions, ten thousand personalities**”; character is “**already portrayed in the literary script**” p0217–0218|Role type is the master switch of writing, not a label (use with §1.4)|

### 3.3 Structure (p0219–0228; one entry on the layout of technique has a different source)

|Point|Quotation or summary|Playwright's move|
|-|-|-|
|Plot arises from character|“**Plot grows out of character, and character is completed in plot**” p0220|**Self-check**: Would this still work with another character? If yes, it did not come from character|
|The three layers of “nothing transmits unless it is extraordinary” (the subject-matter entry is merged here)|① The extraordinary must be true: “choose the extraordinary without losing its truth”; ② the extraordinary must be new: “**the old is the great enemy of the extraordinary**” (ordinary events can also be extraordinary); ③ the extraordinary must have feeling: “**it lies within human feeling and the nature of things**” p0220–0222|**Failure condition**: pursuing the extraordinary outside human feeling and the nature of things produces a “**dead plot**”|
|**Linear structure, not block structure**|“**A fluid scene-by-scene structure**, not… a fixed **act structure**… **taking the characters' entrances and exits as the unit**” p0223|The book explicitly refuses to rank them: the two structures negating each other “are both one-sided”|
|凤头猪肚豹尾 (fèng tóu zhū dù bào wěi — “phoenix head, pig belly, leopard tail”: a three-part opening, body, and ending)|Qiao Ji's (乔吉) six characters + Liu Xizai's (刘熙载) “the beginning should be restrained and measured, the middle should range freely through every change, the end should linger without exhaustion” p0223–0224|Open by planting suspense (“**drama must have suspense**”); in the middle, “raise sudden waves”; at the end, “**it is not the end of the play but its deepening**”|
|The single line is the norm|Three-line plots are the minority; “more often it is **single-line development, one thread to the end, and yet with winding changes**” p0224|Gain “**room to strengthen song, dance, and lyric expression, and to write the key scenes through**”; lose “the thinness of the play”|
|Seven pairs of structural devices|The mutual generation of virtual and real, the interleaving of sparse and dense, the mutual reflection of hidden and shown, the mixing of complex and simple, the tempering of cold and hot, the alternation of hard and soft, the separation of thick and thin p0225|Reduce them to two families: virtual/real and cold/hot; for virtual/real see §2.1|
|Tempering cold and hot|“**Hard and soft alternate, grief and joy alternate, civil and martial alternate, song and acting alternate, 生 (shēng — male) and 旦 (dàn — female) alternate with 净 (jìng — painted face) and 丑 (chǒu — comic)**” p0226–0227|It is also a **roster for the actors' physical stamina** (“keep **the roles' work and rest in proper measure**”) — the same law as the law of alternation in §7|
|The limit of complication|“**To pursue structural complication apart from changes in character feeling is to grasp the branch and neglect the root**” p0227|First comes the change of feeling; only then the change of event|
|**The layout of technique is the fourth layer of structure**|“**The layout of performance technique**… **does not necessarily appear in… the climactic scene; it more often adheres to moments when the character's inner will and emotional activity are stronger and richer**” p0436|**Note the source**: this entry is in **Chapter 9 (the directing chapter)**, by a different author from Chapter 5; it belongs to the same layer as Fan Junhong's (范钧宏) “technical structure” and Wang Jilie's “páichǎng”|

### 3.4 Conflict (p0228–0234)

|Point|Quotation or summary|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|Position|Abolishing conflict “also abolishes the ‘dramatic quality’ of opera” p0229–0230|Do not abolish conflict on the grounds that opera is lyrical|
|Handling apparent counterexamples|《小放牛》 (*The Little Cowherd*) is “a transitional form between song-dance and drama”; 《游园惊梦》 (*Wandering in the Garden, Waking from a Dream*) and 《十八相送》 (*The Eighteen Farewells*) are excerpts from full-length plays: **conflict runs through the whole play, only unevenly distributed** p0230|**The lyric scene in an excerpt is a “continuation segment of conflict”**; when extracting it for solo performance, state which conflict it continues|
|Three-dimensional structure|“Will is the cause of conflict… the mode of conflict depends on the different characters” p0231–0232|Conflict = will + character + action; missing one, it does not stand|
|Form one: external|“The two sides confront each other onstage in direct opposition” p0232|Direct confrontation scene|
|Form two: internal|In 《杀庙》 (*Killing in the Temple*), Han Qi (韩琪): “**external action is completed in an instant after a long period of inner conflict**” p0232–0233|**Proportion rule: the fiercer the external action, the longer the inner aria that precedes it**|
|Form three: **the submerged type (one-sided)**|“Only one side of the conflict is onstage; the other is submerged behind the stage… sometimes it is **a character who never appears**, sometimes **an environment**, sometimes **an idea**, sometimes **an era**”; Zhang Jiqing's (张继青) version of *The Peony Pavilion* “thoroughly hides” Du Bao (杜宝) and Chen Zuiliang; the book judges it “**rather difficult to control**” p0232–0234|Identify who or what the absent side is (a person / an environment / an idea / an era), and have the present side react to it once per scene. **Self-check**: If you delete it, does the present character's action still hold? If yes, it was never truly written in|

### 3.5 Genre (p0234–0248)

|Point|Quotation or summary|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|Three classification systems|By length (short play / full-length play / 折子戏, zhézixì — excerpt play / 连台本戏, liántái běnxì — multi-evening serial); by role-type inclination (文戏, wénxì — civil play / 武戏, wǔxì — martial play); by emotional effect (tragedy / comedy / 正剧, zhèngjù — serious drama) p0234|The book's “civil play / martial play” is coarser than Fan Junhong's (song-dominated / acting-dominated / fight-dominated / balanced) or Weng Ouhong's (翁偶虹) (song / spoken / martial); **use it only as corroboration**|
|Three forms of tragedy|The beauty of 阳刚 (yánggāng — masculine hardness) / the beauty of 阴柔 (yīnròu — feminine softness) / the beauty of 中和 (zhōnghé — balance and harmony) p0236–0242|Fix the form first, then the ending|
|Two techniques of balanced beauty|① Grief and joy intermingle (Li Yu: “within one act, all seven emotions are present”); ② a small symbolic joy after great sorrow. The limit is “**‘to the full’ without overflowing**” p0239–0241|The small joy after great sorrow must be symbolic, not literal|
|The book's own negative example|“‘Resentment without anger’ contains the political implication of preventing the lower classes… from being dissatisfied”; the **harmonizing ending** of 《棒打薄情郎》 (*Beating the Fickle Lover*) is an example p0241|**Self-check**: Is the reunion ending advancing the theme, or “dissolving antagonistic contradiction with the love of a man and a woman”?|
|Three features of comedy|① Joy is born of the good — “**comedies that celebrate the positive… are the majority… they are an affirmative form of life**,” unlike the negative form of Western Europe; ② joy without excess (“write grief through joyful scenery… it doubles the grief and joy”); ③ seeing beauty in the ugly p0244–0247|Writing celebratory comedy need not borrow the Western definition of comedy as negation|
|Three boundaries of serious drama|① Tragedy and comedy need a conflict running through the whole play; serious drama need not (*The Peony Pavilion* turns to joy in its second half, and “**tragic conflict lacks continuity and completeness**”); ② the nature of the protagonist; ③ emotional keynote p0247–0248|The book's verdict: “**Serious drama… far outnumbers tragedy and comedy.**”|

### 3.6 Language (p0249–0258)

Bīnbái and aria lyrics “basically belong to poetic language, because **bīnbái, too, observes tone patterns and rhythm**”; the *fu* mode is used for narrative exposition, *bi* and *xing* for feeling aroused by the scene (p0249). Three requirements: 肖似 (xiàosì — resemblance to the character), 本色 (běnsè — native quality), and 铿锵 (kēngqiāng — sonority).

|Layer|Quotation + page|Playwright's move / failure condition|
|-|-|-|
|Resemblance ①: role-type language|Li Yu: “in the mouth of a painted-face role, **one fears only that it is not coarse and vulgar enough**” p0250|Change role type, change speech style (Xiao En, 萧恩: 老生, lǎoshēng — older male, “vigorous and seasoned” / bangzi painted-face, “rough and bold”)|
|Resemblance ②: limitation|“Role-type language… is typed language” p0250–0251|Role type is only the first layer, not the destination|
|Resemblance ③: **heart / body / place**|**Heart** = inner emotion; **body** = status, quality, cultivation; **place** = linguistic environment, the given circumstances. Jin Shengtan (金圣叹) on 《赖婚》 (*The Broken Promise*): “Would it do to write it for the lady to sing? I say no” p0251|**The Chinese version of “cover the name and the speaker is still recognizable.” Self-check action: take out one line of aria or spoken dialogue and give it to three other people in the same scene; if even one of them could say it, the line fails.**|
|Resemblance ④: an example of “place”|The same passage “placed… in the lament of the empress dowager… loses its lightness and becomes frivolous”; placed in the mouth of Wang Shipeng (王十朋), it is “entirely appropriate” p0252–0253|Rejudge it once you change speaker and situation|
|Native quality ①: boundary|Lü Tiancheng (吕天成): “**structural fitness also discusses method; native quality refers only to 填词 (tiáncí — writing lyrics to a fixed tune model)**” p0253|Judge “structural fitness” and “linguistic native quality” separately|
|Native quality ②: reason|Opera is “gone in one hearing”; Xu Fuzuo (徐复祚): “**if it does not invite a second hearing, what leisure is there for thought?**” p0253|Set the lower bound at “understandable on one hearing”|
|Native quality ③: model|《拜月记》 (*Worshipping the Moon*): “narrates matters of feeling, winding and complete” + “**specific and vivid, danceable and performable**” p0253–0254|The standard for native quality includes “danceable and performable”|
|Native quality ④: **two failure conditions**|① Ornate obscurity is unacceptable (“composing opera with poetic language”); ② **coarseness is not native quality either** — Shen Jing (沈璟): “**thin and tasteless**”; Lü Tiancheng: “**native quality does not lie in copying everyday speech**” p0254–0255|The way out is Lu Xun's (鲁迅) “**refined language**” and “**moving toward the folk**,” especially important for modern opera|
|Native quality ⑤: unity with literary grace|《四姑娘·逼嫁》 (*Fourth Daughter · Forced Marriage*): “clear as speech, yet all of it emotional, imagistic… belonging to the character” p0255|Native quality does not mean blandness|
|Sonority ①: alternation of level and oblique tones|Li Yu: “level, oblique, oblique, level, level, oblique, oblique; oblique, level, level, oblique, oblique, level, level… bīnbái is the same” p0256|Bīnbái must also be arranged in level and oblique tones|
|Sonority ②: **choose resonant rhymes first**|Wang Jide (王骥德): “**东钟 (Dōngzhōng) is grand; 江阳 (Jiāngyáng), 皆来 (Jiēlái), and 萧豪 (Xiāoháo) are resonant; 歌戈 (Gēgē) and 家麻 (Jiāmá) are harmonious — these are the most beautiful rhymes to hear**. **支思 (Zhīsī) is withered and cannot rouse itself**… **侵寻 (Qīnxún), 监咸 (Jiānxián), 廉纤 (Liánxiān)… do not use them much.**” p0256|**Failure condition (the book supplies it)**: if you deliberately use oblique or risky rhymes and the performer can command them, “one should also welcome it”|
|Sonority ③: choose rhymes according to the local dialect of the genre|“Attend to the dialect character of the genre, so that the composer can ‘**fit the melody to the words**’ (按字行腔, àn zì xíng qiāng)” p0257|When writing across genres, choose the rhyme again|
|Sonority ④: **do not write 豆腐块 (dòufukuài — “tofu-block” aria format: uniform line lengths with no variation)**|Tian Han (田汉): “that way it becomes hard to vary the tune”; “**breaking through… the tofu-block format… makes winding variation possible**” p0257|Change at least two sentence patterns within one aria|
|Overall tightness|“**The problem is not that we are too confined by musical rules; it is that many works have no musical law to be found**” p0256|Do not insist that every character match the score, but there must be a discoverable law|

## §4 The Rehearsal Layer: Key Points from the Five Chapters of Li Yu's 《演习部》 (*Yǎnxí Bù* — “Section on Performance”)

Source: li-yu/04. **Page numbers: not in the notes** — the quotations follow the chapter pages on Wikisource (for “Escaping Convention, Part Five,” the Guoxue Dianji website); no printed page numbers were recorded, so this section cites chapter · item throughout. The *Yǎnxí Bù* answers the question “**what happens to a script after it is handed over**”; we take only the part that is eaten away when a script is executed.

### 4.1 Selecting Plays, First

|Item|Li Yu's original words (Selecting Plays, First; page: not in the notes)|What it means for playwrights today|
|-|-|-|
|General discussion|“**I fear that if the script is poor, the patron's heart's blood and the singers' spirit are all spent in vain**”|The first responsibility of the rehearsal layer still lies with the script|
|○ Distinguishing old and new|“When master actors teach songs, **they usually polish the old and hurry through the new**… a new script… **all its clumsiness can be hidden**”|**New scripts are naturally cold-shouldered by those who execute them**: when handing in a script, attach a separate list of new tunes, new blocking, and new conventions|
|○ Tempering cold and hot|“**I say chuanqi has no cold or hot; the only fear is that it does not fit human feeling**… **even if the drum and clapper stop and the stage falls silent, the audience's shouts of admiration can shake heaven and earth**”|**The criterion is fitness to human feeling, not bustle. Self-check**: With the percussion and fight scenes removed, can it still “make people weep and make people laugh”? A cold scene is not unperformable; it has simply **not been polished**|

**Historical limits**: the household-troupe (家班, jiābān) procedure in which “retainers and master actors decide together” on a repertory (“one must choose someone with some literacy to read the new lyrics”), the attribution of performance standards entirely to the audience (“if viewers demand refinement, performers dare not practice carelessly”), and the rule that training must “begin with the old texts” — none of these three holds today.

### 4.2 Changing the Tune, Second

|Item|Li Yu's original words (Changing the Tune, Second; page: not in the notes)|What it means for playwrights today|
|-|-|-|
|General discussion|“**Change, and it is new; do not change, and it rots. Change, and it lives; do not change, and it stiffens**”; “**The value lies in resembling the general design; in the fine turns and details, addition and subtraction are entirely permissible**”|Revivals must change too; not to change is to rot|
|○ Shortening the long to the short|“**Better short with an ending than long without one**”; “**Take the several acts whose plot can be spared and mark them with a private sign**”; **the method of adding within deletion**: “add a few lines to the act after the cut… or prepare a lead-in in the preceding act… **a few lines can stand in for a whole act**”|**When handing in a script, include a long-or-short cut plan**: mark the scenes to be cut in advance; when cutting, have the preceding act “prepare a lead-in” and the following act “add a few lines”|
|○ Turning the old into the new|“**Keep its body, change its bearing**… **What is the body? The aria texts and the major plot junctures. What is the bearing? The comic business and the fine details of spoken dialogue.**” For filling gaps: “**There is already a character in the story who could be used but is not**”; “to conjure a new character out of thin air… **still feels like breaking bones and moving muscles**”|**The first boundary table for revising old plays** (see §5.2), on the grounds that “**times have moved, and human hearts are not what they were.**” **Self-check**: Can the newly added character be replaced by an existing supporting character? If so, do not add one|

**Historical limits**: the original reason for shortening (guests at a private banquet, 堂会, tánghuì, leaving early) and the rule that “**the business of watching a play should be dim, not bright**” (candlelight) no longer hold; his motive for supplementing 《琵琶记》 (*The Story of the Lute*) was the cult of chastity — **take the method from all three, not the motive.**

### 4.3 Teaching the Song, Third

|Item|Li Yu's original words (Teaching the Song, Third; page: not in the notes)|What it means for playwrights today|
|-|-|-|
|○ Explaining the meaning of the song|“**Not knowing what this song speaks of or whom it points to, they sing with the mouth but not with the heart**”; “**To turn dead sound into a living song… depends only on the two words ‘understand.’**”|**If the singer needs an explanation to understand the aria, it is written too deep. Self-check**: Read it to someone unfamiliar with the plot; can they answer “who is this about, and what is happening?”|
|○ Tuning and mastering pronunciation|“**The head, tail, and lingering sound of a syllable are all designed for slow songs… in fast-tempo songs there is only the main sound; head and tail do not apply**”|**Words in mànbǎn passages must withstand being divided into head, belly, and tail and prolonged**; avoid muddy endings and strings of closed-mouth sounds; fast passages may be dense, but every word must be independently distinguishable|
|○ Avoid indistinct words|“When a song is finished and **not one word can be made out**, it is suffocating”|Avoid strings of words with the same initial or the same final in aria lyrics|
|○ Strictly distinguish separate and unison singing|“**Songs for the whole stage must be sung by the whole stage; songs for one voice must remain for one voice. The meaning of the words is clear, and this must not be violated**”; the exception is “**only in plays of travel, setting out on campaign, and the like… may all voices be joined**”|**Scenes in which the characters have different things on their minds must be marked in the script as separate singing**, or rehearsal will merge them. **Self-check** (the test in 《赏月》 “Enjoying the Moon”): Can everyone onstage claim these lines as their own? If not, they must not be sung together|
|○ Percussion must not be cluttered|“**To interrupt an aria may still be forgiven; to blot out bīnbái is intolerable in reason and feeling**”; before an exit, “only a few lines of bīnbái remain unfinished… **to make speaking and not speaking the same is deeply hateful**”|**Do not put key information in the last few lines of dialogue before an exit** — the percussion will eat it. This is the chapter's hardest rule for structure|
|○ The accompaniment should be low|“**The voice must be principal, and strings and bamboo subordinate**”; his own failure condition: “**This method should not be applied universally; it depends on the ability of the singer**”|Song is host, accompaniment is guest; today sound design and orchestration carry this|

**Historical limits**: “the flute and bamboo should sound one note below the song” now belongs to sound design and orchestration (he supplied the failure condition himself); “the one who selects the material bears the blame” carries the color of the household troupe's buying and owning of performers (take only “first clean the teeth and mouth, then master the tune and beat”); and “explanation… has never been the custom” no longer holds (today's disease is explanation reduced to thematic summary).

### 4.4 Teaching Spoken Dialogue, Fourth

|Item|Li Yu's original words (Teaching Spoken Dialogue, Fourth; page: not in the notes)|What it means for playwrights today|
|-|-|-|
|General reversal|“**Singing is difficult but easy; speaking is easy but difficult**”; bīnbái “**has no tune or beat to follow and no score to consult; it relies solely on the singing master's oral teaching**”; “**Good singers are two or three in ten; masters of spoken dialogue are one or two in a hundred**”|**Bīnbái is harder to write than song**: the bīnbái in the script must carry its own weight and rhythm; do not count on rehearsal to supply it|
|=== High and low, rising and falling ===|“**Each passage has its host and guest; each line has its host and guest. The host is high and rising; the guest is low and subdued**” (in 《琵琶·分别》 (“Parting” from *The Story of the Lute*), the first two lines are guest and the last two host; the word “之” (zhī) is **a filler within a filler**); for 上场诗 (shàngchǎngshī — entrance poems), “**three lines are high and slow; one line should be low and quick… as a rule, the third line**”; avoid “**flat, level delivery**”|**When writing 定场白 (dìngchǎngbái — entrance prose that sets the scene) in four-six parallel prose, put the “host” in the last two lines**; make the third line of an entrance poem something that can be “lightly passed over,” and the fourth line something that can bear “high and slow”|
|=== Pace and pause ===|“**Not to break where one should break, but to break suddenly where one should not**”; his one rule: “**When two or three lines speak of only one matter, they should be rushed through in one breath; when one line finishes one matter and the next turns to another… then break slightly**”|**The unit of bīnbái punctuation is the “matter,” not the “sentence”** — divide by matter; punctuation follows meaning|

**Historical limits**: the conclusion compares actors to “**choice objects of the playhouse**” (the objectification of performers is Li Yu's constant perspective; the “Section on Voice and Appearance” opens with “selecting postures”). **This layer is unacceptable**; take only “break and join by units of meaning, not by punctuation.” The prompt-book system of “one dot per line” no longer exists either.

### 4.5 Escaping Convention, Fifth

|Item|Li Yu's original words (Escaping Convention, Fifth, after the Guoxue Dianji website; page: not in the notes)|What it means for playwrights today|
|-|-|-|
|General discussion|“**The whole art of the playhouse lies in producing the extraordinary and changing appearances, so that people cannot predict it. If everyone does the same… then before it is performed I already know it; the sorrowful no longer feels sorrowful, the bitter no longer feels bitter**”|The harm of a formula is not that it is unattractive; it is that **foreknowledge kills feeling**|
|○ Bad habits of costume|The blue and black robes distinguishing gentleman from petty man: “**this is the first playhouse abuse that should be reformed**”; dance costumes stiff as paper armor: “**what business have they on the singing stage and dance floor?**”; “**The fluttering cap is elegant and romantic; the square cap is mature and steady — using them to distinguish young from old may be called appropriate.**”|**Once the correspondence between role type and costume color hardens into a sign of good and evil, it should be reformed** (脸谱, liǎnpǔ — face patterns; 行头, xíngtou — stage costumes and properties, likewise), the same principle as §1.4's “handsome makeup does not equal celebration”|
|○ Bad habits of sound|“If the person is from Wu, they use the Wu accent — **this is taking the person as the starting point**”; “if the place of performance is Wu, they use the Wu accent — **this is taking the place as the starting point**”; the pathology: “every painted-face role uses the Wu accent”|Criterion: **dialect must start from the person or from the place; it must not be the default skin of a role type**|
|○ Bad habits of language|**“Ya!”** = “a cry of shock” (pathology: used for both the expected and the unexpected); **“Wait a moment”** = “the heart consulting itself” (pathology: said dozens of times in one speech); **a tail added to a tail**: “**A tail must not be added to a tail, just as a head must not be added to a head**”; the exception is **吊场 (diàochǎng — a short retention scene that holds the stage after the main action)**: “**yet only when absolutely unavoidable**… **it is a dragon's foot, not a snake's foot**”|① **Exit discipline**: when the 下场诗 (xiàchǎngshī — exit poem) is finished, exit; no added words. Only diàochǎng is an exception, and not in every act. **Self-check** (Li Yu's two questions): If these lines were removed, would an extra act appear, or would earlier words be buried? If neither, cut them. ② **Oral formulas must not be used as mere tone particles; self-check**: count the “ya” and “wait a moment,” and see whether any are used for “the expected”|
|○ Bad habits of comic business|“**The beaten man must escape, and the peacemaker is struck by mistake**” (Dong Zhuo, 董卓, in 《掷戟》 *Throwing the Halberd*); the jealous page boy “**must turn his backside toward the audience after speaking**” (Jin'an, 进安, in 《玉簪》 *The Jade Hairpin*; Qin Tong (琴童 — the lute boy) in 《西厢》 *The Western Chamber*); “**a play within a play is thoroughly tiresome**” (the wine seller in 《旷野奇逢》 *A Strange Encounter in the Wilderness*)|Use these directly as examples of **forbidden formulas**; comic business needs a trigger in the scene|

**Historical limits**: the three costume pathologies belong to the costume system of Ming–Qing Kunqu troupes; today only one layer remains — “costume color must not carry the verdict of good and evil.” “All painted-face roles speak in the official pronunciation” **does not hold in dialect-based genres** (local opera lives precisely by its dialect), and he himself admits, “to reform them all would be more than can be reformed… I have merely given a few examples.” The list was incomplete from the start.

## §5 The Lower Limit of Adaptation

Source: luo-v6 (scanned page; printed page = scanned page − 6). The two core statements are in luo-v6/03, 7.6 (p0110–0111) and 8.6, Case Three (p0123–0124).

### 5.1 The General Criterion: Basic Material Unchanged, Basic Form Unchanged

**Original words** (p0110): “Tradition is still tradition; the most basic things have not changed: **first, the basic material has not changed; second, the basic form has not changed, but it has been upgraded.**”

Three metaphors (p0110–0111): **雪里蕻 (xuělǐhóng — pickled potherb mustard)** (“cured in an old crock: there was still a little mold inside… today it is more hygienic”); **窝窝头 (wōwotou — steamed cornmeal bun)** (“in the past… at least ten times the size, with grandmother's fingerprints on it, but we cannot say it is no longer a wōwotou”); **旗袍 (qípáo — Chinese dress)** (the slit went from “to the knee” to “to the waist,” but “**the line has not changed, the material has not changed**… **grandmother's qípáo was also the Republic-era innovation upon the Qing**”).

**Applied to a genre**: material = vocal material, dialect, and conventional pieces; form = the skeleton of genre. **If both are present, it is still this genre.**
**Failure condition (supplied by Luo himself)**: this criterion decides only “whether it still counts as this genre,” **not whether it is good or bad** — an adaptation that preserves material and form can be a bad play, and a work that crosses the lower limit can be a good work; it simply should no longer bear the name of this genre.

### 5.2 What May and May Not Be Changed

**Original words** (p0123–0124; four versions of 《西施归越》 *Xi Shi Returns to Yue*: Guo Xiaozhuang (郭小庄), Taipei Yayin Xiaojí, 1993 / Xue Mu (薛沐), Jiangsu Provincial Peking Opera Company, 1995 / Shanghai Yue Opera Company / Shanxi Pu Opera Company): “I revised the script in all four versions, but one thing did not change: my **theme, framework, principal character relationships, and basic dramatic plot** — these do not change. **Some rhymes may change, scenes may be added or removed, but the literary spirit is unshakable.**”

### 5.3 What It Is Called Once the Lower Limit Is Crossed

The two lower limits are two parallel gates; cross either one and it is no longer called an “adaptation”:

1. **Cross 5.1 (material or form replaced)** — it is no longer a work of this genre. Luo's criterion governs genre membership, not quality, so the correct handling is not to reject it but to **rename it**: stop flying the genre's banner, and do not apply for publicity as “an innovation in such-and-such opera.”
2. **Cross 5.2 (any one of theme / framework / principal character relationships / basic dramatic plot replaced)** — it is no longer an adaptation of that work but **another play**: change the title, sign your own name, and reduce the original to a “based on” credit. Luo's prior advice (p0121–0122, Chen Xiting's (陈西汀) lawsuit over authorship of 《红楼梦》 *Dream of the Red Chamber*): “**If you do not accept the original script, you need not start from this script at all; find another that suits you better.**”

**The relation between the two gates**: 5.1 decides “is it still this genre”; 5.2 decides “is it still this play.” Neither entails the other — **a change of genre can still be an adaptation of the same play (cross-genre transplantation), and preserving the genre may already mean it is another play.**

**Three final self-check questions**: ① Are the vocal material and the skeleton of genre still present? ② Are theme, framework, principal character relationships, and basic dramatic plot each still the same sentence as before? ③ If any item in ② answers “no,” am I ready to sign a new title under my own name?

## §6 Which Period Does “Tradition” Refer To?

Source: luo-v6/02 4.1 (p0050, p0053–0054, essay 4, 《在创新中继承》 (“Inheriting through Innovation”), Zhengzhou, 2006-11-21).

### 6.1 Defining the Period (Original Words)

> (p0053–0054) “By **京剧 (Peking opera) tradition** we mean **the Peking opera of the 1920s–1950s, the period represented by Mei Lanfang (梅兰芳)**. **It does not refer to the three great laoshēng actors before him, nor to the Thirteen Masters of Tongzhi and Guangxu, still less to the Anhui opera that preceded it.**”
> “By **the tradition of local opera** we likewise mean… **the generation of artists who became famous around the 1950s**… This tradition certainly does not mean Yuan zaju, nor Ming–Qing chuanqi.”

Overall judgment (p0053): “**Tradition must be seen as dynamic and fluid; it must not be seen as something frozen at one stage.**” Metaphor (p0050): “Opera is not a leaf; you cannot assume it is still green now because it was green then.”

### 6.2 Why It Matters: It Decides Where “Returning to Tradition” Returns To

A reply to an open letter from a hundred scholars in Hong Kong and Taiwan opposing the Third China Kunqu Festival (p0052–0053): “**Not one of you has ever seen Kunqu at its source**… **You merely want Kunqu to return to some stretch of the stream, never to the source.**” The counterproof follows in the same place: “If you want Kunqu to go back to that place to perform, you should not go into a theater.”

**Consequences for the playwright**: ① In script discussions, “guarding tradition” and “returning to tradition” almost always mean **a particular stretch of the stream**; unless it is identified, the discussion cannot converge — one party means the performance text of the 1950s, the other means Ming–Qing chuanqi. ② **It is also a move in choosing material**: 《徽州女人》 (*Huizhou Women*) “actually returned to a tradition older than this tradition” (p0056); before writing Yue opera (越剧), Luo checks “the layer earlier than the current impression” (p0015–0016, the ancient Yue kingdom of “cut hair and tattooed bodies, bare feet and swift strides,” not the soft beauty of Jiangnan). ③ **Counterexamples**: “youth Kunqu” (p0109) — “**its substance is teenage life transmitting the decrepit breath of late-Ming literati, scholars, and loyalists**”; “pseudo-return” (p0173–0174) — “**it takes the benefits of modernity, then dresses up as contempt for modernity and closeness to so-called tradition in order to swindle the audience's emotions.**”

### 6.3 The Playwright's Self-Check Questions

1. In this project, which **decade** does “tradition” specifically mean? If you cannot name a decade, you cannot name a tradition.
2. Is this stretch the **source** or a **stream**? (The same question as the base-text entry in §5.2.)
3. Does the stretch you want to return to **still have living practitioners** (performers, musicians, recordings)? If not, it is not a return but a reconstruction; reconstruction must take its own creative responsibility and cannot borrow the name of “tradition.”
4. If you return to a layer **earlier** than the current impression (the *Huizhou Women* model), are this genre's audience and company ready? This is a risk in choosing material, not a matter of right or wrong.
5. Who said “tradition” in this discussion? **First put on the table the stretch each party means, then talk about whether to change anything.**

## §7 The Law of Alternation in 排场 (Páichǎng — the Overall Arrangement of Scenes and Action) (Common to Both Systems)

Source: wang-jilie/02 4.9 (volume 2, p0169); the short transitional scene is in 4.5 of the same notes (volume 2, p0166). **Note**: the quotations in those notes are in traditional characters; here they are converted to simplified characters without changing wording.

### 7.1 Original Text

> (volume 2, p0169) “Anyone writing chuanqi must arrange the distribution of roles in advance, as the person we today call the **stage director** does. **If the first act (折, zhé) is sung by a shēng and the second by a dàn, the third must use a broad-voice role (阔口, kuòkǒu — an open, forceful voice, typically a jìng or lǎoshēng) or a lively ensemble play; if two or three acts of slow arias and long suites have passed, a short transitional scene or a comic scene performed by chǒu or jìng must be interspersed.**”

Criterion (same page, p0169, using the fifty acts of 《长生殿》 *The Palace of Eternal Life* as the sample): “**Not only are the qupai not repeated throughout; the mode of one act does not repeat the mode of the previous act, and the principal role of one act never repeats the principal role of the previous act.**” He explains why he concentrates on páichǎng (volume 2, p0166–0167): “**Without attention to páichǎng, a play cannot be performed on the carpet stage (氍毹, qúshū)**… **Therefore this collection discusses páichǎng in particular detail.**”

### 7.2 How the Qupai System Uses It

In the qupai system there are **three non-repetitions**: the qupai, the mode of two adjacent acts, and the principal singing role of two adjacent acts. This works together with the classification of 曲情 (qǔqíng — the emotional mood of a qupai) — Southern qupai suites are divided by mood into six categories (joy / excursion / grief / hidden resentment / action / expression of feeling), with four further categories outside the six (general use / martial-play use / short transitional scene use / quiet short scene use; volume 2, p0162–0164). Accordingly, “the third act must use a broad-voice role or a lively ensemble play” means in practice: **change the mood category, change the suite, change the role bracket**. The rule of role assignment (volume 2, p0166): “The category of expressing feeling… consists mostly of long suites and long arias; **the principal act should use this kind of suite**, and it suits a shēng (xiǎoshēng, young male) or dàn”; “the joyful category suits unison singing”; “when a shēng, jìng, or lǎoshēng meets a tragedy, it is best to use Northern qupai” — “**broad-voice roles sing mostly Northern lyrics, only twenty or thirty percent Southern. Southern qupai are soft and lack heroic sound, and so are not suited to the mouth and courage of shēng and jìng.**”

**Practical move**: list every act of the play in four columns — act number / mode / principal singing role / mood category — and read down the rows to see whether any column repeats between adjacent rows. The complete páichǎng table for the fifty acts of *The Palace of Eternal Life* (volume 2, p0170–0173) is a ready-made comparison sample.

### 7.3 How the Banqiang System Uses It

The banqiang system has no modes or suites, so only two of the three repetitions remain — **the principal singing role does not repeat, and the nature of the scene does not repeat**; the third is replaced by the **板式档 (bǎnshì dàng — bǎnshì bracket)**: two adjacent scenes do not use the same bǎnshì bracket (no two consecutive scenes in 慢三眼 (màn sānyǎn — slow three-eye tempo), and no two consecutive scenes in liúshuǐ fast tempo). This dovetails with two points from zhang-guo: the three-way classification of song (p0136, p0283) and the general rule of bǎnshì combination, “from slow to fast… free, slow, medium-fast, free” (p0290).

**Practical move**: list every scene in four columns — scene number / principal role type / bǎnshì bracket (màn sānyǎn / 一眼, yīyǎn — one-eye tempo / liúshuǐ / sǎnbǎn) / nature of scene (civil or martial, cold or hot) — and read down adjacent rows. **The criterion is that role type and bǎnshì bracket do not repeat consecutively, not that scene lengths differ.** One further reinforcement is unique to this system: a change of vocal mode = a major turn of emotion (zhang-guo p0291: “**a change in vocal mode means a change in the drama's emotion and atmosphere**”), and it can be used to satisfy the demand to “change bracket.”

### 7.4 Self-Check by Lining Up Cold and Hot

Lay out all the scenes of the play in one horizontal row, writing two characters for each scene — **the first character of the principal singing role type + cold/hot** (example: shēng-cold | dàn-cold | jìng-hot | chǒu-hot | shēng-hot | dàn-cold). Then look for two things: ① Are there three consecutive cells with the same role-type character? If so, the first half of the alternation law is violated. ② Are there three consecutive cells with the same cold/hot character? If so, the second half is violated.

This row is also **a roster for the actors' physical stamina** — the same law as zhang-guo p0227, “tempering cold and hot… so that **the roles' work and rest are kept in proper measure**”; the two passages point to each other.

### 7.5 The Short Transitional Scene: “Without It the Plot Does Not Run Through and the Threads Do Not Connect”

**Original text** (volume 2, p0166): the short transitional scene (popularly called “过脉戏,” guòmàixì — a “pulse-connecting” scene) “may not have many arias, but **without it the plot does not run through and the threads do not connect; it is absolutely indispensable in chuanqi**. It should use short arias and urgent arias, **and must never use the slow arias of a long suite**.”

- **It is not filler; it is the seam of the threads.** Cutting the transitional scene breaks the line.
- **A hard musical constraint**: short and urgent arias; no slow arias with 赠板 (zèngbǎn — added-beat ornamented passages). The same holds for the banqiang system — transitional scenes use liúshuǐ, fast tempo, or sǎnbǎn, never mànbǎn.
- **Self-check**: Is there a transitional scene between two major scenes? Does the transitional scene mistakenly use mànbǎn or a slow aria with zèngbǎn?
- A related role restriction (volume 2, p0148, p0166): when jìng and chǒu enter, they do not use a yǐnzi but short, coarse arias, mostly 干唱干念 (gān chàng gān niàn — dry singing and reciting without accompaniment) — **writing a long slow suite for chǒu or jìng = writing the wrong role type**.

### 7.6 Failure Conditions

1. **When a short play has too few acts, only the baseline is kept**: within ten acts, the three non-repetitions cannot be achieved; keep only “no two consecutive acts with the same principal singer.”
2. **This law is a method of arrangement, not of evaluation.** Wang Jilie writes: “**If the author reads many chuanqi, he can naturally match them at will and vary them as his heart desires.**” (volume 2, p0164) — the table is an entrance, not a cage.
3. **The rule that shēng and dàn must appear in the second and third acts has a public exception** (volume 2, p0167): 《邯郸梦》 (*The Handan Dream*) is led by a lǎoshēng, and 《钧天乐》 (*The Music of Heaven*) by a lǎoshēng and a xiǎoshēng — “**If an author can invent from his own heart and capture a singular realm, he need not cling to the established methods of the ancients.**” The alternation law enjoys the same exemption.
4. **Modern plays and one-person plays do not use the first half**: with only one or two principal singing roles there is no room to “change to a broad-voice role”; keep only the alternation of cold/hot and of bǎnshì brackets.

## §8 Two Common Interfaces

### 8.1 The Address Interface: From Speech to Song, Name the Person Before Singing

**Rule**: put an address term or a short line of spoken dialogue in the line before each aria as an interface — **name the person first, then begin singing.**
**Why it is marked “common to both systems”**: the rule appears independently in unrelated materials (different vocal systems, different eras); it was not generalized from one source. **Gu's own closing sentence** (p0403): the two are “completely consistent on the important principles of dramatic language; the difference lies in the form of language” — that is, **common principles, mutually exclusive conventions**. That is how to use this table: to judge “whether this general dramaturgical rule can be transplanted,” not to judge superiority.
