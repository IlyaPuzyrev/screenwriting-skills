# Ozu Screenplay Style (小津安二郎剧本写法)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

This skill distills its method from a scene-by-scene breakdown of six screenplays in 《小津安二郎剧本集》 (The Ozu Yasujiro Screenplay Collection — screenplays by Noda Kogo and Ozu Yasujiro, translated into Chinese by 吴菲 [Wu Fei]): 《晚春》 (Late Spring, 1949, 103 scenes), 《麦秋》 (Early Summer, 1951, 145 scenes), 《东京物语》 (Tokyo Story, 1953, 176 scenes), 《浮草》 (Floating Weeds, 1959, 121 scenes), 《秋日和》 (Late Autumn, 1960, 110 scenes), and 《秋刀鱼之味》 (An Autumn Afternoon, 1962, 98 scenes).

Core creed: **every major event happens off-screen; the script writes only the domestic routine before and after the event.** What the audience sees is not the event but the ripples it raises in human relationships.

The scene-by-scene structural tables and original-text examples for each screenplay are in [reference.md](reference.md) in this directory.

## 0. Medium Boundaries: Which Layers Travel and Which Do Not

This is a method for **Japanese cinema**, and the format section describes the **Japanese script format**. Choose by layer:

| Layer | Does it travel? |
|---|---|
| **The structural skeleton and the three templates — marrying off a daughter / bereavement / secret** | **Yes.** This is the skill's main value. |
| **The fifteen techniques** (events off-screen, minimal-syllable acknowledgment, the go-between relays, the echoing object in three escalating appearances, supporting characters speak for the protagonist, the diminishing ending) | **Yes.** They can be used directly in film and series. Two of them come with capacity preconditions; read those before moving them into a series: Section II, item 1 (events off-screen) and item 14 (the single monologue). |
| **Dialogue technique** (the politeness gap, repeated greetings, no explanation) | **Yes.** |
| **Format spec (Section 8)** | **No.** It is the Japanese 柱 (hashira — scene heading) / ト書き (tosho — action line) format (`○place (time)`, ト書き indented three full-width spaces, `CHARACTER NAME「dialogue」`). For a Chinese-language script use 场号制 (chǎnghào zhì — the Chinese scene-numbering system); for Hollywood style use Fountain; see `sw-format-adaptation` Section 2.2. |
| **Scene length of 1–1.5 pages** | Use with caution. It rests on Ozu's rhythm and the Japanese page layout; recalculate for a Chinese script using the conversion chain in `sw-format-adaptation`; do not apply the page count directly. |

**Take the patterns and techniques; do not take the format or the page counts.**

## I. The Shared Skeleton of All Six: One Member of the Family Leaves

| Film | Core event | Driver | Lie/scheme | Absent suitor | Closing pillow shot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Late Spring | Father marries off daughter | Aunt | Father lies that he will remarry | The prospective match, Satake | Apple peel breaks → night sea |
| Early Summer | Family marries off daughter | Director + whole family | None (daughter decides for herself) | The proposed match, Manabe (face unclear in the photo) | Wheat field + another family's wedding procession |
| Tokyo Story | Parents visit their children → mother dies | None (time) | None | — | Empty platform at dawn → motorboat pulls away |
| Floating Weeds | Father sees his illegitimate son | Mistress | The "uncle" identity; bribes a seduction | — | Night train |
| Late Autumn | Mother marries off daughter | Three old classmates | Friends invent a "remarriage" for the mother; whether the mother's "I don't want to remarry" is true cannot be decided | Goto (appears but functions weakly) | Folds a haori (a Japanese formal jacket) → empty corridor |
| An Autumn Afternoon | Father marries off daughter | Old classmate + mirror character | Two pranks (fake death, fake matchmaking) | The prospective match ("very tall") | Full-length mirror in the dark |

Four are "marrying off a daughter," one is "losing a wife," one is "acknowledging a son." **The real event is always "one member of the family leaves"**; the script writes the domestic routine before the departure and the emptiness after it.

To write an Ozu-style script, answer three questions first:
1. Who is leaving this family? (the one who leaves)
2. Who is left behind? (the one who stays = the protagonist)
3. Who is pushing this to happen? (the driver — usually with a selfish motive)

## II. Fifteen Techniques Repeated Across the Films (ordered by reusability)

### 1. Events Off-Screen, Reactions On-Screen
- Weddings, matchmaking meetings, deaths, proposals, and confessions are never shown. Late Spring scene 74 is simply "the matchmaking was a week ago"; the wedding shows only "Dad..." before the mirror as she leaves and, after the return, the bar and the empty room.
- Tokyo Story, the mother's death: scene 143 the father keeps vigil → scene 144 "Dawn: an empty platform — a street with no pedestrians — small waves lapping the stone seawall" → scene 145 "a white cloth is laid over Tomi's face." No death rattle, no wailing.
- Early Summer has no proposal scene at all; Noriko gives her answer to Kenkichi's mother. Kenkichi himself comes home drunk and, told "she's willing to come," says only "Come where—" "To do what—."
- **Directive: list the three biggest events in the script and move them all off-screen; write only the scene before and the scene after each one. Let a group of unpeopled landscape shots carry the event itself.**
- **Capacity precondition:** this method requires **at least 100 scenes** in the carrying unit (Ozu's six films run from 103 to 176), so that the white space has somewhere to spread. A 25–35-scene episode cannot sustain a whole episode off-screen — it can only be used **locally**: choose one event in the episode to move off-screen, give it one scene before and one after, and write the remaining events on-screen as usual; to use the method across a whole stretch, enlarge the carrying unit to a **season or movement (板块, bǎnkuài — a large structural block, larger than a single episode)** rather than a single episode.

### 2. Lie-Driven Propulsion
- Late Spring: the father lies that he will remarry → the daughter marries; the father never admits the fiction to his daughter on-screen; the truth is brought out by another character (Aya) at the end: "I couldn't tell such a beautiful lie."
- Late Autumn: the three old classmates invent a "remarriage" for the mother → the daughter accuses her innocent mother of being "dirty" → on the last night the mother says, "I still want to be alone... Don't think I lied just to marry you off" — it can be read as a lie or as the truth, and the character herself denies that it was a lie.
- Floating Weeds: the identity lie of "Uncle"; Sumiko spends a thousand yen to hire Kayo to seduce Kiyoshi.
- An Autumn Afternoon: two pranks (Kawai invents that Horie has "died"; Horie invents that he "already had a matchmaking meeting yesterday") rehearse the thing the protagonist fears most, and both are jokes by the people closest to him.
- **Directive: let the driver tell a well-meaning lie; let a bystander expose the truth, or never expose it; write the parent's final statement so that truth and falsehood cannot be separated.**

### 3. Minimal-Syllable Acknowledgment
- Late Spring scene 71: three "Mm"s complete the entire turn (see reference example A): "Are you remarrying?" "Mm..." "You will remarry, won't you?" "Mm." "To the person from today?" "Mm."
- Early Summer scene 119, the family meeting: "Definitely not?" "No." "Really not?" "......" "Definitely not?" "No." Then the parents: "Mmm... mmm... mmm... let's sleep..."
- **Directive: the key admission or refusal gets a single syllable, and the other person asks three times; write silence "......" as a full line of dialogue.**

### 4. The Go-Between Relays
- The aunt relays messages up and down the stairs, and Noriko's "Mm" is translated by the aunt into "She says she's willing to marry!" (Late Spring 77–80); Koichi hides behind the paper door, pops his head out three times, and coaches his sister-in-law to "ask more cleverly" (Early Summer 61); Shige "outsources" her parents to Noriko by telephone (Tokyo Story 58); the trio sits around the table to deliberate (Late Autumn); the father sends his son to ask Miura (An Autumn Afternoon 64–71).
- **Directive: always keep a person between the protagonist and the suitor; the authority figure's instructions must pass through a go-between, and the relay itself is comedy.**

### 5. The Absent Suitor
- Satake exists only in three jokes — "looks like Gary Cooper / like the meter reader / like Kumataro"; Manabe exists only in a golf photograph where "his face is completely unclear," a Who's Who entry, and hearsay; Okazaki's second son is only "very tall."
- **Directive: give no face to the person who is chosen or rejected; give only three descriptions from other people. The audience does not need to know whom the protagonist rejected — the wrong option is just a set of information.**

### 6. Pillow Shots as Scene Breaks
- Between every emotional passage there is a 1–2-line landscape scene: "The sunlight lingers — a bush warbler calls"; empty theater seats → Noriko walking alone in Marunouchi (Late Spring 40–42); carp streamers; a balloon with a broken string; a wheat field; the night sea; a train.
- **Directive: write transitional pillow shots as independent scene numbers, one line of description, no emotional adjectives, no explanation.**

### 7. Echoing Objects and Catchphrases
- "Dirty" (Late Spring 25→26→85→88→90, five times: from joke to guilt); "the lotus-root pickle that stays connected by its fibers" (藕断丝连, ǒu duàn sī lián — "the root breaks but the fibers hold") twice, the second time with its meaning reversed; the pipe (Late Autumn four times: Akiko gives it to Mamiya → Ayako delivers it to the director's office → Taguchi gets one too → at the end Hirayama says "You're lucky, you all got pipes"); the Warship March (An Autumn Afternoon three times: Sakamoto salutes and circles → Hirayama declines → on the wedding night he listens voluntarily, a drunk customer says "We lost the war" → at the end he hums it alone); "lucky" (Tokyo Story three times: the neighbor's envy → Numata's irony → the old couple's self-consolation, devalued each time); "a quilt over the grave" (Keizo first says it as a joke, later as regret); the matchsticks (Floating Weeds, two struck at the end).
- **Directive: choose one small object or phrase and arrange three appearances, each context pushing the meaning one step further (joke → wound → reconciliation, or envy → irony → self-mockery). A dead person's belongings can circulate among the supporting characters; at the end, count "who didn't get one."**

### 8. Supporting Characters Speak for the Protagonist
- In Tokyo Story the old people never complain themselves: Atsushi rages that his desk has been moved, Kyoko says "They're too selfish," Numata says "They treat me as a burden"; the old man's heaviest comment on his own situation is said with a smile: "I've become homeless after all."
- Late Autumn: Yuriko storms into the director's office and puts the three old men on trial: "Why throw stones into a calm pond just to stir up trouble?"
- An Autumn Afternoon: the daughter turns it back on him: "Aren't you thinking the same, Dad? That it would be better if I just stayed here..." The father can only answer "That's not it."
- **Directive: give the protagonist's grievance to the lowest-status character; let the youngest outsider challenge authority, and turn the challenge into comedy immediately after (drunkenness / sushi restaurant); the protagonist himself only smiles or looks at the sky.**

### 9. The Truth Is Told Only to Outsiders
- Shukichi to his old friend Numata: "This is the selfishness of parents everywhere; selfishness has no end"; Tomi to her daughter-in-law Noriko: "If you find someone suitable, you should marry him"; Shukichi at the end to Noriko: "Compared with my own children, it's you, who should count as an outsider..."; Akiko to Yuriko; Komajuro to Oyoshi.
- **Directive: put the most private lines in relationships outside blood; give the "theme debate" to two young characters (Kyoko / Noriko), and give the "emotional conclusion" to one line of domestic talk ("If I'd known this would happen, I should have treated her better while she was alive").**

### 10. Mirror Characters
- Onodera's remarriage (Late Spring) → the father's "remarriage"; Numata's son "calls a section chief a department head" (Tokyo Story) → Shukichi's disappointment; the Hyotan father and daughter (72/48, noodle shop) → the Hirayamas (57/24) thirty years later; Horie's 28-year-old second wife → the comic version of remarriage.
- An Autumn Afternoon: Hirayama sees the Hyotan father and daughter (26–27) → hears Kawai say "If you're not careful, you'll end up like that too" (30) → hears Hyotan confess "For convenience I kept my daughter at home to serve me... I was wrong" (57) → that night at home he asks "Don't you want to marry?" (60). The decision is never delivered through the protagonist's own psychological monologue.
- **Directive: give the protagonist someone who has already lived through the protagonist's future, and let the protagonist decide that very night after seeing him. Let a minor character "rehearse" the theme first (Noriko first says Onodera's remarriage is "dirty"), then transfer the same situation to the protagonist.**

### 11. The Children's Subplot as Metaphor
- Early Summer: Minoru wants a train track; his father brings home bread → he kicks the bread → gets spanked → runs away; meanwhile the whole family is choosing a "good match" for Noriko — the adults give bread, the child wants tracks.
- Tokyo Story: Atsushi rages that his desk has been moved into the corridor; Late Spring: the baseball bat painted red.
- **Directive: let a child rage over a small object; that object is the metaphor for the main line; the two lines need not refer to each other in dialogue.**

### 12. Ensemble Catchphrase Relay
- Early Summer scene 40: the three women's "Right?" "You think so, right?" "Right?" in a parallel tongue-twister, married faction vs. unmarried faction; Late Autumn scene 65: the trio's "—Well, no hurry..." "Mm, no hurry..."
- **Directive: in an ensemble scene, pass one short phrase among three people; use repetition to create sides and rhythm, not content.**

### 13. Displaced Quarrel
- Late Spring scene 68, the cream cake argument: "Give it to me to eat!" "I just don't want to!" "I tell you, eat it! Even if you don't want to, I'll make you eat it!" "I just won't!" — from beginning to end they argue about "whether to eat the cake," but the real subject is "whether to marry."
- Early Summer: Takako and her husband fight over carrots and a pipe; An Autumn Afternoon: Koichi and his wife over a golf club and a white handbag (45–51).
- **Directive: characters never quarrel over the real problem; they quarrel over a small object; all lines are short repeated phrases, expressing emotion through rhythm rather than content. Use a young couple's fight over an object to show the audience "what marriage is like," replacing talk about marriage.**

### 14. The Single-Monologue Rule
- Each film has only one long speech, placed in the penultimate passage: Shukichi on happiness (Late Spring 93, about 300 characters), Akiko's last night (Late Autumn 101), Hyotan's confession (An Autumn Afternoon 57), Shukichi on selfishness (Tokyo Story 101). All the earlier "Mm"s and "......"s are storing energy for it.
- **Directive: the entire script may contain only one speech longer than 150 characters, placed at the final outlet of emotion.**
- **Series conversion:** "one film" becomes a **movement (板块, bǎnkuài)** in a series: each movement gets only one such speech, placed in its penultimate episode or the penultimate passage of that episode; counting by episode would produce a dozen long speeches in a season and lose all the accumulated force. For how to divide movements, see `sw-series-engine-bible` item 19.

### 15. Diminishing Ending
- Late Spring: bar → home → pillow shot; An Autumn Afternoon: friend's house → bar → home → full-length mirror in the dark; Late Autumn: the trio's restaurant (laughter) → apartment (folding a haori) → empty corridor.
- **Directive: after the climax, diminish in the order "social space → semi-private space → home → unpeopled space," with the last scene unpeopled. Give the comic line and the lonely line one scene each; laugh first, then emptiness.**

## III. Character Configuration Template

| Position | Function | Examples across the films |
|---|---|---|
| The one who stays | The parent left behind, the protagonist; emotionally passive, enduring | Shukichi ×3, Akiko, Komajuro, Hirayama |
| The one who leaves | Daughter / son / deceased wife; the decision comes in an instant, and the reason is given only as a slice-of-life metaphor | Noriko ×3, Ayako, Michiko, Kiyoshi, Tomi |
| The driver | Matches, urges, pressures; has a selfish motive; botches the job | Aunt Masako, Director Satake, the trio, Sumiko |
| The foil | The leaver's same-age friend, holds the opposite position (divorced / unmarried faction / sushi restaurant daughter) | Aya, Yuriko, Takako |
| The mirror | The protagonist's future | Onodera, Numata, Hyotan, Horie |
| The go-between | Messenger | Sister-in-law Fumiko, son Koichi, daughter-in-law Akiko, the aunt |
| The absent one | The dead / missing person, paired with a concrete object | the mother (watch), Shoji (ear of wheat), Shoji (photo/quilt), Shuzo Miwa (pipe), Kimura (the advance scout who never returns) |
| The bystander | The frame at beginning and end | the neighbor ("How lucky you are" / "It's so quiet now"), Shige and her husband, the waiting-room attendant |

Configuration points:
- **Write the driver's motive honestly**: in Late Autumn the three old men's main motive is a secret crush on their late friend's wife; their wives all know and laugh at them ("They use her as a source of amusement").
- **The cruelest character must make sense in every line**: Shige gives 3,000 yen to send them to Atami because "it's cheaper"; "It's best if the mourning clothes we bring turn out to be unnecessary"; she asks for a sash and hemp cloth as soon as the mother dies; "It would have been better if Father had gone first" — she is not a villain, only "busy." The script never judges her; judgment is left to the audience and the younger characters.
- **The moralist betrays in the next scene**: in Floating Weeds, Kichinosuke rails at a companion who wants to steal, "If you forget a kindness, you're worse than a dog," and in the next scene runs off with the troupe leader's wallet. No explanation.
- **The absent person as glue**: Shoji, killed in the war, runs through Early Summer — the credit agency finds that "Shoji and Kenkichi were classmates" → "He won't be coming back" → "There was an ear of wheat tucked in Shoji's letter" → the film's title. Noriko's trust in Kenkichi comes from "He was my brother's friend."
- **The dead need only one living person who "looks a little like" them**: "There's a woman over there who looks like your mother when she was young... When she lowers her head, just a little, right there." No need for a flashback scene.
- **Old forms of address carry the layer of time**: the teachers all have nicknames (Hyotan/Gourd, Lion, Badger, Emperor); Sakamoto calls Hirayama "Captain"; Hirayama calls Hyotan "Teacher." One utterance brings out a relationship decades old.

## IV. The "Marrying Off a Daughter" Master Structure (ready to use)

Synthesized from the order of passages in Late Spring, Early Summer, Late Autumn, and An Autumn Afternoon:

| Passage | Content | Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Prologue | Idle talk at the station / over breakfast / on the death anniversary; a pillow shot establishes the season | Establish the whole family through a bystander or a meal; the marriage topic appears in the first scene (An Autumn Afternoon opens with a clerk "collecting contributions") |
| Set-up | The everyday ease between father and daughter (mother and daughter) | "More tea, more tea" dependence; the surface of the dialogue is "small talk" (asking directions, mahjong, spelling) |
| False lead | Let the audience think someone (Hattori) is the match | Use comic rhythm at the reversal (Noriko stifles a laugh and asks for tea) |
| First push | The driver proposes; the daughter refuses, saying she must care for her father/mother | The proposed match never appears |
| The mirror enters | The protagonist sees his own future (the Hyotan father and daughter / Onodera's remarriage) | First let the daughter judge the mirror character ("remarriage is dirty") |
| Midpoint | The driver suggests the parent remarry (truth or lie) | The daughter "suddenly darkens" — the real wound has been touched |
| Cold war | Almost no dialogue | "Going shopping..." and she leaves forlornly; empty theater seats |
| Wordless climax | At the Noh theater: a nod / a returned bow / a gaze | Write jealousy through gazing; not one word about the marriage in the whole scene |
| Displaced explosion | Quarrel over a cake / a golf club | Short repeated lines |
| Showdown | Three "Mm"s | The daughter rushes upstairs and cries |
| Decision | The foil urges marriage ("Men are no good... just get married once"); the daughter agrees | The parent asks "You're not marrying because you've given up?" — the decision is reluctant, and the parent knows it |
| Farewell trip | Kyoto / Ikaho / Lake Haruna | The only long monologue goes here |
| Wedding day | One line before the mirror, "Dad..." → "Oh, I understand" → the empty room left with only the mirror and the chair | The wedding = a photograph or a group photo |
| Epilogue | Bar → home → pillow shot | The truth is exposed by a third party; the apple peel breaks; humming the Warship March, "all alone" |

Variants:
- Early Summer puts the "decision" in front of the wrong person (Kenkichi's mother's "It's just my own daydream"), does not show the protagonist's wedding, and closes with a stranger's wedding procession.
- Late Autumn makes the drivers a comic trio with selfish motives, and the accusation lands on the innocent (when the daughter calls her mother "dirty," the mother has no idea — the audience knows the truth first).
- An Autumn Afternoon adds "missing the chance": the man the daughter likes already has someone, and the reason is the father's own hesitation ("Didn't you say at the time that she wouldn't be marrying for a while?") — **the protagonist's hesitation itself becomes the cause of the loss, and a third party points it out in one line.**

## V. The Reusable Skeleton of the "Bereavement" Structure (Tokyo Story)

1. The bystander's frame at the start: "How lucky you are."
2. Arrival: the son's home is not what they imagined — the old man says only "I thought it would be a bit livelier," and does not comment.
3. First set aside: the child's anger expresses it for the old people.
4. "Outsourced": the daughter calls and asks her sister-in-law to take them.
5. The only sincere hospitality uses "borrowed" sake and takeout.
6. What the children take for filial piety = sending the parents away (Atami); the illness is foreshadowed in a single action: "She swayed as if dizzy."
7. Midpoint: the old man's only comment on his situation, said with a smile: "I've become homeless after all."
8. **Two parallel nights**: the father's drinking session and the mother's night talk at her daughter-in-law's are cross-cut — one confesses disappointment while indulging, the other speaks compassion from under the quilt, and the two are not together all night. The audience pieces their words together for them.
9. The death omen is passed over three times: "She swayed," "She's been forgetting things lately," "If anything happens to us, you don't need to rush back" — each time someone interrupts with a laugh.
10. Death happens in a pillow shot.
11. The funeral: mourning and calculation at the same table; a proverb first said as a joke, later as regret.
12. The chain of objects: Shoji's photo → Shoji's quilt → Tomi's watch → Noriko holds the watch to her ear on the train. The last object is "heard" at the end.
13. The ending returns to the neighbor: "It's so quiet now."

## VI. The Secret Structure (Floating Weeds): A Layered Leak Chain

- The audience knows first (scenes 23–27) → Yatakura sees it in the barbershop mirror (45) → Sumiko interrogates (48) → Sensho says "a bond for a lifetime" (50) → Rokuzaburo identifies her through the lattice window of the accompaniment room (57) → Sumiko storms the shop (69) → Kayo is sent (77) → Kiyoshi learns that Kayo was sent (90) → Oyoshi reveals the parentage (115).
- **Each link passes only a little, and always through a "third party"; the protagonist never volunteers it.**
- Information travels through "seeing," not "hearing": mirrors, lattice windows, "(long shot)," "long shot from the inner room" — the script specifies the viewpoint.
- The used person's confession is the turning point (Kayo: "I meant to deceive you at first"), not an exposure.
- **At the moment the truth is revealed there is only rejection** ("I don't want a father! Tell him to get out!"), and reconciliation stays off-screen (Oyoshi: "In her heart she has already forgiven him").
- The protagonist's violence is answered once, at the end, by the person he most wants to protect (his son strikes back, and the parentage is exposed immediately after).
- The big quarrel escalates in three stages — **debt of gratitude → origins → blood** — and the last line must be irretrievable ("My son is not the same breed as you people").
- A line in the play-within-the-play predicts the main plot's ending (Chuji: "setting out on a journey with no destination and no end" → Komajuro boards the night train); let "the people watching the play" also be watched.
- Reconciliation uses a small action that can be repeated twice: the first matchstick is refused, the second is accepted, then "borrow a light" is turned around to light the other's cigarette, and not one word of apology is spoken.

## VII. Dialogue Technique

### 1. The Surface Is "Small Talk"
- Asking directions ("Is the sea this way?" "No, this way"), a mahjong rinshan kaihō (岭上开花 — a win on the replacement tile drawn from the dead wall), spelling out LISZT, "What are they playing this time?"
- Function: establish a relaxed relationship; **let important things be said in the middle of small talk** ("How is Noriko?" "It's about time that girl settled down").
- Directive: hide the key line in the middle of small talk; wrap it front and back with small talk.

### 2. Adverb Bank for Tone (original stage-direction words)
faintly / coldly / vaguely / cheerfully / with a bright smile / forlornly / as if to oneself / in a lowered voice / as if nothing were wrong / with feeling / involuntarily / with growing sharpness / as if doubting her own ears / full of emotion / still murmuring contentedly / suddenly deflated.

### 3. One Catchphrase per Character
Oyoshi: "I'm used to it" / "He'll come back... he's bound to come back"; Komajuro: "Ah, there's no helping this business"; Shukichi: "Mmm..."; Shige: "Honestly, you." Repetition is character.

### 4. Motive Gets Only One Slice-of-Life Metaphor
Noriko explains why she will marry Kenkichi: "It's like when you're cutting cloth and you don't know where you put the scissors, and you look everywhere, and then you find they were right in front of you." Aya presses: "That means you like him!" Noriko insists: "No!... It's that I can trust him." **No psychological explanation.**

### 5. The Theme Line Is Spoken by the Young and Misread in the Elders' Idle Talk
Ayako: "Love and marriage should be considered separately" → the three old men repeat it on the golf course as "That means the world is getting harder and harder."

### 6. The Three Steps of Acceptance
Accept with a smile ("It's all right, Dad... I just don't want to regret it later") → a third party reports crying ("Sister seems to be crying") → the parent goes upstairs and says, "If you don't like it, it's all right to refuse," then goes to the corridor and looks at the sky.

### 7. Ritual Actions Replace Confession
The group photo (Noriko asks to take one of her parents alone; no line says "this is the last time"), the farewell banquet, applying lipstick before the mirror (= saying goodbye to life in Tokyo), folding a haori, peeling an apple.

### 8. The "Reaction Queue" after Major News
Early Summer 118–121: family meeting → the parents cannot manage the stairs → Noriko applies lipstick → Koichi and Fumiko stay where they are; 133–134: the old couple suddenly rise and walk out → Koichi goes to his desk → Fumiko writes in the ashes → Noriko eats ochazuke (rice with tea) in the icy atmosphere. **Each character reacts alone in his or her own space; no group argument is shown.**

## VIII. Script Format Conventions (induced from the original texts)

```
**SCENE NUMBER　LOCATION (　TIME/NOTE)**    ← bold; location first, time optional
One line of landscape or action description— ← a dash at the end = continuation/unfinished
NAME: Dialogue. (action/tone in parentheses) ← action inserted inside the dialogue
NAME: ......                                 ← silence is a full line of dialogue
NAME: (coldly) What?                        ← tone cue in parentheses at the start of the line
Says it and walks out.                       ← action on its own line, subject often omitted
```

- Scenes are extremely short: 1–1.5 pages on average, the shortest a single line ("**22　A poster for the Shun'yō-kai**").
- A single continuous move is split into several scene numbers: entrance → corridor → living room, one scene each (Tokyo Story scenes 30–35 are continuous action inside the same house).
- Time markers appear only at the start of a passage: "The next day," "That night," "Several days later."
- On a character's first appearance, note age and status in parentheses: "Noriko (28, wife of Shukichi's second son Shoji, killed in the war)."
- Stage directions include the director's viewpoint: "(long shot)," "long shot from the inner room," "overhead."
- Scene sample:

## X. Workflow: Writing an Ozu-Style Script

1. **Fix the one who leaves and the one who stays.** Write a one-line story: "X (age, status), in order to make Y leave, does Z; after Y leaves, X is alone..."
2. **Fix the driver and his selfish motive**; give the driver a way of doing things that can go wrong (eating an apple, taking up a pipe, hiding behind the paper door and giving directions).
3. **Fix the mirror character**: who has already lived through the protagonist's future? Arrange the scene in which the protagonist sees him and the decision that night.
4. **Fix the absent person and the object**: the dead / missing person + one concrete object (an ear of wheat, a watch, a pipe, a photograph).
5. **List the three biggest events and move them all off-screen**; for each event write "the scene before" and "the scene after"; use a pillow shot for the event itself.
6. **Choose one echoing object / catchphrase** and arrange three appearances with escalating meaning.
7. **Design a subplot for a child or a young couple** that acts out the main line's lesson with one small object.
8. **Write the showdown scene**: only "Mm" and "......" are allowed; the other person asks three times.
9. **Write the displaced quarrel**: quarrel over a cake / a golf club, short repeated lines.
10. **Write the single long monologue**, placed in the penultimate passage (the night talk on the trip).
11. **Write the diminishing ending**: social space → home → unpeopled pillow shot; one comic scene + one lonely scene.
12. **Format check**: bold scene numbers, location first, dashes for continuation, silence as a full line, age and status in parentheses at first appearance, transitional pillow shots as independent scene numbers.

## XI. Self-Check List

- [ ] Does the protagonist ever state his grievance directly in the film? (He should not — a supporting character says it.)
- [ ] Is the most important admission longer than one syllable?
- [ ] Does the rejected suitor ever show his face? (He should not.)
- [ ] Is there one line of pillow shot between every emotional passage?
- [ ] Is there only one long speech, and is it in the penultimate passage?
- [ ] Is the quarrel about a small object rather than the real problem?
- [ ] Is anyone present in the final scene? (No one should be.)
- [ ] Is the theme line spoken while looking at a landscape?
- [ ] Is the driver's selfish motive exposed with laughter by someone else (a wife / an outsider)?
- [ ] Does the dead person have an object or a living person who "looks a little like" them?
