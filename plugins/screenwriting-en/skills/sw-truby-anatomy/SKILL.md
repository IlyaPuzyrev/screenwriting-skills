# Organic Story Anatomy (Truby's Organic Anatomy)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

This skill is distilled from John Truby's *The Anatomy of Story* (2007), published in Chinese as 《故事写作大师班》 (The Master Class in Story Writing). Truby's role in the industry is that of a "script doctor" — he diagnoses structural ailments in other people's screenplays for production companies, and this method is the diagnostic sheet itself: **the designing principle, the seven key steps, the twenty-two steps, four-corner opposition, the moral-argument chain, and the scene weave**. The book demonstrates the method through step-by-step breakdowns of classics such as 《教父》 (The Godfather), 《卡萨布兰卡》 (Casablanca), and 《窈窕淑男》 (Tootsie); for examples, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. Relationship to the Three-Act Page-Number System (Read First)

**The disagreement is kept side by side** (a convention of this library):

- Truby's position: the seven steps "**unlike mechanical story structures such as the three-act structure, cannot be imposed on a story from the outside at will. They exist inside the story**" (Ch3); the twenty-two steps "will never tell you what to write; that is what a formula or a genre asks you to do" (Ch8).
- Field/Snyder's position (`sw-story-structure`): structure = a page map. One page = one minute; the plot points, the midpoint, and all is lost fall on specific pages.

**The two are not mutually exclusive; they serve different questions**: the page map governs "how long, at what rhythm," while organic anatomy governs "whether the skeleton grows out of the characters." Two approaches are recommended; choose one, not both:

1. **Map first, then verify** (most common): after the BS2 board is finalized, use this skill to check for gaps in the middle-act components (see Sections IV and V) and in the moral-argument chain (Section VII).
2. **Skeleton first, then page numbers**: start from the designing principle and the seven key steps to establish the organic skeleton, then convert to page numbers following the workflow in Section XI of `sw-story-structure`.

## II. The Designing Principle

- **Definition**: the **overall strategy** for telling the story, expressible in one sentence. "A story's designing principle is how the story premise is expanded into a deep structure... It is the story's internal logic, which gathers the parts together organically so that the whole is greater than the sum of its parts. This is precisely what makes a story original" (Ch2).
- **Division of labor with the premise**: "All stories have a premise, but only good stories have a designing principle. The premise is concrete, what actually happens; the designing principle is abstract, a deeper process unfolding within the story... **A designing principle = the story's process of development + an original technique**" (Ch2). The premise answers "what happens"; the designing principle answers "in what form it is told."
- **Three layers, each handling one thing; none substitutes for another** (the interface with `sw-premise-theme`): Egri's premise (character trait + cause + outcome) = the burden of dialectical proof; McKee's controlling idea (value + cause) = the meaning of the climax; Truby's designing principle = the formal strategy of narration. Write all three for the same project, each as one line in the story bible.
- **How to find it**: work like a detective, "inducing" the form from the premise. Common sources: a journey that serves as a metaphor (the voyage upriver in 《黑暗之心》 (Heart of Darkness)); a single symbol (the letter A in 《红字》 (The Scarlet Letter), the whale in 《白鲸记》 (Moby-Dick)); a unit of time (《相逢圣路易》 (Meet Me in St. Louis) organized by the four seasons); a distinctive use of narrator (the multiple narrators of 《公民凯恩》 (Citizen Kane)); the process itself (《窈窕淑男》 (Tootsie) makes a male chauvinist live a woman's life). For the full table of examples, see Section I of reference.md.
- **Warning**: "Most writers do not find a unique designing principle; they choose a genre and force it onto the premise... The result is a mechanical, unoriginal piece of ordinary fiction" (Ch2). This is the same warning as `sw-premise-theme` Section III (Cron): "a good story comes first, structure afterward"; Truby supplies the positive tool for it.

## III. The Seven Key Steps and the Accordion Principle

**The seven key steps** (the organic spine, Ch3):

| # | Step | In one sentence | Corresponding existing tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Weakness / need | The protagonist is held back by a severe weakness and must meet an inner need in order to live a better life | Cron's misbelief, Hicks's inner need, Egri's three dimensions |
| 2 | Desire | The goal he consciously wants to achieve (externally visible) | want (terms.md, Section 2) |
| 3 | Opponent | The person who blocks the protagonist from achieving the goal, competing for the same goal but with different values | `sw-character-conflict`, Section III |
| 4 | Plan | A strategy or method for defeating the opponent | the "preparation" element among Hicks's ten elements |
| 5 | Battle | The final conflict with the opponent, deciding who achieves the goal | McKee's climax |
| 6 | Self-revelation | The insight, won through the gauntlet of the battle, into how he has wronged himself and others | Hicks's self-revelation, McKee's arc |
| 7 | New equilibrium | The new state after the need is met or left unmet | Indick's "resolution vs. ending" |

Key points: (1) the protagonist **should not be aware of his need** at the beginning; he becomes aware only at the self-revelation; (2) need must be split into two layers — **psychological** (harmful only to himself) and **moral** (at the beginning he is always harming others in some way). A story that supplies only the psychological need has a moral engine that will not turn (see Section VII for details).

**The accordion principle** (Ch8): "A story may have more or fewer than twenty-two steps, depending on its genre and length. Try to imagine the story as an accordion... It cannot have fewer than the seven key steps, because these are the minimum that an organic story should possess. Even a thirty-second commercial... will have these seven key steps." A short film or a sitcom runs on the seven steps alone; a feature film usually has at least twenty-two; **a multi-strand plot = each strand runs through the seven key steps once**. The order need not be doctrinaire: "Every good story, as it operates through these twenty-two steps, has a slightly different order. You must find the order most appropriate to your particular story and characters" (Ch8).

## IV. The Full Table of the Twenty-Two Steps (with BS2 Anchor Cross-Reference)

The left column follows Truby's original order in Ch8; the right column gives the nearest BS2 anchor (`sw-story-structure`, Section III). **The correspondence is rough, meant for finding gaps, not for cell-by-cell alignment.** "Main subsystems" is summarized from the book's account: the twenty-two steps are an interweaving of four systems — the character web, the moral argument, the story world, and the plot — and each step may belong to more than one subsystem (for example, "drive" is both a plot step and often an immoral action).

| # | Step | Key point | Main subsystems | Nearest BS2 anchor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Self-revelation, need, desire | Set the destination first (what he will learn), then return to the beginning (what he knows, what his misconception is) | character web + plot | (overall frame, covering the opening image / final image) |
| 2 | Ghost and story world | The past event haunting the protagonist; the world expresses and worsens the protagonist's weakness | story world + character web | opening image / set-up |
| 3 | Weakness / need | psychological + moral layers; unaware at the start | character web | set-up / the six things that need fixing |
| 4 | Inciting incident | The event that breaks the balance | plot | inciting incident |
| 5 | Desire | A clear, specific goal | plot | end of the inciting incident |
| 6 | Ally | Helper; give the ally his own desire, but do not make him more interesting than the protagonist | character web | (can go into the subplot) |
| 7 | Opponent / mystery | The best opponent is one who "must exist"; a mysterious opponent is harder to defeat | character web + plot | (within the set-up) |
| 8 | Opponent / fake ally | An opponent appearing in the guise of an ally, with a double revelation | character web | **not in BS2** |
| 9 | First revelation and decision | Revelation must carry a decision and change desire and motive | plot + moral argument | around the break into Act Two |
| 10 | Plan | A concrete strategy for defeating the opponent; only when the plan fails is there real struggle | plot | **not in BS2 (scattered through fun and games)** |
| 11 | Opponent's plan and main counterattack | The opponent is also acting; give the opponent a plan | plot + character web | bad guys close in |
| 12 | Drive | A series of actions by the protagonist pursuing the goal, often including immoral actions | plot + moral argument | fun and games → midpoint |
| 13 | Ally's attack | An ally criticizes the protagonist's methods; the protagonist rationalizes | moral argument | **not in BS2** |
| 14 | Fake defeat | A double blow; he appears to have lost everything | plot | all is lost |
| 15 | Second revelation and decision | The obsessive drive; desire and motive change again | plot + moral argument | dark night of the soul |
| 16 | Audience revelation | The audience knows what the protagonist does not; the tension reverses | plot | **not in BS2** |
| 17 | Third revelation and decision | The final pressure and decision | plot | before the break into Act Three |
| 18 | Gate, gauntlet, visit to death | The narrow gate before the climax; a symbolic death | plot + story world | break into Act Three |
| 19 | Battle | The final conflict with the opponent; values are decided here | plot + moral argument | finale |
| 20 | Self-revelation | Insight into his own error and how he should treat others | character web | (within the finale) |
| 21 | Moral decision | Choose one of two actions to **prove** the self-revelation; BS2 has no such beat | moral argument | **not in BS2** |
| 22 | New equilibrium | The world is restored, but different | plot + moral argument | final image |

**How to read the gaps**: the six places marked "not in BS2" (8 fake-ally opponent, 10 plan, 13 ally's attack, 16 audience revelation, 21 moral decision, plus the whole category of the three revelation-and-decision pairs) are the middle-act structural ailments that a BS2 board cannot detect — when Act Two feels loose, check here first. See Section V for details.

## V. Five Key Middle-Act Components (Where BS2 Is Thinnest)

### 1. Opponent / Fake Ally (Step 8)
An opponent who appears in the guise of an ally: the person the protagonist trusts most happens to be the one who wounds him most deeply. This carries a **double revelation**: the revelation to the protagonist (he finally sees clearly) and the revelation to the audience (the audience knew all along). The book's criterion: the fake-ally opponent occupies "the best position from which to win by exploiting the protagonist's weakness" — the opponent attacks the weakness in the name of friendship, and the protagonist has no way to defend himself. In 《教父》 (The Godfather), Michael's chain of fake allies: the driver, the bodyguard Fabrizio, his brother-in-law Carlo, and Tessio, who defects to the enemy camp (reference.md, Section III, item 8).

### 2. The Revelation-and-Decision Sequence (Steps 9 / 15 / 17)
Truby's fundamental demand on the middle act: **every revelation must carry a decision and must change the protagonist's desire or motive** — a revelation that does not change the direction of action is merely decorative information. "In fact, these twenty-two steps ensure that the plot is driven by the main characters" (Ch8). The division of labor among the three revelations: the first changes desire and motive (entering the confrontation); the second is the obsessive drive (sinking deeper and deeper); the third is the final pressure (driving toward the battle). When designing, you can work backward: fix the battle first, then lay in the three-stage staircase of revelations leading up to it.

### 3. The Ally's Attack (Step 13)
A device that dramatizes the moral argument: the protagonist begins to take immoral actions in order to win, **an ally steps forward to criticize him, and the protagonist rationalizes on the spot** ("I did it because..."). Both the criticism and the rationalization must be dramatized; they must not be declared in dialogue (the interface with McKee: never resort to explanatory dialogue). Without this step, the protagonist's fall is just a stream of events, not a moral arc.

### 4. Audience Revelation (Step 16)
The audience knows what the protagonist does not. The tension reverses from "what happens next" to "when will he find out" — the same information gap serves two kinds of suspense (it has the same source as dramatic irony in `sw-series-structure`; Truby makes it a plot step rather than an optional technique).

### 5. Moral Decision (Step 21)
The beat between the climax (the battle) and the resolution: the protagonist chooses between **two actions**, proving through action that the self-revelation is true. Projects that "end as soon as the climax arrives" usually lack this beat. McKee's crisis dilemma governs "what is chosen"; Truby's moral decision governs "what proves it after the choice" — the two connect without duplicating each other.

## VI. Four-Corner Opposition

"A better story goes beyond the simple opposition of protagonist and main opponent" (Ch4). The method: protagonist + main opponent + at least two secondary opponents, each occupying one corner of a rectangle.

Four key features (summarized from Ch4, in worksheet form):

1. **Each opponent attacks the protagonist's major weakness in a different way** — the opponent's core purpose is to attack the weakness, and the first way to distinguish opponents is by their different methods of attack; this organically links all conflict to the protagonist's flaw, and the four corners are at the same time a microcosm of the group.
2. **Conflict between every pair**: not only does the protagonist fight three opponents; the opponents also fight one another — "the number of conflicts multiplies."
3. **All four sets of values are put into conflict**: give each character a set of values (not just one word), and find the positive and negative form of each value (resolute / aggressive; honest / insufficiently sensitive) — this is how "the circumstances in which he is most likely to go wrong while fighting for his beliefs" become visible.
4. **Push the characters toward their corners as far as possible**: maximize the differences between them.

Worksheet (write it into the character section of the story bible):

| Corner | Character | Method of attacking the protagonist's weakness | Value set (+ positive and negative forms) | Conflict with the other three corners |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Division of labor with existing tools**: "give the opponent a knife" (`sw-character-conflict`, Section III) adds weight to a **single** opponent; four-corner opposition is a **multi-opponent topology** — use the four corners when a single opponent cannot carry the theme. The Jungian quaternity (hero / shadow / wise old man / love object) divides roles by **complementary function**, while four-corner opposition divides them by **conflictual opposition**; the two tables can be layered: the quaternity determines "who is missing," and the four corners determine "who collides with whom."

## VII. The Moral-Argument Strategy Chain

McKee (`sw-premise-theme`, Section II) supplies the principle: idea versus counter-idea, with equal force. Truby supplies the chain: dramatize the theme as a series of the protagonist's actions. The basic strategy (Ch5, in the book's order):

1. Values: the protagonist begins with a set of beliefs.
2. Moral weakness: at the beginning he harms others in some way (not evil, merely affected by weakness).
3. Moral need: he must learn to treat others rightly in order to grow.
4. **First immoral action**: taken almost immediately; it is the first evidence of the protagonist's fundamental moral flaw.
5. Desire: he sets a goal and collides head-on with an opponent who holds different values.
6. Drive: a series of actions by both sides.
7. **Immoral action → criticism → rationalization**: after losing to the opponent he goes all in; others criticize; the protagonist defends himself.
8. **The ally's attack**: the person closest to him points out that he is wrong (the same source as Step 13).
9. Obsessed drive: immoral actions intensify, and criticism and rationalization escalate in step.
10. The theme is grasped by the audience in four places: **the battle** (values are decided) → **the moral self-revelation** (born of the gauntlet) → **the moral decision** (proved by choice) → **the theme revelation** (passing beyond the characters to the universal insight of "how one human being should treat another").

**The power-balance criterion**: "The balance of power between the protagonist and the main opponent matters not only for character and plot but also, and crucially, for the moral argument. If the protagonist is too strong and too good, the opponent cannot bring a sufficient test, and he will not therefore make a moral-level mistake. If the opponent is too strong... the protagonist becomes a victim, and the opponent becomes evil or nearly evil" (Ch5). The book's counterexample: 《一位女士的画像》 (The Portrait of a Lady). This points in the same direction as McKee's "forces of antagonism in proportion," but what it supplies is a criterion for **whether the moral engine can start**, not merely a criterion for the intensity of conflict.

## VIII. Ghost and Story World (Boundary Section)

We take only four principles; **this library does not elaborate on the two layers of the story world and the symbolic network** — 陆军 (Lu Jun)'s 戏核 (xìhé — the core premise without which the play would not stand) / 戏胆 (xìdǎn — the branch: a prop or key plot event that performs a special function) / 戏眼 (xìyǎn — the leaf: a key character or vivid scene) (`sw-premise-theme`, Section X) and McKee's system of imagery already cover most uses:

1. The ghost = an event from the past that still haunts the protagonist, an "exposed wound," often the source of psychological and moral weakness. You can think of the ghost as **the protagonist's inner opponent** — "desire pushes him forward, the ghost pulls him back" (Ch8). It is the same thing as Cron's original scene: Cron governs the writing (a detailed first-person account of the moment it took root), Truby governs the structural position (it is Step 2, before the inciting incident).
2. The second kind of ghost (rare): a paradise world in which the protagonist is initially free; after an attack, that world is lost (《相逢圣路易》 (Meet Me in St. Louis), 《越战猎鹿人》 (The Deer Hunter)).
3. Discipline for opening exposition: "Try to withhold much information about the protagonist, including the details of his ghost. The audience will guess that you are hiding something, and that is how they truly enter your story" (Ch8).
4. Two key points about the story world: the world should be a way of expressing the protagonist; if the protagonist is enslaved at the beginning, the world should also be enslaving, and it should worsen his major weakness. For a deeper treatment, see `sw-scene-craft`, Section VIII (the scene must be apt).

## IX. Scene Weave

The last step before writing the complete script; "its more common names include the scene list and the step outline" (Ch9). The division of labor with `sw-scene-craft` and the BS2 board: the forty cards govern the four-row distribution and the placement of turns; the scene weave governs three things for **each scene**:

1. **A single action**: "list only the single, necessary action of each scene" — write it in one or two sentences; the whole list should fit on a few pages; the average Hollywood film has 40–70 scenes.
2. **Structural-step labels**: tag the scenes that represent structural steps (desire, plan, fake defeat...); it is normal for many scenes to have no tag.
3. **Narrative-line numbers**: in a multi-strand or multi-subplot story, mark each scene with the number of the line it belongs to — a multi-strand plot = each line runs through the seven key steps (the accordion principle, Section III).

Three revision moves after the list: **reorder** (first arrange the overall sequence of progression, then check adjacent juxtapositions), **combine** ("sometimes a writer creates a new scene only to accommodate one good line of dialogue"), and **cut or add** (the less fat the better; add a new scene only when a cut leaves a gap).

Interface: the step-outline row format in Stage 4 of `sw-workflow` already has a "structural position" column — BS2 beat names and the names of the twenty-two steps can both be used in that column, and multi-strand projects add the line number at the end of the row.

## X. Diagnostic Checklist (Ten Questions for a Loose Middle Act)

When Act Two scatters into isolated events, or "every scene is right but they do not connect," ask these questions one by one:

1. Is there a "drive" — a **continuous** series of actions by the protagonist toward the goal, or a string of events that do not propel one another?
2. Is there a fake-ally opponent? Which of the protagonist's weaknesses does he attack?
3. Are all three revelations and decisions present? Does each revelation **change desire or motive**? If not, it is a decorative revelation: cut it or change it.
4. Are the opponent's plan and main counterattack on the page? Does the opponent have a game of his own?
5. After losing to the opponent, does the protagonist take immoral action? Is there criticism and rationalization? Has an ally attacked him?
6. Is there an "audience revelation" — when does the audience begin to know more than the protagonist?
7. Before the battle, is there a gate / gauntlet / visit to death?
8. After the climax, is there a moral decision (a choice between two actions), or does the story simply stop?
9. Is the power balanced: is the protagonist too strong (no moral cost) or too weak (a pure victim)?
10. Are the four corners filled: does each opponent attack the protagonist's weakness in a different way? Do the opponents conflict with one another? Do the four sets of values collide?

The first three questions cover 80% of "soft-belly second act" cases. The division of labor with McKee's progressive complications (`sw-story-structure`, Section VII): the gap governs the force with which action escalates; this checklist governs **whether the middle-act components are present** — check the components first, then the force.

## XI. Workflow (Interface with the Feature-Film Stage Table in `sw-workflow`)

1. **End of Stage 1**: after the premise and controlling idea are finalized, find the designing principle (method in Section II). If after two iterations it is still the same sentence as the premise, the idea has not yet found a form — send it back.
2. **Stage 2**: after the BS2 board is finalized, go through the comparison table in Section IV and check whether the six components marked "not in BS2" were meant to be there in the first place (for a short film, skip them under the accordion principle).
3. **Stage 3**: build the character list with the four-corner opposition worksheet (Section VI); verify the power-balance criterion.
4. **Stage 4**: mark the step outline with the names of the twenty-two steps and line numbers; run the three scene-weave revision moves (Section IX).
5. **During Stage 5, writing**: stuck in Act Two → run the first three questions in Section X.
6. **Stage 6**: the ten middle-act questions plus a link-by-link check of the moral-argument chain (Section VII, links 1–10).

**When not to use this skill**: a short film or one-act play needs only the seven steps; Chekhovian/Ozu-style anticlimax and multi-protagonist projects (`chekhov-dramaturgy`, `ozu-screenplay-style`) have no central battle, so Steps 19–21 do not apply; series projects follow the series stage table in `sw-workflow`, working in units of the act-out engine rather than forcing the twenty-two steps (for the priority of scene-level tools in S5, see that table). Chinese opera projects are out of scope (for the media boundary, see `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang`).

## XII. Anti-Patterns

- Treating the twenty-two steps as a second page-number table and aligning them cell by cell — it is an anchor, not a grid.
- Inventing fake steps to fill out the twenty-two; the accordion principle allows expansion and contraction.
- Turning four-corner opposition into four villains — opponents need not be evil (Hicks and Hoxter agree); attacking the weakness is enough.
- Writing the moral-argument chain as declarations in dialogue — criticism, rationalization, and the ally's attack must all be carried by character action.
- Filling in both the BS2 board and the twenty-two steps in detail for the same project — make one table the detailed one and use the other for verification.
- Using the designing principle as a substitute for the premise or the controlling idea — each of the three layers handles one thing (Section II).
