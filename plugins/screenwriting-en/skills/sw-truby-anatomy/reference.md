## I. Table of Designing-Principle Examples (Ch2: Premise vs. Designing Principle)

| Work | Premise (what happens) | Designing principle (in what form it is told) |
|---|---|---|
| 《教父》 (The Godfather) | The inner workings of a Mafia family | Uses the strategy of the classic fairy tale to show the youngest of three sons becoming the new "king" |
| 《窈窕淑男》 (Tootsie) | An actor who cannot find work disguises himself as a woman and falls in love with an actress in the same show | Make a male chauvinist live a woman's life |
| 《公民凯恩》 (Citizen Kane) | The life story of a wealthy newspaper magnate | Through the accounts of multiple storytellers, present the life of someone who cannot be fully known |
| 《摩西》 (Moses) | An Egyptian prince discovers he is a Jew and leads his people out of slavery | A man who does not know his own identity leads his people to freedom and receives a new moral law that redefines both his identity and theirs |

| 《四个婚礼和一个葬礼》 (Four Weddings and a Funeral) | A man falls in love with a woman, but the two become engaged to other people in turn | A group of friends searching for marriage partners pass through four utopias (weddings) and one hellish moment (a funeral) |

| 《长日入夜行》 (Long Day's Journey into Night) | A family confronts the mother's drug addiction | A family moves from day into night, confronting past sins and ghosts |

## II. Example for Step 1: 《卡萨布兰卡》 (Casablanca) and 《窈窕淑男》 (Tootsie) (Ch8)

> Self-revelation, need, and desire represent the overall extent of the protagonist's transformation in the story. This is a combination of Steps 20, 3, and 5... Starting from this frame of the story, we first establish the endpoint of the plot.

**《卡萨布兰卡》 (Casablanca)**: Self-revelation — Rick realizes that he cannot abandon the fight for freedom merely because he has been disappointed in love. Psychological need — overcome his resentment toward Ilsa, find a reason to live again, and regain faith in his ideals. Moral need — stop benefiting himself at others' expense. Desire — to have Ilsa back at his side. Misbelief about himself — Rick believes he is one of the living dead and that the world's affairs are no concern of his.

**《窈窕淑男》 (Tootsie)**: Self-revelation — Michael realizes that he treats women merely as sexual targets and therefore is not a real man. Psychological need — overcome his arrogance toward women and learn to give and receive love sincerely. Moral need — stop lying and stop using women for his own ends. Desire — win the heart of Julie, an actress in the same show. Misbelief about himself — Michael believes his attitude toward women is appropriate and that lying to women does not matter.

The three frame questions (Ch8): (1) What will the protagonist learn by the end of the story? (2) What does he know at the beginning (no character is a blank slate; he must have specific beliefs)? (3) What misconceptions are there at the beginning?

## IV. The Ghost (Ch8, Step 2)

> Most writers are familiar with the term "backstory"... The audience is not interested in everything that happens to the protagonist; they are interested only in the essential elements, so the term "ghost" is much better. There are two kinds of ghost in a story. The first and most common is an event from the past that still haunts the protagonist. This ghost is an exposed wound, often the source of the protagonist's psychological and moral weaknesses... You can also think of the first kind of ghost as **the protagonist's inner opponent**. It is a great fear that prevents the protagonist from committing to action. Structurally, the ghost functions as a force counter to desire. The protagonist's desire pushes him forward, while the ghost pulls him back.

Examples: Hamlet (his uncle murders his father and marries his mother — the ghost before page one; in the first few pages the actual ghost appears to collect the debt); George Bailey in 《生活多美好》 (It's a Wonderful Life) (his desire is to get out and make his way in the world; his ghost is the fear of what the tyrant Potter will do to his friends and family if he leaves). The second kind of ghost: the protagonist initially lives in a paradise-like world, and everything changes after an attack (《相逢圣路易》 (Meet Me in St. Louis), 《猎鹿人》 (The Deer Hunter)).

Discipline for the opening (Ch8): "Many writers try to tell the audience everything about the protagonist on page one, including why and how he suffers from his ghost. This heap of information actually pushes the audience away from the story. You should do the opposite: try to withhold much information about the protagonist... The audience will think, 'Something is going on here, and I want to find out what it is.'"

## V. Four Key Features of Four-Corner Opposition (Ch4)

1. **Each opponent should use a different method to attack the protagonist's major weakness.** The opponent's core purpose is to attack the weakness, and the first way to distinguish opponents is by their different methods of attack; "this technique allows all conflict to be organically linked to the protagonist's major flaw," and the four corners are at the same time a microcosm of the whole group.
2. **Put every character into conflict**: conflict with the protagonist and with every other character — "through four-corner opposition... the number of conflicts that can be created and structured multiplies."
3. **All four characters' values are put into conflict**: "Do not give each character only one value. Imagine for each character a set of values he might hold"; and "find the positive and negative form of each value" — examples of the positive and negative forms of the same value: resolute vs. aggressive, honest vs. insufficiently sensitive, patriotic vs. self-righteously obstinate. This shows the situation in which a character is most likely to go wrong while fighting for his beliefs.
4. **Push the characters toward their corners as far as possible** — maximize the differences.

Examples from the book: 《哈姆雷特》 (Hamlet), 《美国丽人》 (American Beauty), 《呼啸山庄》 (Wuthering Heights), 《樱桃园》 (The Cherry Orchard) (for the corner-by-corner placement table and the value sets, see the charts in Chapter 4 of the book).

## VI. The Full Chain of the Basic Moral-Argument Strategy (Ch5)

Values: the protagonist starts from a set of beliefs and values → moral weakness: at the beginning he harms others in some way (not evil, merely affected by weakness, or not knowing the right way to treat people) → moral need → **first immoral action** (taken almost immediately; the first evidence of the protagonist's fundamental moral flaw) → desire (the goal leads to direct conflict with an opponent who has the same goal and radically different values) → drive → immoral action (in the early and middle stages the protagonist usually loses to the opponent and begins to act immorally in order to win) → criticism (other characters criticize his methods) → rationalization (the protagonist defends himself) → **the ally's attack** (his closest friend points out that he is wrong) → obsessive drive (under the stimulus of a new revelation, he will do anything to succeed) → immoral actions intensify, and criticism and rationalization strengthen in step → the theme is grasped in **four places**: the battle (values are decided, including the final action against the opponent); the moral self-revelation (born of the gauntlet of the battle); the moral decision (choosing between two actions, showing that the self-revelation is true); the theme revelation ("actually the audience's insight into how ordinary people should treat one another... far larger in scale than a few characters").

Counterexample for power balance (Ch5): Henry James's 《一位女士的画像》 (The Portrait of a Lady) — Isabel deceives herself from beginning to end and faces the calculating Osmond; the power is unbalanced, and the moral argument is therefore weakened.

## VII. Scene-Weave Example (Ch9)

> The more common names for the scene weave include the scene list, the step outline, or the scene breakdown. It is the last step before writing the complete story or script... Try to describe each scene in one sentence... List only the single, necessary action of each scene. Next, beside the scene description, list all the structural steps connected to it (such as desire, plan, or fake defeat). Some scenes may carry such an identifying tag, but many scenes do not.

Four example scenes from 《教父》 (The Godfather) (original text):
- Michael saves the old Godfather in the hospital, preventing his assassination.
- Michael accuses Police Captain McCluskey of working for Sollozzo; McCluskey responds with his fists.
- Michael says he will eliminate McCluskey and Sollozzo.
- Clemenza teaches Michael how to eliminate McCluskey and Sollozzo.

Three revision moves (in the book's order, Ch9): (1) **rearrange the order of scenes** (first arrange the overall sequence of progression, then check the juxtaposition of individual scenes); (2) **combine scenes** ("sometimes a writer creates a new scene only to accommodate one good line of dialogue"); (3) **check for cuts or additions** ("the less fat the better... once you have cut all the fat, you may find gaps in the scene weave that must be filled with entirely new scenes"). Scale reference: the average Hollywood film has 40–70 scenes.
