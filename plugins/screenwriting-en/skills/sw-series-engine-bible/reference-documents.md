## VIII. The Full Structure of Rabkin's Mooresville Pitch Document (Ten Sections)

**Source**: Rabkin, *Writing the Pilot: Creating the Series* (2017), Appendix "Mooresville Pitch." It is the finished product of the NASCAR derivation process in Chapter 7—a **written pitch (series pitch document)** addressed to NASCAR executives and the network, not a pilot script; it amounts to a complete real-world sample of a mini-bible.

**Two things to know before using it**: ① The total page count and the word count of each section **are not recorded**; the notes preserve only two length clues—the Overview is "a one-page positioning," and How We Got Here describes itself as a short version of "about 60 pages of pilot script." ② The book (2017, a sequel to the 2011 original) **does not contain** a pilot typology—a full-text grep for "premise pilot / cold open / four-act" returns nothing; so the criteria for premise vs. typical-episode must be taken from Blum ch17, Smith ch20, Landau, and Oberg 3.2 (see Sections IV and IX of this document), and should not be attributed to Rabkin.

### VIII.1 Specifications for the Ten Sections, One by One

| # | Section title (as written) | What this section must answer | Length indication | Key points of the model content given in the book |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Overview** | What kind of show this is and why it is worth buying—with one selling point per line | **One page** (the note's exact words: "one-page positioning") | Four lines, four selling points, each line carrying a genre or a hook: ① "a **fish-out-of-water comedy** about a lifelong New Yorker forced to live on the NASCAR circuit."; ② "a **romance** about the growing, impossible attraction between two completely incompatible personalities."; ③ "a **drama** about the challenges of staying free in a world that wants you to conform … what it means … and what it costs." (= the pitch version of Chapter 7's theme "the individual vs. the community"); ④ "most of all, it's about the **thrill of NASCAR Racing** – not the sterile sport we see on TV, but the raw power of the real thing." (a direct promise to the buyer, NASCAR, followed by "the spirit that comes straight down from the original moonshiners racing on the Daytona sands"). Closing line: "Mooresville. It's all about the love for speed and freedom." |
| 2 | **Our Hero** | Who the protagonist is, what he wants, why he is trapped here | Not recorded | Open with an **analogy** (a Spartan warrior dropped onto the board of a Manhattan private-equity firm), then flip the analogy: Marshall Breckman = "**Warrior of the Deal**," born in a Fifth Avenue penthouse, New Haven & Cambridge (Yale/Harvard MBA), "prepped to win"; then dropped into a land where "people like plain talk, not smooth words … the only market people care about is what's on sale at the A&P." The situation: exiled by Wall Street to Mooresville, North Carolina, as the new CFO of "the last independent racing team in NASCAR"; he has never seen a race, **cannot drive**, thinks the audience are hicks, and considers the job "only slightly better than a life sentence in Guantanamo." The engine sentence (want and weekly task fused): **"For him, the only road back to Manhattan is the straightaway at Daytona …"** |
| 3 | **Last of a Dying Breed** | Who the female lead is, her family history, and where her "never give an inch" comes from | Not recorded | Start with three generations of family history: grandfather **Jack Stagger** (former moonshiner, racing pioneer); father **Jimmy Lee Stagger**—a mid-pack driver famous for "independence" (to others, "orneriest SOB on the circuit"), who, after the sponsors fled, painted over the logos with white paint and kept racing; husband and wife die when their school bus goes off a cliff on the way to a church picnic (a passing touch of black humor). Then the female lead **LEIGH PETTY STAGGER (Pet)**, 27, named after Jack's old friend and rival (Richard Petty); "thrown out of more schools than most people apply to"; brilliant but **"she's got this thing about authority. Well, up to a point, anyway." Writing point: **the female lead's page is built around the one conflict-bearing trait that guarantees friction with the protagonist** (Chapter 7: "she exists to fill his opposite space") |
| 4 | **How We Got Here** | What the pilot tells and how it establishes the series' normal state | Described by the author as a short version of "about **60 pages** of pilot script"; the number of paragraphs is not recorded, but the note records it as "one turn per paragraph" | Marshall is a rising star at the private-equity firm Barzun Keegan, sent to Mooresville for a day trip to help Jack complete the sale of the team to NASCAR giant **Raymond Fester Industries (RFI)** → he meets Pet, "his resolve started to waver" (the note adds that she is "absolutely, knock-down gorgeous … It's part of her power. She uses it, she dresses for it."—he hates being manipulated by appearances yet cannot stop thinking about her) → he takes one more look at the documents → finds the deal is wrong → catches a colleague working for RFI → the truth: **Barzun Keegan is negotiating to buy RFI, and Marshall is expected to deliberately lose on his own side** → instead he gives the Staggers a new plan that can save the team and buy them a few months to find sponsorship, "went to war," and defeats RFI and his own firm → **he is fired and blacklisted across the industry** ("this man can't be trusted") → the only offer: stay on as CFO of Stagger Racing. Writing point: **the pilot synopsis tells only "how we got here," and it must end on the establishment of the series' normal state (he is trapped here), not on a plot climax** |
| 5 | **Life in Mooresville** | What this world is and what the protagonist has lost | Not recorded (the content is a **two-column comparison list**) | **What Manhattan has** (as written): a two-bedroom with a Central Park view; opera season tickets (orchestra seats); the private phone number of the city's top maître d' and a guaranteed table anytime; a 24-hour chauffeur; bespoke suits; elegant women who long to be seen with the "Warrior of the Deal"; top-tier art, music, and culture; an expense account that covers everything. **What Mooresville has**: a "two-room suite" at the local Motel 7 (slogan "It's one better than 6!", actually two adjacent rooms with no connecting door); a 24-hour Wal-Mart across the street; a young female taxi driver who calls herself JONES (discounts in exchange for him taking her to New York to break into the business someday). **36 weeks a year** the office is a cubicle in the back of a car transporter, and his home is a partitioned RV shared with Jones (strictly a roommate). Add his judgment of Pet: he comes from "a world of subtext, of hidden meanings, of codes," while she is "as blunt and direct as a hurricane – and she leaves the same kind of messes behind her"; "To someone like Marshall, she's practically a savage … she should be locked in a cage. If only he could stop thinking about her …" Writing point: **the two-column list makes the fish-out-of-water gap concrete; the world's physical constraints (36 weeks on the road, an office in a trailer) are themselves the container for weekly stories** |
| 6 | **It Goes the Other Way, Too …** | The protagonist seen from the other side—why the attraction is **mutual** | Not recorded | In her eyes he is "that little wormy thing wriggling on the ground" (three-piece suit, calculating, talks too much); deeper hatred: he nominally works for her, yet runs her business in ways she cannot understand, "he's turned himself into the one thing she hates more than everything else in the world: **an authority figure**."; she already has a boyfriend, **KYLE COKER** (the team's only driver, "strong, quiet, ruggedly handsome, defiant … and fast"). Why she still thinks about Marshall: everyone who has known her for years indulges her as a willful child (love without respect; Kyle sees himself as her protector and rock, not her equal); **Marshall treats her as a CEO and argues when he disagrees, but "start from the position that they are both rational, intelligent adults"**—"When Marshall treats her like an adult, she starts to act like one … she likes herself a lot better when he's around." (= the source of the "rational axis" item in Section VI of this document) |
| 7 | **Live Free or … Well, You Know** | What the weekly operating reality is and what the protagonist's ongoing task is | Not recorded | Operating reality: a five-person team; one small disaster can destroy the team; **every non-mechanical matter falls to Marshall**. The engine's final formulation: Pet loathes authority → she hires only people like herself → "Incredibly talented technicians who have been fired from every other crew on the circuit" → **"It's going to be up to Marshall to take this collection of misfits and turn them into a team."** ("he's got to learn to be Daddy") |
| 8 | **The Rest of the Characters** | What team function each supporting character serves and what unexploded material they carry | Not recorded (one paragraph per person, seven people; see VIII.2) | Formula: **each supporting character = one team function + one concrete form of "stubbornness/freedom" (serving the theme) + one unexploded relationship or desire (a landmine)** |
| 9 | **The Antagonist** | What the villain wants, why he must crush the protagonist's side, and what personal history he has with the protagonist's camp | Not recorded | RAYMOND FESTER (see the last row of VIII.2). Writing point: **the villain's motivation is supplied by the theme** (the extreme individualist version of "the individual vs. the community"), and he has a personal history with the female lead |
| 10 | **Sample Story Areas** | Whether this pattern can run repeatedly—the evidence | Six entries (see VIII.3); the length of each is not recorded, and the note transcribes each as a paragraph of three to four sentences | All six run the same pattern, and each simultaneously engages both the A story (business) and the B story (triangle/relationship); **this is how the pitch document verifies the story pattern from Chapter 8 and the theme from Chapter 7** |

### VIII.2 Nine Characters Plus the Villain: Team Function, Thematic Position, Landmine

(Source as above, Rabkin appendix Mooresville Pitch §The Rest of the Characters and §The Antagonist. Theme = "the individual vs. the community" as developed in Chapter 7.)

| Character | Team function | Position on "the individual vs. the community" | Landmine planted (unexploded relationship/desire/contradiction) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marshall Breckman** | CFO / outsider | **Community** (dealing = compromise; his task is to mold a group of misfits into a team) | Wanting to return to Manhattan vs. falling in love with Pet; his friendship with Kyle |
| **Pet Stagger** | boss | **Extreme individual** (against all authority) | Sees Marshall as authority, yet grows because of him; keeps postponing the wedding to Kyle |
| **Kyle Coker** | only driver | Individual (has his own complete theory of the universe) | Two obstacles: ① a self-taught "cosmic theorist" (cancer was a biological weapon invented by Atlantis priests, the cure lies in ruins on the California coast…; he falls apart the moment a camera points at him); ② he still races well in an old car, proving his talent. Why he does not leave the team: **he has loved Pet for 25 years** (he is 28), they met in preschool, and he has always cleaned up her messes; after her parents' funeral he proposed and she said yes, **but she keeps postponing the wedding**. He regards Marshall as his best friend (the only person who will seriously debate his theories with him), "There's no way Marshall could ever betray him – people just don't act like that." → **the triangle landmine** |
| **S.T.A.N. (Spiro T. Agnew Nathan)** | genius mechanic | Individual (**communicates only by arguing**) | Builds high-tech parts from junkyard scrap, about twice per race; lives off dismantling stockpiled wrecked Firebirds and spends half his time on the road hunting cheap third-generation Firebirds. Every team has fired him ("Sometimes within minutes of hiring him")—if you lose too fast, he switches sides and keeps arguing. Family history: his father Fred Nathan never forgave Nixon for resigning ("a quitter") until his death, and his son, born October 11, 1973, was named Spiro T. Agnew Nathan to scold the president |
| **Aurora Nathan** | crew chief (drives the transporter + a thousand odd jobs) | Individual (the only person who can argue with S.T.A.N. as long and as hard) | A fragile, shy exterior + "either fighting furiously – or making love violently" (thin motel walls) = the marital war itself is a renewable source of scenes |
| **Winfield Winfield (Dub)** | jack-of-all-trades / slacker / troublemaker | Individual (no sense of responsibility) | Last descendant of a prominent Georgia family (WW→Dub); his true identity is **the great-grandson of the longtime sponsor**, and the sponsorship agreement requires that he be given a job (but does not require that he do any work); he charms his way through everything; he has never felt anything strongly in his life—**except that he is madly in love with Jones** |
| **Jones** | assistant / driver | **The opportunist version of community** (rising by attaching herself to someone) | A follower of Trump / *The Apprentice*, she has memorized all his books and quote calendars and wants to see her name on the Manhattan skyline; her studies at NC State were interrupted when her ex-husband stole her student loan to build a mobile meth lab; she drives a taxi to repay the loan and refuses to make quick money from her looks ("a good girl with strong morals"). When Marshall gets in her cab it is as if "God has sent her an angel"—she wants to make herself indispensable and make the team a success so she can be taken back to New York. **She is starting to fall in love with him**: "She's the only one he's completely honest with … So why is he so hung up on Pet?" |
| **Elwood Winfield** | lead sponsor (owner of Winfield's Chew) | **Community in the old style of loyalty** | He has sponsored Jack since the Daytona beach-racing era (sponsorship = buying a tank of gas) and still does; the older he gets, the stranger and more demanding he becomes—every request for money comes with an eccentric condition (making Marshall mow his 40-acre lawn). Marshall wants to replace the lead sponsor, but Pet will not listen—"the flip side of her hatred of authority is a fierce loyalty to those who stand by her"; and Winfield **would pull his money out immediately if he ever got wind of it** |
| **Jack Stagger** | adviser ("wartime consigliere") | Individual (retired from the 1970s circuit when ABC's Wide World of Sports came along, on the grounds that it was "too commercial") | NASCAR's Zeus—everyone has heard of him and no one quite remembers why; his opinion = fact; even Pet is afraid of him; under the hard shell are a warm heart and a sharp mind, and Marshall has to endure hours of abuse before he can ask him for anything |
| **RAYMOND FESTER (villain)** | RFI chairman | **The extreme of individualism** (swallowing the entire sport) | "bought up every independent racer in the field"; not content with the largest team, **he wants to own the entire sport**, now backed by Barzun Keegan money, and he will steal, cheat, lie, and extort. Stagger is no direct threat—"It's much worse than that. Because they're a **symbol** – proof that you can stand up to Fester and win … a 180 mph billboard advertising RFI's impotence." Landmine: his father Hank Fester was also an independent driver and a friend of Pet's father; when the two children were young, the families joked about a marriage alliance, and "he'd still be just as happy to marry Pet as to wipe her out"; Hank went bankrupt in the mid-1990s → "only the strong and rich survive. He will never be weak – or let himself appear weak – again." |

**How to read this table**: none of the nine characters is a "functional supporting character"—every row fills all three cells at once (function, thematic position, landmine). Compare Section VI's Landau familial role library and Section VII's Story landmines item: Rabkin's appendix is the concrete proof of those two rules being executed at the same time.

### VIII.3 The Six Sample Story Areas (Key Points as Written) and What They Prove

1. Marshall finds a new corporate sponsor, and Elwood furiously withdraws his money; the new sponsor turns out to be an RFI trick—Marshall must win Winfield back before Pet discovers he has bankrupted the team.
2. On the 25th anniversary of their first meeting, Kyle proposes again and demands a date or his freedom; Pet refuses anything she is "asked" to do, and Kyle quits to join another team—Marshall must get Kyle back before the next race, and **the only way is to let the woman he is beginning to love marry someone else**.
3. Overnight in a small town on the way to a race, S.T.A.N. gets into a fight and lands in jail; Pet wants to break him out, Marshall insists on due process; Marshall argues before the judge—and ends up in the cell next door, and must get them both out before Pet launches a "daring and stupid jailbreak."
4. An old friend from Manhattan calls with a bank job at twice his salary; the condition is that Pet agrees to release him from his contract—she agrees, but only if he **first teaches Kyle to do his job**.
5. Three of Kyle's paychecks bounce, and he takes a job running moonshine across state lines; Marshall and Pet catch up to stop him; but Kyle fails to deliver, the moonshiners hold him hostage, and Marshall and Pet have to make the delivery for him…
6. The team is on the verge of bankruptcy, and Marshall arranges a bank loan through an old friend in New York; Pet is furious when she learns of it—she refuses to owe anyone anything and will quit otherwise; and she is right: the bank belongs to Barzun Keegan, the paper has already been sold to RFI, and if the loan is missed foreclosure follows immediately.

**The shape shared by all six (Rabkin's own analysis)**: crisis → **Marshall solves it with a deal / procedure / outside resource** → triggers Pet's anti-authority / anti-dependence stance or the team's individualism → **blowback** → Marshall must switch to a "community" approach (pulling people back into the team) to close it out. And each one engages both the A story (business) and the B story (triangle / relationships).

→ Three immediately usable tests: ① Is the **driver** of all six the protagonist? (If two or more are driven by supporting characters, your engine is actually an ensemble engine, and you need to rewrite the "weekly task sentence" in Section 7.); ② Can all six be summarized by a single story pattern? (If not, the pattern has not taken shape.); ③ Do all six touch both the A and B stories at once? (One that touches only one will become filler in the show.) Compare Blum ch17's **five-story test** (Section VII of this document): Blum checks that "the source and ending shape must not be too similar," while Rabkin checks that "the shape must be too similar"—**the two do not conflict: the shape (pattern) must be the same; the source material and endings must differ.**

### VIII.4 Document Rules Extracted from This Real-World Sample (Rabkin's Own Item 21)

Pitch document must contain: ① an Overview with multi-layered genre positioning (one selling point per line); ② **two-way** perspectives on the protagonist and the antagonist (two sections, not one); ③ a pilot synopsis (How We Got Here) that lands on the series' normal state rather than a climax; ④ a **two-column comparison of the world's losses and gains**; ⑤ a single "weekly task" sentence; ⑥ each supporting character's function + landmine; ⑦ a theme-driven villain; ⑧ **at least 5–6 Sample Story Areas proving the pattern can repeat**.

Alignment with the other books (differences preserved): Rabkin's ten sections give "the other side's perspective" its own section, **which none of Landau's, Oberg's, or Smith's field tables has**—relationship dramas and dual-protagonist shows should add it; conversely, Rabkin's ten sections **lack** a logline, an explicit theme section, a season arc, an episode logline table, and a package/budget section, all of which must be supplied from Oberg's twelve components (Section X) and Smith's eleven sections (Section IX).

## IX. Smith's Eleven-Section Series Format (the Selling Document for a Half-Hour Comedy, 10–15 Pages)

**Source**: Evan S. Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms* (2009), Chapter 20, "Creating a New Series," pp. 0292–0295. **Note on the year**: this chapter's market judgments ("most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot," the ratings arguments in the market analysis, new-media sizzle, WGA pay figures) are all **in the context of the 2009 American broadcast networks**; the mechanisms still hold, but the networks and numbers are outdated.

**Positioning**: series format = "a brief selling document that describes the series premise and hints at how future episodes would develop." Veteran writers sometimes pitch with a format instead of a pilot script, or bring both (pp. 0291–292). Length is **usually 10–15 pages, with plenty of white space** (excluding attachments such as articles that inspired the show, ratings data, or letters of intent from partners).

### IX.1 Field-by-Field Specifications for the Eleven Sections

> **For a small project, just follow this one sentence**: for a project with no track record, no star, no budget sheet, and no option, **write only Sections 1, 3, 4, 5, and 6** (five sections), and omit Sections 2, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 11 entirely—this is not laziness; it is this skill's own rule. Do not assume that because the checklist has eleven rows you are missing six sections.

| # | Section | Required/Optional | What to fill in (key points as written) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Title page** | Required | Show title, creator's name, contact information for the creator's rep (or the creator); format follows the title-page specification in Appendix A |
| 2 | **Opening sell copy** | **Optional** | A one-sentence logline, the protagonist's personal creed, a statement or question expressing the theme, or a quotation—the only purpose is to make people want to keep listening. Warning: **many formats omit this section, and if it is badly written it looks cutesy** |
| 3 | **Series premise description** | Required, **1–3 pages** | Characters, relationships, setting, time frame, story type, **the source of continuing conflict**. Exact words: "This is a tough section to write because it must introduce the show's universe... in an amusing and compelling fashion. And it must convince the reader that the show has **legs**, a premise rich enough to generate a hundred or more episodes down the road." Two techniques: ① you can **use the pilot's story to carry this information directly**, "disguising" it with comedy and drama; ② **in this section, introduce each character with a single phrase** and let their actions and dialogue define them (save detailed character descriptions for Section 6) |
| 4 | **Pilot story** | Conditionally required, **1–2 pages** | If Section 3 did not use the pilot story, supply it here. The reason: a real story demonstrates the premise, "it's the best way to prove that the series will actually be funny," and proves that you can write the show |
| 5 | **8–10 loglines/springboards for future episodes** | Required | Prove that it has legs and hook people with funny concepts. For a serial, rewrite this as **several major story arcs for Season 1**, or mix the two (thread the arcs through the springboard list) |
| 6 | **Regular character list** | Required | One paragraph per person, more for leads and less for supporting characters; **you must point out the key relationships that generate conflict and laughs (character mix)**. Explicit instruction: put detailed character descriptions in this section, **do not stuff them into Section 3** (it will sink that section) |
| 7 | **Market analysis** | Optional | Recent ratings performance of comparable shows plus the shows currently competing for the same audience. "It's best to keep this section very short and objective"; add it only when the numbers strongly support it; **some argue for leaving it out entirely**—dryness dilutes the excitement of the creative portion |
| 8 | **Budget summary** | Optional, **1–2 pages** | Provided by an experienced producer, or a cost estimate for the pilot/first batch of episodes; if there are money-saving production or co-financing arrangements, write them here |
| 9 | **Unique aspects** | Optional | A star already attached, a basis in published material (**that you have optioned**), a strong producer or financing partner. **Warning (2009 context, still valid)**: many people in the industry are eager to attach themselves to get work—"Do not assume that this (used-to-be-a-star) actor or that (working-his-way-up) producer will help you to make a sale"; if the person is not currently hot, **attaching them can sink your project** |
| 10 | **Credits of the creator and attached talent** | Optional | **If you have no track record and no names, omit the entire section** |
| 11 | **Closing sell copy** | Optional | "a corny closer can kill a sale" |

**Three general rules** (same page): ① This is only a blueprint; **the order and combination can be adjusted to the project**; ② **the format itself is a sample of your writing ability**—"keep those adjectives and adverbs at bay," and prove everywhere that the show is timely, unique, and **funny**: "you can't sell a sitcom with a document that reads like an annual report"; ③ length 10–15 pages, **with plenty of white space**.

### IX.2 How to Prove That "Legs = a Hundred Episodes" (Smith's Three Chains of Evidence)

1. **Section 3 makes the promise directly**: the premise description must "convince the reader that the show has legs, a premise rich enough to generate **a hundred or more episodes** down the road." That is: legs is not claimed with adjectives; it comes from writing the "source of continuing conflict" clearly in this section.
2. **Section 4 uses the pilot story as a sample**: a story actually written out proves that "the show is really funny" and "you can write it"—this is the **quality** proof of legs.
3. **Section 5 uses 8–10 springboards as proof of capacity**—this is the **quantity** proof of legs. The companion pilot-chapter criteria (pp. 0295–298): **proximity counts**—"you need your characters to keep bumping into each other so they can keep generating conflict and comedy in episode after episode"; you do not have to make another office/living-room show (single-cam allows roaming), but **the premise must contain some setting or dramatic device that regularly squeezes the characters together**.

**Set alongside the thresholds in the other books (they do not override one another)**: Smith ch20's "**a hundred or more episodes**" / Goldberg & Rabkin ch17's four questions, which likewise demand "**100 or more episodes**" (Section VII of this document) / Landau 1e Tip 7's **six-episode test** / Landau 2e ch14's **three** prepared pitch ideas, and Blum ch17's **five or six** for a presentation / Rabkin ch6's **99 stories**. How to read the three tiers together: **99/100 episodes is the engine's design standard, 5–10 is the document's delivery standard, and 3 is the live standard for an oral pitch.**

### IX.3 How to Write the 8–10 Springboards

- **Format**: for a half-hour comedy, write a logline or springboard, not a synopsis; the criterion is "**hook people with a funny concept**"—the concept itself must be funny in one line, not funny only in execution.
- **The serial's alternative**: replace this section with **several major story arcs for Season 1**, or mix the two—thread the arcs through the springboard list (i.e., "this springboard in Episode 3 also advances which step of the arc").
- **A real-world example**: the **14 episode loglines** for *B-List* in Miller's appendix are a filled-in sample of this section (see Section XII, item 8), and Miller says explicitly that it was the most successful part of the whole draft—"He knocked it out of the park with his proposed episode loglines. They made me laugh, and I totally got Henry's show."; the last one has only a title (*Attack of the Giant Clam*.), and the title itself is the joke.
- **Another generation method**: Jeffries's "ten anxious questions at the end of the pilot → ten short synopses for future episodes" (Section IV, item 40 of this document)—questions first, springboards derived from the questions.

### IX.4 Where Smith Differs from the Other Books (Differences Preserved Side by Side)

| Point of dispute | Smith ch20 (2009) | The other side | How to use it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whether to open with sell copy | Optional, and "many formats omit it"; badly written it turns cutesy | Oberg 3.1, component 3: the Series Overview may include an **opening statement** (the creator's personal connection to the subject, "a discrete way to tie yourself to the show"), which can stand as its own section | If there is a genuine personal connection, write it as an opening statement (not ad copy); if not, omit it |
| Whether to write market analysis | Optional, extremely short and objective; "some argue for leaving it out entirely" | Among Miller's six appendix samples, only *Minotaur* has a **Markets** section; the argument formula = the target platform's existing tone + the gap it lacks (Section XII, item 5) | If the numbers are not strong, do not write it; if you do, write about the "platform gap," not a ratings table |
| Which section characters go in | **A separate Section 6**; Section 3 gives each character only a phrase | Oberg: Character Breakdowns, 3–5 leads at ≤1 page each; Douglas 3e ch2: one page per person, only a few people; Landau: introduce characters embedded in the story, do not list them | The hard rule all three agree on is "**do not stuff a character list into the overview**"; the disagreement is only about the length of the detailed character paragraphs |
| premise pilot or typical-episode pilot | **"most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot"**; a premise pilot cannot demonstrate an ordinary episode ("that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week") and repeats poorly | Landau: not a trap; just say in the pitch which kind it is | See the four criteria in Section IV, item 37 of this document; for half-hour comedy, follow Smith |
| Whether a format can replace a pilot script | A veteran writer can; you can also bring both | Oberg 3, First Up: **people without a track record are strongly advised to write the pilot first**, and to prepare a written version they can leave behind; Landau: "write your pilot scripts on spec"; the opposing view, Aranya: "a script is also 60 pages of reasons to say no" | No track record → write the script first; with a track record → format first |

### IX.5 How This Document Interfaces with the Live Pitch (pp. 0299–0302, 2009 Context)

- **When pitching a single episode, bring a long list of springboards; when pitching a new series, bring only that one big idea, plus one or two backup new-series ideas.** When they like the big idea, **never pitch the backups** ("you'll confuse matters and kill the sale"); only if the big idea is rejected do you throw out the backups one by one; if all are rejected, **ask what kind of show they want** and go home and develop according to their needs.
- When the project is based on existing material or a true story and **you have secured the rights**, you can bring the book/article/video to the meeting (this is relevant visual support, not a gimmick). Companion hard rule: **"You can't sell what you don't own."**—option or buy the rights before adapting; the first question producers and executives ask about an adaptation project is always "Do you own the rights?", and if you cannot answer, "you'll look like an amateur."
- Register the work with the Copyright Office or the WGA before submitting it—"Unlike an ordinary spec, this is an entirely original work." (Compare Miller ch10's reverse detail: **do not put a registration number or copyright notice on the title page.**)
- **Pay (2009–2011 WGA figures, now outdated, for order-of-magnitude reference only)**: for a half-hour prime-time sitcom on the four major networks or a pay cable channel (HBO, Showtime), the **minimum pilot script fee = 150% of the minimum episode fee**—2009/5/2–2010/5/1: **$33,349.50**; 2010/5/2–2011/5/1: **$34,350.00**; additional rewrites are negotiated separately at **$5,000–$10,000**; the writer is usually hired as one of the pilot's producers, a fee outside WGA jurisdiction of **$10,000–$50,000 or more**; if the show goes to series there is a bonus, guaranteed episodes, a **"Created By" credit and a per-episode royalty**, possibly net profit participation ("though few series ever see profits"), and residuals. Basic cable is a little more than half the network rate; non-WGA members + non-signatory networks = "everything's up for grabs."
