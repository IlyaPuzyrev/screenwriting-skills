## X. Oberg's Twelve Bible Components, Item by Item, and the Difference Between Bible / Pitch Deck / Format

**Source**: Emmanuel Oberg, *Writing a Successful TV Series* (2023, Screenplay Unlimited, Volume 3 of the Story-Type Method), Chapter 3, Project Development, "3 First Up" and "3.1 Creating an Irresistible Bible," subsection "Bible Components"; term definitions are taken from the same book's Glossary (see section 1, "Series Forms and Industry Terms," of the fifth set of notes in this pack); the compressed version of the twelve specifications is Rule 19 of the 20 operating rules at the end of Chapter 3. **Year caveat**: this is the 2023 European and American streaming context — "streaming favors straight-to-series," Netflix commissioning a pilot for a single-camera comedy for the first time in twelve years, Amazon letting viewers vote for Transparent, the rise of FAST channels — and these **market facts will date**; page counts and field specifications are craft and do not change with the year.

### X.1 First, Distinguish Three Words: bible / pitch deck / format

The three are often used interchangeably in the industry. Oberg's approach is **to admit first that there is no standard, then to ask the other party in the meeting which one they mean** — "there is no industry standard, especially internationally … make sure that you clarify what they mean." (3 First Up)

| Word | Oberg's definition | What it contains | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bible** | A document containing characters, setting, theme, tone, style, structure, and storylines; used **both** to pitch producers/networks/studios **and** to guide writers and the showrunner during production (Glossary §1) | Usually a logline, introduction, per-episode summaries, breakdowns of the main characters, a description of the story world, and a vision for future seasons | Throughout the whole process; also called **format / book / TV treatment / pitch document / proposal** |
| **Pitch Deck** | "**a more visual version of a bible**" (Glossary §1); not a separate component but the visual version of a bible — **the same sections, fewer words, more images, often landscape format** (Components §12) | Synopsis, characters, a handful of plot scenes, target audience, production details, creative team | "a no-brainer" when there is a strong visual element (comic-book adaptation, spectacular locations, a distinctive style); can accompany a pilot script or treatment |
| **Format** | **Three distinct meanings** (Glossary §1): (1) in industry shorthand, just **episode length + broad genre** (most commonly one-hour drama, half-hour comedy); (2) the **underlying concept/rights** bought when adapting a foreign series or IP — the US versions of The Office and The Bridge both bought the format from British/Danish rights holders; (3) some people call the **pitching bible** a format and reserve "bible" for the reference document continuously updated after pickup | Depends on the meaning | Rule 1 of the book's 20 rules requires that the first step of development label three things: series type, story-type, and **format (episode length + broad genre)** |

**A real-world example**: the benchmark pitch deck is the one the Duffer brothers made for **Montauk** (later Stranger Things) — it contained a moodboard and a list of inspirational reference films, and played the retro card with the typeface of Stephen King novels from the same period; the distributor later released the Blu-ray in a fake VHS box, **the same visual strategy running from the pitch all the way to the sale to viewers** (Components §12, Glossary §1). Oberg also recommends studying six real pitch bibles: **Stranger Things, Fargo, Grey's Anatomy, Lost, New Girl, True Detective** (introduction to 3.1).

**One more layer: the same bible has three forms (3 First Up)**; before writing, decide which one you are making. ① **For an oral pitch** (the typical US path: pitch first → commission a pilot script → shoot the pilot → decide whether to order a series, at which point **there may still be no pilot script**): only the arcs of the first few seasons plus a handful of episode loglines; **the oral pitch runs 15–20 minutes or less**, and "anyone without a track record is strongly advised to write the pilot first and prepare a written version they can leave behind." ② **For straight-to-series** (common with streamers and in Europe): the first episode has usually already been written on spec, a serial comes with arcs for several seasons, and **synopses (at minimum loglines) for every episode of the first season**; **the written pitch bible is 10–30 pages (excluding the script), with roughly 15 pages as the sweet spot**. ③ **The reference document after pickup**: it grows from the pitch bible into a living document, continuously updated by an assistant/coordinator, length not recorded — Oberg notes that "many people in the industry call **only** this a bible."

### X.2 The Twelve Components, Item by Item (Points as Written)

(Source: 3.1, Bible Components. Entries marked **hard spec** are those with a number or a prohibition and can be used directly as a delivery checklist.)

| # | Component | Hard spec | How to write it (points as written) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Series Title** | **≤3 words**; together with the author's name, the **only mandatory item in the whole document** | "an up-to-three-word pitch" — on the Netflix wall, paired with a single image, it delivers the first hook; it should encapsulate tone and concept and **ideally suggest conflict**. Good examples: Sex Education, Breaking Bad, Occupied, Killing Eve, Stranger Things, Fleabag, Squid Game. **Ironic/punning titles are riskier**: Happy Valley (a valley awash with drugs), Transparent (trans parent), Life in Pieces. The title page may include an introduction or a table of contents |
| 2 | **Series Logline** | **Three lines maximum** | For plot-led / character-led shows, state clearly **who wants or needs what and why**, the obstacle, and the cost of failure; with theme-led or ensemble shows this is "often impossible," but it remains the core selling point at any stage. The accompanying real example (3.2, Killing Eve): the official logline "…two fiercely intelligent women, equally obsessed with each other, go head to head in an epic game of cat and mouse." — it contains genre, premise, and franchise, original characters, and abundant conflict; "**the DNA of the series lies in these few lines.**" |
| 3 | **Series Overview / One-Sheet** | **Two things are forbidden**: ① **it is not a pilot synopsis**; ② **do not use terms like story-type** | What you write is the world and the **story spine** (is it a quest? a transformative arc? a social problem?); it may include the format (comedy/drama + episode count and length) and the series type; it may include an **opening statement** (the creator's personal connection to the subject — "a discrete way to tie yourself to the show," which can stand as its own section); state the time, place, genre, tone, theme, context, and main characters (brief, castable). The reason for banning jargon is that "readers with different theoretical reference points will drive the discussion into a dead end" — the Stranger Things and Fargo bibles stay plain even when they touch on such matters |
| 4 | **Character Breakdowns** | **3–5 protagonists at no more than one page each; less for everyone else** | Name + approximate age + want/need, motivation, background, main traits — "**convey their essence**," unique yet relatable. The test: "there is no charismatic actor on the page to save them"; the goal is "**we won't tire of seeing them week after week, even if they never change**," with **clarity, complexity and originality** |
| 5 | **Story World** | No page rule; **can be folded into the character section** | Give it its own section only when the world is complex/distinctive — for a sitcom it may be just a description of the main sets; you can discuss rules, production design, costumes, geography, and scale. The boundary line: "**You're brushing the big picture, not briefing the art department.**" |
| 6 | **Pilot Story** | **A complete synopsis, 3–5 pages** | **Tell the whole story**, not just the beginning; adjust to local convention |
| 7 | **Arc of the First Season (and Possibly More)** | **The first-season arc is mandatory**; **every season arc must end on a hook / cliffhanger** (except procedural, limited, and anthology) | A serial may sketch what follows. **Unless you are adapting a well-known IP, later seasons need not be detailed** — veteran showrunners often develop in blocks of 5–6 episodes to absorb change; casting and audience reaction shift the direction (#JusticeforBarb affected Stranger Things S2), "**all you have is the current season**" (2023 example) |
| 8 | **Arc of the Series** | **Not needed** for a procedural/sitcom; optional for a serial | Writing one can demonstrate command, but "**Showing rigidity regarding what might happen in Season 5 … is more likely to trigger alarm bells**." Alternative: recast it as "the themes, characters, and stories you want to explore in the future" |
| 9 | **Tone, Genre and Style** | **The bible's prose style must match the show's genre** (a comedy must be funny; a thriller needs suspenseful moments) | A straightforward sitcom does not need this section; shows like The Office / Fleabag must explain **how** the mockumentary and the fourth-wall break **generate** comedy and drama. This is also where you can write the target audience — the US commonly uses the brackets kids 6–11, teens 12–17, adults 18–34 / 18–49 / 25–54 / 55+ (**2023 context; brackets change with the market**). Closing line: "**Sell the sizzle, not the sausage.**" |
| 10 | **Episode Loglines or Synopses** | **A serial usually has 8–15 loglines**; a mini/limited can have as few as 2 but with more detailed synopses; a well-developed bible runs about **one page** of synopsis per episode (the Grey's Anatomy bible is the example); a sitcom/procedural gives only a few to demonstrate the franchise | The warning: "**Having to write dozens of loglines or synopses might not be a good sign…**" — if the world and characters are rich enough and the franchise and springboard are clear, readers can imagine unlimited episodes for themselves |
| 11 | **Package** | No page rule; **do not name actors** unless (1) you are sure you can get them or (2) it is a plus for that particular buyer | Non-story elements such as producers, creator, showrunner, writers, cast, budget, visuals, and moodboard. Using a well-known actor as a "**casting example**" to help the buyer imagine the show is acceptable |
| 12 | **Pitch Deck** | Not a separate component | See X.1 |

### X.3 The Overall Length Threshold and the Case for "Don't Overwrite"

- **The whole document is 10–30 pages, with about 15 as the sweet spot** (3 First Up, form ②); the oral version runs **15–20 minutes or less**, and elsewhere the book gives a dual-use formulation, "a 15–25 minute oral pitch or a written bible / pitch deck" (note to the Components subsection).
- The **structural reason** not to overfill (3.1, question 9, Are You Overplanning or Boxing Yourself In?): you do not know how long the show will run, whom the audience will love, or what chemistry the actors will have (**Jesse Pinkman was originally meant to die in the first season**; the Cranston/Paul chemistry made Gilligan change his plan). The quota line: **"Put 90% of your energy into making the best first season … and 90% of that energy into making your pilot the most exciting episode."** Another point at the character level (3.1, question 8): **do not imply that we already know the endpoint of the character arc** (except in a limited series) — "**In series, often characters don't change that much, it's how the audience perceives them that changes.**"
- How the twelve components complement the **Rabkin ten-section** table (Section VIII) and the **Smith eleven-section** table (Section IX): Oberg alone has **Series Title ≤3 words**, **Package**, **Pitch Deck**, and **Arc of the Series**; Rabkin alone has **a separate section for the antagonist's perspective** and **a two-column loss list**; Smith alone has **Market analysis**, **Budget summary**, **Unique aspects**, **creator credits**, and **Closing sell copy**. The only things all three share are **title / logline (Rabkin has no logline section) / overview / characters / pilot story / episode logline or springboard** — **these six items are the minimum deliverable set**.

### X.4 Rule 19 of the 20 Rules in Chapter 3 (a One-Line Compression of the Bible Spec, Transcribed as Written)

> bible: title ≤3 words, logline ≤3 lines, overview neither retells the pilot nor uses story-type jargon, 3–5 protagonists at ≤1 page each, pilot synopsis 3–5 pages, a mandatory first-season arc ending on a hook, later seasons sketched only as direction, 8–15 episode loglines for a serial; the whole document 10–30 pages (sweet spot 15); prose style matching the show's genre.

**The prerequisite rule that must be read alongside it** (Rule 1 of the 16 at the end of the book's Glossary section): **the first step of development is to label three things — series type, story-type, and format (episode length + broad genre); if any one of the three is vague, do not write an outline yet**, let alone a bible. For the reason, see Section III, Rule 46: **"The wrong combination of series type and story-type is the root cause of many series failures"**.

## XI. Two Real Writers' Guidelines from Goldberg & Rabkin (Mini-Bibles for Outside Writers)

**Source**: Lee Goldberg & William Rabkin, *Successful Television Writing* (2003), Appendix B, *Diagnosis Murder* Writers' Guidelines (**p0155–p0158**), and Appendix C, *Martial Law* Writers' Guidelines (**p0159–p0168**); the methodology is set out earlier in Appendix A, *Plotting a Mystery* (**p0149–p0153**). **Year caveat**: this is the 2003 US broadcast-network one-hour context — the VHS-era fairness argument, the four-act commercial format, the economics of shooting days and standing sets, and the "$7.50 movie ticket" all belong to that year; the document form itself — **a tone statement + a list of prohibitions + a character-tension web** — remains valid today.

**Positioning**: these two are not documents for selling a show; they are **documents sent by an already-picked-up series to freelancers invited to pitch**, and they are the only **real mini-bibles** in this file (compare the page-count table in Section I: a mini-bible is about 5 pages; DM is four pages, ML ten, **ML is longer because it is a second-season reboot**). Compared with Rabkin's Mooresville pitch in Section VIII, **the two sets of fields barely overlap** — a pitch document sells possibility; guidelines sell enforceable boundaries.

### XI.1 Comparison of the Two Documents' Section Structure

| Section | *Diagnosis Murder* (structure = basic principles → characters → two hardest scene types → bottom line) | *Martial Law* (structure = positioning statement → list of principles → character overview → character detail → common ground) |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive positioning** | "**We are a mystery series. We always have a murder.** And usually there is a medical clue that plays a major role in solving the crime. **But that's it. Beyond that, we have no formula.**" | "action-adventure comedy," offering the thrill of "until now, you had to pay $7.50 to see" |
| **Negative positioning** | Did not want to be **Murder, She Wrote**; the bottom-line section says outright, "we don't want to be an old, tired Murder, She Wrote knockoff again — and back then people were right to see us that way" | "**This is not a cop show.**"; "Our stories are not about crimes, suspects, and clues, they are about **fun, comedy, and pure adrenaline**." |
| **Tone reference points** | **Not recorded** (only negative reference points given) | A homage to the 1960s — **The Wild Wild West, I Spy, The Avengers, The Man from U.N.C.L.E.** — with a contemporary twist and "martial arts that only Sammo Hung and Stanley Tong can deliver"; it wants the wildest action of the best Hong Kong films plus the character comedy of **48 Hours** and **Lethal Weapon** |
| **Continuity / reboot statement** | A note on the star: Dick Van Dyke is versatile, and they encourage you to use that | "**last season didn't exist.**" No backstory is carried over; **only the central concept is kept**: a police officer from China working at the LAPD through an exchange program |
| **List of principles** | Fairness (see XI.2, item 1); per-season quotas and variations; clue preferences | Ten items: Start hot / Heroes in conflict / Stakes / no formula for the martial arts / a major turn at the end of Act Two / crimes are not grim / Sammo outside his own environment / Terrell no longer has his gun kicked away / no reliance on the medical examiner / not every villain knows martial arts |
| **Structural requirements** | Each season should do several classic whodunits ("a murder, four suspects, one of them did it") plus several **Columbo-style open mysteries**, **but each must find a unique twist to avoid formula** | "**Each story should have a major turn at the end of Act Two**—and if you can find major turns for the ends of Acts One and Three, we won't object."; "**If we know the whole story in Act One, then it's not a story we should be doing.**" |
| **Production parameters** | Scripts **68–70 pages**; **Mark Sloan appears in no more than 32–35 pages** (Van Dyke's work schedule); **7 shooting days**; at least **2–3 days on standing sets** (the hospital, the police station, Mark's beach house); they like action but have no budget | **Not recorded** (this document gives no page count or shooting days, only equivalent prohibitions: crimes are not grim, not every villain knows martial arts) |
| **Character section** | 3 people in separate paragraphs (Mark / Amanda / Jesse), each with profession + family relation + one emotional gap | Two layers: **character overview** (4 people + unit positioning) + **The Nitty-Gritty character detail** (a long paragraph per person, including a list of cultural assumptions and a comparison table) |
| **The two hardest scene types** | Yes, two separate sections: **Cat-and-Mouse Scenes** and **Our Big Finales** (XI.4) | **Not recorded** (the equivalent content goes into the principles "no formula for the martial arts" and "Heroes in conflict") |
| **Ending** | **The Bottom Line**: over two seasons they worked to change industry and public perception and succeeded (critical praise, **entering the top thirty in the ratings**) | **Common ground**: "Despite their differences, they genuinely like and respect each other, and together make an extraordinary team." |

### XI.2 "The List of Things Never to Touch" (Thirteen Prohibitions, Item by Item as Written)

This is the core asset that distinguishes guidelines from a pitch bible: **a pitch bible says what there can be; guidelines say what there will never be.**

| # | Prohibition | Source | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Never hide clues from the audience**; Mark never discovers the incriminating thing during a commercial or off-screen | DM | "**Assume the viewers are taping the show and when it's over, will rewind the tape to see what clues they missed.**" (VHS context; still holds if you substitute pause/replay). Appendix A's counter-note: on television "most mysteries … do it all the time," because cheating is easier |
| 2 | **Never** catch the culprit with fingerprints, DNA, or "easy forensic stuff" | DM; same in ML | They prefer to convict through **behavior, habit, personal quirks**; "Usually it's a combination of a medical clue, a behavior, and a mistake." |
| 3 | **Do not revive old bits from The Dick Van Dyke Show or his films** | DM | This is a new character |
| 4 | **Do not let cat-and-mouse scenes slide into Columbo imitation** | DM | Mark has his own style and humor — "**he's Dick Van Dyke, after all**" |
| 5 | **The big finale cannot be a Mark monologue**; "What are you talking about?" / "You're crazy" / "This is ridiculous" are not allowed as filler | DM | These lines "reveal nothing about the character, advance the story in no way, and do not shake Mark in the slightest" |
| 6 | **"Terrell will never, ever have a gun kicked out of his hand again."** | ML | Do not elevate the martial artist by making the other leads look stupid, weak, or incompetent |
| 7 | **Crimes must not be ugly or grim** — this is not a show where a child dies in a drive-by or a pregnant woman is raped | ML | "**This show is an escape, not a dark reality. If it's not fun, we don't do it.**" |
| 8 | Stakes **do not rely on "family or friends as victims/villains,"** nor on someone being arrested and facing death every week | ML | "**That's the easy way out.**" (but every story must have stakes for the protagonist) |
| 9 | **No formula for the martial arts**: not every act needs to end with a fight, the show need not end on a fight, and not every episode needs to close with Sammo dueling the villain; **the protagonist can lose, and should lose** | ML | Let the story decide where the fight goes, with whom, and how long; it must not be "irrelevant, tacked on or obligatory" |
| 10 | **Do not put Sammo back in Chinatown** | ML | Helping Chinese people in Chinatown is "neither funny nor fresh"; **helping Hasidic Jews in the Fairfax district is far more interesting than helping a Chinese-restaurant family** |
| 11 | **Not everyone in Los Angeles knows martial arts — especially not the villains** | ML | "**It is only interesting when he faces a villain with an Uzi, a bomb, or a tank bare-handed.**" |
| 12 | **Sammo does not use handcuffs or guns** | ML | Substitutes: rubber bands around the thumbs, a stick wrapped around the limbs, a man tied around a lamppost; **minimum necessary force** — "the Chinese MacGyver of self-defense" |
| 13 | **"If the story you have in mind can be done on any other show, then it's dead wrong for Martial Law."** | ML | The master rule behind all the prohibitions: **interchangeability = failure** |

### XI.3 The Protagonist's "License to Intervene" (Written as an Institution)

Section III, Rule 49 (Miller) gives the test: **"A character's agency completes the story engine"** — a journalist can meddle but has **no license to intervene**. The value of these two guidelines is that they **write that license as a checkable job description**:

- ***Diagnosis Murder***: **Dr. Mark Sloan** = head of internal medicine at Community General Hospital + **LAPD consultant**; his son **Steve** (homicide detective) **lives with him in the Malibu beach house**; **Dr. Amanda Bentley** = pathologist and **assistant county medical examiner**, **often arriving at the murder scene before Mark/Steve**; **Dr. Jesse Travis** = emergency-room resident, Mark's "second son," "an apprentice at solving crimes." → Three things are institutionalized at once: ① the protagonist has a legitimate reason to be present (consultant status); ② bodies and medical evidence flow legitimately into the protagonist's hands (medical examiner's assistant); ③ police power lives in the same house (father and son under one roof). So the "medical clue" is not a coincidence but a product of the job — Appendix A's companion principle: **"The medical clue comes out of character."**
- ***Martial Law***: **Sammo Law** = a Shanghai police officer, **seconded to the LAPD through an exchange program**, attached to an elite major crime unit that "handles high-profile and international cases and **operates outside LAPD bureaucracy**"; the unit's positioning is "for people who don't fit in anywhere else in the department, but are **too damn good to let go**," and it **does not merely react but actively prevents** — undercover work, cons, traps. → The license provides three things: ① he can appear on any case; ② he can break procedure; ③ **he can go looking for trouble** (the thing a procedural most lacks: a reason the protagonist does not wait for a case to come to him). A bonus: his exchange-program status makes him **permanently a fish out of water**.

**An executable test**: break the protagonist's license into four questions — **① On what grounds can he legitimately be present? ② On what grounds do evidence/cases flow into his hands? ③ On what grounds can he break procedure? ④ On what grounds can he take the initiative instead of waiting?** If any one of the four can only be answered by coincidence, the engine is missing a piece.

### XI.4 The Two "Hardest Scenes to Write Each Episode," and How DM Handles Them

- **Cat-and-Mouse Scenes** (the investigative scene in which Mark questions a suspect) = "**the hardest scene to write in each episode, and usually the best**." Mark must extract a great deal of information, yet must do it "**almost effortlessly, without Mark seeming to 'question' the person**" — getting the other person to talk **without realizing they are being interrogated**; he never behaves like an accuser or an interrogator, **only like someone who is interested**. This is "Mark's charm and his greatest investigative weapon" (for the prohibition, see XI.2, item 4).
- **Our Big Finales** (the closing explanation of the clues) are "always hard to write," but should be the show's **most satisfying scenes** — Mark enjoys them, "it's probably the reason why he's a detective at all." The method: dramatize (or comicize) the clues and the explanation; the key line is **"We need to make these finales a dialogue, not a speech. Mark is talking with, not to, the suspect."** The specific technique is to **give the killer an attitude** — rebut the accusation, point out the holes in Mark's reasoning, and let Mark then outmatch him; otherwise it is "just a lecture."

### XI.5 The Eight-Section Template Distilled from the Two Real Documents (This Pack's Synthesis, Not an Original Table of Contents in the Book)

① **Positive positioning + negative positioning**; ② **tone reference points**; ③ **a show-specific list of prohibitions** (XI.2); ④ **structural requirements**; ⑤ **production parameters**; ⑥ **characters + a two-way tension vector for every relationship**; ⑦ **how to write the two hardest scene types** (XI.4); ⑧ **the bottom line and the invitation**. The most copyable part of item ⑥ is the **two-way vector** — for each relationship, write one "admires / can't stand" trait and **write both directions** (Grace envies Amy's political adroitness; Amy envies Grace's ability to fit into any situation, since she herself is "**all cop**"; the Sammo↔Terrell comparison table closes on "**Terrell improvises / Sammo plans**"), while making it clear that **the conflict is not personal** (the paradigm cases: Kirk/Spock/McCoy, Murtaugh/Riggs). → Read against Landau's library of familial role assignments in Section VI and Rabkin's "It Goes the Other Way, Too …" in Section VIII.

**Conclusion (the delivery baseline)**: the only five things everyone has are **type of show / logline / world / characters / story engine**; tone, theme, season plan, and the six questions are the parts you "thicken as the project requires." *The Guardian* uses world-building to swallow three cells — theme / engine / tone (Miller's comment is precisely "skillfully focuses on world-building") — **a fantasy series is allowed to hide its engine in the rules of the world, but then it must write the engine into the world**: its engine is that the guardian is reborn after death to maintain the balance, that the **seeker crystal** in each country's tower catches a beam from the sky to locate someone, that identification is by the eyes, and that destructive magic **costs health, even life**.

### XII.3 *Jerusalem, TN*: Two Story Models + a Three-Season Power-Progression Table

The overall formula: "The story in this show comes from **collisions between institutions and individuals.**" Institutions = the church, the SBC, the factory farm, the new cult, the town itself. **Model 1** = Luke or Ann feels threatened by an institution, or sees a way to get what they need through an institution, and acts; **model 2** = a conflict between two institutions puts the protagonist **dead center** ("solve it, intensify it, or be destroyed by it"). It also designates an **emotional anchor character**: Kacey "is **the human center of the show**."

| Season | Plot scale | Can | Cannot / cost |
|---|---|---|---|

### XII.4 *Girl Out of Water*: Egri's Three Dimensions + a Signature Trait Form

**Format**: each character gets **three paragraphs — Physiology / Sociology / Psychology — ending with a line beginning "Signature trait:"**. The full table of Egri's character bone structure fields is at L0785–850 (Physiology: three items / Sociology: nine / Psychology: eleven). The Signature trait prompt: **"What does the protagonist do every week that makes the audience love watching?"** — with the note that "not every protagonist has one."

| Steven (father) | "his **immaturity and obvious lack of business sense** can be infuriating. His relationship with Sierra is one of the most complex on the show." |

**The other three cells**: **World is only three lines** ("Sierra's school and family home, as well as the offices of her physical therapist and psychologist."); **the engine is one sentence with four sources** ("sports and sporting events, the mental-health system, business ventures, and family"); **the three-season arc advances three character threads simultaneously each season** (Sierra / Felicity / Steven-Maria) and flips their status once at the end of each season (S1: attempted suicide; S2: the patent lawsuit; S3: **Maria discovers her cancer has returned and hides it**).

### XII.5 *Minotaur*: The Only Markets / Platform Analysis

**Points as written**: the best fit is **FX** — it already has "moody, violent, prestige" shows like *American Horror Story*, *The Americans*, *Sons of Anarchy*, *Justified*, and *Fargo*, **but no superhero show**, so this would fill the gap; it also airs reruns of *Buffy the Vampire Slayer*, which is not superhero but has a similar protagonist and story structure. **HBO** would also work (it likewise has no superhero show and promises sex / violence / prestige); **Showtime, A&E, and even NBC** are possible too.

→ **The argument formula = a list of the target platform's existing tone + the one slot it currently lacks + backup platforms**, and **never a ratings number throughout**. This is exactly the solution for the "should you write a market analysis" cell in Section IX, IX.4: if the numbers are not strong, do not write them; if you do write, write the **platform gap**. Two other copyable moves in this sample: **open directly into the story instead of writing the fields** ("The minotaur waits in the center of the labyrinth… **Leon will not survive.**"), and **engine = four sources + a weekly promise sentence** ("Every week, we get to see Leon as the Minotaur…").

### XII.6 *The Guardian* and *Exit Stage Left*

*The Guardian*'s order is **world first → subsections by country → a separate Magic section → characters last**; an example of the three dimensions building a relationship web: Myron has a scar on his face from the time **Sargon** refused to join, **Sargon is his brother**, and **Terra is his niece**. *Exit Stage Left*'s **engine is written in two layers**: "**Each new show brings a fresh round of building/directing/prop-finding challenges.**" plus Georgia's need to balance the theater against her biology coursework; and it **sells "time compression" as a feature**: "the same students would be in college for between six and eight TV seasons." Miller points out that what it lacks is precisely **an ongoing character conflict and contemporary social relevance**.

### XII.7 Criteria for Filling In the Six Questions

**unique** compares against predecessors and states the difference; **research** writes out specific findings **and explains how they will be planted** (*B-List* studies Roger Corman's three moves — provocative titles and posters / reused sets and small budgets / hiring talented young actors by offering them experience and lead roles); **influences** names what is borrowed from each work; **why me** offers a perspective others do not have **and acknowledges the difficulty of the subject** (*Jerusalem, TN*: "**I stand at a distance from faith, but I am not alienated by it or embittered toward it.**").

### XII.8 *B-List*: 14 Episode Loglines Instead of a Season Plan

Miller's own words: **"He knocked it out of the park with his proposed episode loglines. They made me laugh, and I totally got Henry's show."** — this is the **filled-in sample** for the Section IX, IX.3 cell (Smith's 8–10 springboards), and the only evidence that "a comedy can use 13–14 episode loglines in place of a season plan."

**Three points of format**: ① **the Type field states the episode count and the mode together** — "a single camera half-hour sitcom with **13 episodes per season**. The show is **procedural in that every episode will revolve around one of the films being made**, but there is also room for character-driven stories within that setting."; ② **every logline names both a film-within-the-show and a character who is stuck** (forcing them into the same shape; compare Rabkin's six in Section VIII, VIII.3); ③ **World turns the in-show budget into a generator of out-of-show locations** — "the opportunity to travel to different locations **depending on the fictional film's budget**."

### XIII.1 Why This Table Is "Sustainable" Rather Than One-Off

1. **The seven wants collide directly, pairwise, so no separate conflict design is needed.** S1 notes, item 6, as written: **"Give every main character a small want they can achieve within the episode, and make it collide directly with someone else's want; an ensemble show does not need extra conflict design — just put the seven wants in one room."**
2. **All the wants require the same indivisible thing** (Logan's position), so **there is no win-win solution**; Shiv's want is self-contradictory (win outside the family on merit + keep the Roy leverage), so she is necessarily always standing on both sides.
3. **The gap in need guarantees that no one exits by getting their want.** The S1 need column records, item by item, "what the series thinks he lacks": Logan = to admit he will die / Kendall = to exist without his father's approval / Roman = to grow up / Shiv = to choose a side / Tom = to admit he wants power, not love / Greg = to admit he is in fact very good at calculating / Connor = to accept that he is not at the table — **not one of the seven is satisfied, so the wants can run unchanged for another season**.
4. **"They cannot leave one another" is written physically.** S1's pressure-cooker episodes (E2 the hospital, E5 the Thanksgiving apartment, E8 the warehouse party, E9/E10 the castle) each come with a communications restriction (**phone-collection bags**, a phone check desk + signal blocking + a falcon), and the pattern is stated as written: **"First restrict the flow of information, then make information travel by body."** S4 escalates to the container episode: 8 of the 10 episodes confine the entire episode to a single container with a fixed procedure, **procedure nodes replacing act breaks**.
5. **The chain of humiliation generates scenes automatically.** S1 notes, item 13: **"Humiliation must travel downward — whoever receives a humiliation must pass it on to someone weaker in the next scene (Logan → Roman → Greg)."**

### XIII.2 When This Web Fails: The Engine-Pressure Test

S4 ended not because the characters were finished — **"the motor in the room was still running hot"** — but because **"the big plot engine, the brutal materialist plumbing which ran under the show, was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure"** (S4 foreword). Three supporting pieces of evidence: ① the outside world supplies a deadline (legacy media versus tech "**was not a fair fight, it was a rout**"; Redstone's death, Murdoch's fade-out — **2023 industry facts that will date**); ② one of the four immovable anchors is **at the world level**: the GoJo deal "**had to go through**," on the grounds that "**That was the case in the world, so it had to be the case for us.**"; ③ the ending is derived from world logic, not character desire — the children "arrive at the wrong time."

→ **The full use of this section's table**: after filling in the seven rows, ask one S4-style question — **"In which season will the material engine driving these seven wants (the deal, the empire, the job, sources of cases) begin to lose pressure?"** However well the seven wants interlock, when the engine loses pressure you can only change engines or end (compare SKILL.md Rule 10 and Exercise 11).
