# Series Engine & Bible

> **The output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terms back to their original wording (see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md)); do not invent translations.

It governs four layers: **the engine** (why there is a story every week), **the character web** (where conflict arises automatically), **pilot types and strategy**, and **documents** (logline → pitch → treatment → bible).

**What it does not cover**: act structure within an episode or season, page counts, the typology of act outs and cliffhangers (→ `sw-series-structure`); writers'-room process and pitch-meeting etiquette (→ `sw-writers-room`); the mechanics of jokes and character configuration in half-hour comedy (→ `sw-sitcom-comedy`); Chinese-series proposal documents, per-episode outlines, episode counts, and censorship (→ `sw-chinese-series-practice`). Medium-agnostic material such as premise, controlling idea, three-dimensional character, and dialogue is not rewritten here (→ `sw-premise-theme`, `sw-character-conflict`).

The reference material is split across three files: engine breakdowns, series types, and character webs in [reference.md](reference.md); the full field tables for the document templates (Rabkin's ten-section pitch document, Smith's eleven-section series format, Oberg's twelve-component bible) in [reference-documents.md](reference-documents.md); filled-in samples (two writers' guidelines, Miller's six-treatment matrix, Succession's seven-want table) in [reference-samples.md](reference-samples.md).

## Sources and How the Books Relate

**Rabkin supplies the principles of the engine, Landau the fields, Oberg the types and specifications, Douglas the industry vocabulary, Goldberg/Rabkin the industrial bottom line, Blum the document hierarchy, Miller/Calvisi/Smith the forms, and the four primary texts the evidence.**

- **Rabkin, *Writing the Pilot: Creating the Series* (2017)**: the four elements of a franchise, story pattern, story landmines, and the principle that problems lead to process.
- **Landau, *The TV Showrunner's Roadmap*, 1e (2014)/2e (2022)**: tacit contract, central question vs. mystery, micro mission, story tentacles, guideposts, the 11-item pitch recipe, the three pilot categories.
- **Oberg, *Writing a Successful TV Series* (2023)**: the eight series types, story-type, Maslow and the M-Factor, bible specifications, "don't box yourself in."
- **Douglas, *Writing the TV Drama Series*, 3e (2011)** (Chinese translation: 《美剧编剧入门》 [Introduction to Writing American TV Dramas], based on the 2nd edition, 2009): franchise + springboard, three tiers of bible, the four steps of a pilot, official Chinese-translation terminology.
- **Goldberg & Rabkin, *Successful Television Writing* (2003)**: franchise = a rule set, the four questions for an original series and 100 episodes, two real writers' guidelines (for the section structure of the two mini-bibles, Diagnosis Murder and Martial Law, the never-touch list, and the intervention-authorization mechanism, see reference-samples.md section 11).
- **Blum, 《电视与银幕写作》 (Television and Screen Writing; English 4th ed. 2001 / Chinese translation 2003)**: the three-level concept → presentation → bible ladder, three marketability tests, three network pilot strategies, the five-story test.
- **Miller, *The Hero Succeeds* (2016)**: the 10 elements of a treatment plus six big questions, the inherent problem of an arena, story engine = license to intervene.
- **Calvisi, *Story Maps: TV Drama* (2016)**: Compelling Crisis, Week-to-Week, the 17-column Basic Story Map.
- **Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms* (2009), ch. 20**: the eleven sections of a series format, and "executives default to preferring a typical-episode pilot."
- Corpus: **The Sopranos scripts** (Chase's introduction + pilot), **Fleabag: The Scriptures**, **坂元裕二 (Sakamoto Yuji), 《花束般的恋爱》 (We Made a Beautiful Bouquet)** (film script), **Succession S1–S4 scripts**.

## I. Defining the Engine

**1. A franchise = concept + conflict + theme + story pattern** (Rabkin ch. 5, ch. 8). He himself said the seven-item checklist from his 2011 edition (characters, setting, story type, dialogue style, mode of interaction, narrative style, central conflict) was like describing an elephant as "grey hide, a long trunk, and big ears": a fine checklist when writing stories for an existing show, but **it cannot guide you in building your own engine**.

**2. A theme may not be a single word; it must be a debatable oppositional proposition**: "There is no such thing as a one-word theme for a television series." (Same chapter.) The Shield — "How much evil can a good man do in the pursuit of good before he becomes the evil he is fighting?"; Mad Men — "a man who makes his living selling the idea that consumption brings happiness, while knowing it doesn't hold true in his own life"; Lost — "Faith versus reason."

**3. The theme must land as conflict between characters** ("TV doesn't handle abstract concepts too well"). The Shield's minimal configuration: a good man capable of fighting evil + one evil + **other good people who fight evil by traditional means and fail** (otherwise the audience won't understand why the protagonist adopts the enemy's methods). The concept is only a container and is replaceable (Lost's concept came from an ABC executive).

**4. The meshing test** (Rabkin ch. 6): "Quick, come up with a story that naturally explores both concept and conflict. Now come up with 99 more." Counterexample: **Agents of SHIELD** — its only central conflict (Skye is anti-authoritarian yet happy to join SHIELD) sits inside one person and has nothing to do with the concept — "you can feel its emptiness"; the author himself suffered from the same disease with **Baywatch**, his first NBC season (concept but no conflict, no theme, "every week you are reinventing the franchise").

**5. The analogy is not a novel; it is jazz improvisation** (Rabkin ch. 4): conflict and theme are the chord changes; each episode is a new melody improvised over them.

**6. Five industry ways of saying the same thing**
- Landau 2e ch. 4: the pilot is a **tacit contract** with the audience; every episode is "fundamentally the same – only different"; when an executive asks "What's the franchise?", they are asking **what the protagonist does every week**. The soft-concept formula from 1e Tip 3: **"Each week, the characters will struggle to achieve ______."**
- Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 2: **"It has to be the same show every week, and yet at the same time, it has to be new, fresh, and different."** A franchise is **the rule set that permits creation**. Counterexample **Stingray**, which changed genre every week; one showrunner declared "It's got no franchise," and it was cancelled within two months.
- Douglas 3e ch. 1: a franchise "is both a boundary and an opportunity"; each episode is launched by a **springboard** — natural in case-driven shows (a crime, a commission, a patient), while family, workplace, school, and romance shows must be launched by **a personal (possibly internal) inciting incident**.
- Oberg 3.1: **"A series isn't a story that brings answers. It's a story engine that generates questions."** A film is an express train; a series is a train that stops at every station, yet each episode must still deliver a film's worth of plot.
- Miller ch. 1 / Calvisi §9: the engine depends on characters **having the license/agency to intervene in other people's lives** (to arrest, to defend, to treat) or on a unifying situation that pulls new characters into the protagonist's world; Calvisi's two mandatory columns are **Compelling Crisis** and **Week-to-Week** (the network always asks "what does he DO each week?"). Blum ch. 17 gives the economic reason: a series has to stay on screen for three to five years before it yields financial returns.

**7. The story pattern = the shape that repeats every episode** (Rabkin ch. 8): Sons of Anarchy is not "about a motorcycle gang"; it is about Jax trying to protect **three families** — spot the threat → move to protect → the blowback endangers another family. → Write the pattern in one sentence and run it in full in **the second half of the pilot** (the first half only establishes the world and triggers the story). (For the original text of Breaking Bad's pattern, the two layers of structural constants and character patterns, and counterexamples, see reference.md section 5.)

**8. Five tests for "does it have legs"**: 99 stories (Rabkin ch. 6) / **the six-episode test** (Landau 1e Tip 7) / prepare three pitches and five or six presentations (Landau 2e ch. 14 / Blum ch. 17) / **100 episodes and the four questions** (Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 17) / **the five-story test** (Blum ch. 17). (For the thresholds, exact wording, and sources of all five, see the full table in reference.md section 7.)

> ⚠️ **Closed-ended long-form series, limited series, and miniseries released all at once do not run this test.** The 100-episode test and "can it sustain five seasons" are criteria from American broadcast-network renewal economics; a story confined to one county, one office, or one old case simply cannot reach 100 episodes by genre arithmetic, and applying the test literally would reject perfectly good projects. For closed-ended long-form series, use instead **the engine pressure-loss calculation in rule 10**: work out at which episode the material engine loses pressure, and that episode is where the series should end; the episode count is thereby **argued** rather than accepted. Choose between the two criteria according to the medium and the commissioning model.

**9. Conceptual strength does not determine sustainability: the Torchwood counterexample** (Rabkin ch. 9): on concept alone you would bet on Torchwood, yet it lasted only four seasons while Doctor Who ran to its tenth — the alien threat is merely **premises/pretexts, not stories**. → **If you can't think of three episodes after the pilot, the disease lies in the character conflict, not the concept.** (For the full case comparison, see reference.md section 5.)

**10. The engine pressure-loss theory** (Succession S4 introduction): to decide whether a series should end, look at **the material plot engine** (deals, the empire, the job), not at whether the characters still have drama — Armstrong ended the show not because the characters had run out of story ("the motor in the room was still running hot") but because "**the big plot engine, the brutal materialist plumbing which ran under the show**, was looking to me like it would eventually start to lose pressure."

> How to use it: this rule is a **substitute** for rule 8, not a supplement. For a series that can be renewed indefinitely, use rule 8 (can it run another 100 episodes); for closed-ended long-form series and limited series, use this rule (at which episode does the engine lose pressure). **Determine the medium and the commissioning model first, then choose the criterion.**

**Four engine samples, one sentence each** (for details, see reference.md section 5): **Sopranos** = the therapy room + two families (the ethical ground rule Melfi lays down at the outset — "if I hear about a murder, I have to call the police" — is itself the engine); **Fleabag** = a web of relationships whose members are already wounding one another + one device (talking to the camera); **Sakamoto's 《花束般的恋爱》 (We Made a Beautiful Bouquet)** = the two leads' wants fall out of alignment over five years, and the film has no antagonist; **Succession** = "inheritance," the problem that can never be solved.

## II. Writing the Engine as a Sentence

**11. The central question must be a process sentence** (Rabkin ch. 3): **"Pilots need to raise questions that lead to processes, not solutions."** A film asks "Who killed Marion Crane?"; a series asks: how will the Earl of Grantham keep the estate in an economic system unsuited to it (Downton)? How will a mild-mannered chemistry teacher stay alive in the violent drug trade (Breaking Bad)? Can Richie Finestra revive rock before the alcohol and drugs kill him (Vinyl)? → Write it as "How will X keep doing Y in a world that Z?"

**12. Do not stage the central mystery in the pilot** (same chapter; the threefold error in Extant is the textbook case): the verdict is **"execution is not the problem here. Conception is."** (For each of the three errors, see reference.md section 7.)

**13. Story landmines** (Rabkin ch. 9) = character conflicts planted in the pilot's **subtext** that detonate several episodes later. → Plant one in every major character (an unspoken fear/desire/contradiction) and write down the episode in which you expect it to detonate; it must be at the character level — "she is a detective's daughter" does not count. (For the three reasons and the positive examples from Doctor Who / Crazy Ex-Girlfriend, see reference.md section 7.)

> **The conflict between the number of landmines and "one overarching mystery," and how to solve it.** Eight major characters = eight landmines, which in a Chinese long-form series amounts to eight concurrent mystery threads, and the Chinese system treats "multiple concurrent mysteries" as an illness (see the "one problem" configuration and the 《皇城根儿》 (At the Foot of the Imperial City) counterexample in `sw-chinese-series-practice`). The solution is not to reduce the number of landmines but to **change how they are hung**: hang every landmine beneath **the same overarching mystery**, making each a layer of its decomposition; reveal one layer per episode, and the moment a layer is revealed, immediately plant a new mystery with a time limit. The audience is therefore always chasing a single question, while you still have eight charges that can be detonated on a timer. When you lay out the board, give each landmine two episode numbers: the episode in which it detonates, and which layer of the overarching mystery it reveals.

**14. Suffering is not conflict; two attributes of one person must get in each other's way** (same chapter; counterexample Feed the Beast): **"suffering isn't conflict, and so suffering alone can't drive story."** (For the point-by-point breakdown of Tommy/Dion and the show's slide into soap opera, see reference.md section 7.)

**15. Sweet spot** (Landau 1e Tip 16) = the intersection of genre, tone, theme, central conflict, franchise, and central question/mystery; the formula in 2e ch. 4 is **sweet spot = character development × franchise**, and the one-hour version is **"a series must deliver the promise of its premise every week"** — which is the tacit contract. (For the original text, the TWD/Homeland examples, and the concept of currency, see reference.md section 7.)

**16. The marketing intern test** (Rabkin ch. 10) | **Optional, a zero-cost recheck, not a workflow step** — run it after the logline is written; it usually amounts to rereading it and declaring it passed. Its value lies in "having this reader in mind while you write the logline," not in checking afterwards: can one sentence make a junior marketing staffer secure you a few column inches in *Entertainment Weekly*? **Bigger does not mean a bigger budget; it means the scale of the conflict that defines the series.** (For comparative examples, see reference.md section 7.)

**17. "Big does not have to mean shallow."** Three ways to go bigger (same chapter): **change the profession** (the origin of Sopranos changed only one thing: the job), **change the place**, **change the era**. The opposing position (Miller ch. 1–2): **"having great characters trumps high concept every time."** (For the full text of the three examples and Miller's argument, see reference.md section 7.)

**18. The bookkeeping episode: whatever an episode "resolves" must leave an account to be settled in a later episode.** (An extension by this pack, taking Succession S1's season-level practice down to the episode level — the original rule is that "at the season finale all ten threads are placed, but every placement generates a debt for the next season: Greg's photocopies, Tom's cruise ship, Roman's manslaughter, Shiv's dual loyalty"; see `succession-series-writing`.)

A conventional case-of-the-week show starts repeating around episode 12 because closing a case every week amounts to resetting to zero. Change "closing the case" into "keeping accounts" and it won't: the agreement signed this week, the person protected, the complaint suppressed — each leaves an entry on the ledger, to be settled in a designated later episode. When laying out the outline, write each account as one line: `Episode N: X does Y → Episode M: Z pays the bill`; M must be a specific episode number. 6–10 such accounts per season is about right: too few and the episodes remain islands; too many and the audience can't keep track.

- **Relationship to "one case per episode"**: this is the third road between the procedural and the serial — the unit still closes (the audience gets a result each week), but the cost does not close.
- **Relationship to the story pattern (rule 7)**: the story pattern governs "the repetition of each episode's shape"; bookkeeping governs "the accumulation of each episode's consequences." Both must be present: shape without accumulation = a series of stand-alones; accumulation without shape = the audience doesn't know what they're buying each week.
- **Self-check**: point at any episode and say whom it owes, who will pay, and in which episode. Any episode you can't answer for is an episode that has been reset to zero.

**19. Segment a long-running series by "the nth use of the same skill set," not by chronological order.** (An extension by this pack, based on Landau 1e Tip 13's movement-based season segmentation: at the start of the season define only the tent poles, split the season into movements of 4–6 episodes, and give each movement one major event.)

The protagonist's skill stays the same; each change in **how it is used** is one segment, and the number of changes determines the number of segments: learning to use it to win → winning while investigating / using it for personal ends → turning it against its original purpose and setting off a greater cost → abandoning the original goal and using it to change the rules. One person, four uses, four consequences — naturally four segments; 24 episodes divided by four = six episodes each, landing exactly in the 4–6 episode range of a movement.

- Benefit: the segment boundaries grow out of the character, not out of the calendar; and each segment's climax is "the blowback of this use," so it needs no separate design.
- Test: if you can't say **how** the protagonist's use of this skill set in a given segment differs from the previous one, that segment is filler.
- Relationship to the season arc: first define the sequence of uses, then the tent poles, and only then lay out the episodes — reverse the order and you end up "drawing a timetable first and filling events into it."

## III. The Character Web

**20. Internal conflict must be externalized into a circle of characters who pull at him** (Rabkin ch. 6). Buffy's "wanting a normal life vs. saving the world" is externalized as a mother who wants her to be normal, a watcher who wants her to kill vampires, friends in the everyday world, and a lover who is a century-old vampire; SHIELD's Skye is the reverse — the conflict stays inside one person and cannot generate story.

**21. Every series is a family drama** (Landau 2e ch. 6): **"Every television series is about a family – whether they're related by blood or not."** Corollary: **every family on screen must be imperfect and, at some level, discontented.** (For six family types and examples, see reference.md section 6.)

**22. A library of familial roles** (same chapter): you don't have to assemble a complete set of genders and ages; you need to determine "how each character will treat the others in both the positive and negative directions." (For the full role library and the corollaries for law-enforcement partners / workplace colleagues, see reference.md section 6.)

**23. The transactional family (the Logan Roy paradigm)** (same chapter): "seeking your boss's approval replaces seeking your parents' approval — and the former comes with a salary as extra confirmation"; Logan "withholding his approval and validation," "His relationships with his kids are all transactional."

**24. Three scene-level rules** (same chapter): **"the people who love you most know how to hurt you the most."** → Before writing a two-hander, determine "which button he knows the other has," then decide whether to press it. (For the new-character test and the original subtext formula, see reference.md section 6.)

**25. Division of expertise** (Landau 1e Tip 20): different combinations of skills are used to **create conflict, present multiple POVs, and help the audience tell characters apart**; Grey's Anatomy **defines characters by specialty**. (For the Breaking Bad division table, House's anti-agreement principle, and the Out of Their Element generator, see reference.md section 6.)

**26. The rationality axis** (Rabkin appendix, Mooresville pitch): **"start from the position that they are both rational, intelligent adults"** → the mutual attraction in a relationship drama must have a **structural reason**, not just chemistry. (For the full case, see reference.md section 6.)

**27. Complementary dual protagonists** (Landau 1e Tip 9): **"two incomplete people together you get one whole."** (Mulder/Scully = belief and doubt.) (For Hacks's mutual need/shame template and Gomorrah's logline generator, see reference.md section 6.)

**28. Sakamoto-style dual-protagonist misalignment** (《花束般的恋爱》 / We Made a Beautiful Bouquet): value shifts come not from opposition but from **consideration** (scene 85, while blow-drying her hair, she says "I'm thinking of looking for a job"). (For the four pairs of mirror characters and the dialogue budget allocation, see reference.md section 5.)

**29. Shame and the void** (Landau 2e ch. 7, citing Brené Brown's *Daring Greatly*): **shame = our fear of disconnection**; trauma creates **emotional voids** → voids produce coping mechanisms that avoid vulnerability. **"Anyone who challenges any of these constructs becomes an antagonist."** (For a design menu of the three types of defense, see reference.md section 6.)

**30. A grounded superpower is also a liability** (Landau 2e ch. 5, Gilligan's method of building characters): Walt's superpower is "the ability to lie to himself." 1e Tip 8: **"when a character's greatest strength also becomes his or her greatest liability… The trick to dramatic sustainability is keeping your main character in this form of limbo."** (For the three examples and the full boxed text, see reference.md section 6.)

**31. The same backstory stated twice** (Landau 2e ch. 7, Mare of Easttown): **first give the polished version; several episodes later, in a safer or more forced setting, give the true version; the gap is the character's shame.** (For the original text of both speeches and the ensemble balancing, see reference.md section 6.)

**32. The rhythm of revealing a flaw** (Landau 1e Tip 8): a flaw must originate in backstory if it is to run long, but in the pilot the backstory is only **obliquely hinted at** — "what we don't know about a character is often more compelling than what we do know." (For Don Draper / Scandal's delayed reveals and the Girls counterexample, see reference.md section 6.)

**33. Define the nature of the opposing force first; then decide whether it needs a face** (Landau 2e ch. 9): **Succession = greed**; **Mare of Easttown and Fleabag = grief.** The general principle (footnote credited to UCLA professor emeritus Howard Suber): **"All villains are antagonists, but not all antagonists are villains."** (For the comparison of the nature of the opposing force across nine series and Oberg's supplement, see reference.md section 6.)

**34. The villain is the hero of their own story** (same chapter): **"Every villain is the hero of his/her/their own story."** — "they usually feel justified, entitled, and downright righteous." Structural judgment: **"as soon as that primary goal is successfully attained, the series is usually over – unless it pivots to another overarching mission or goal."** (For the four corollaries and Rue's misalignment case, see reference.md section 6.)

**35. Don't set up straw men** (the Kings, Landau 2e ch. 4): **"Propaganda is confirming what you already believe."** What is more valuable is to let **regular characters we like disagree with one another, with each of them having a point.** (For the full passage and the Good Wife example, see reference.md section 6.)

**36. The three-piece kit for a sustainable conflict web** (Blum ch. 17, the third test): **"If all the series protagonists loved one another, all they could do is smile and agree with each other."** The engine = **a plot pattern of "group/family bonds" + character attributes pre-installed in each character that inevitably chafe against the others + a coincidence or situation that prevents them from leaving one another**; without all three, it won't last three to five years. (For the item-by-item breakdown of Downton and Succession, Succession's three devices, and the Tom counterexample, see reference.md section 5; **the filled-in version of this web = Succession's seven wants / what each can lose / whom they are bound to / engine function table, see reference-samples.md section 13**.)

## IV. Pilot Types and Strategy

**37. Three categories** (Landau 1e Tip 1 / 2e ch. 14; Douglas 3e ch. 2, Oberg 3.2, and Calvisi §6 use different names for the same thing): **premise pilot** (what happens in episode 1 is necessary for "starting the series from day one") / **non-premise / ongoing / midstream** ("It may be the audience's first episode, but it's not day one for the characters.") / **hybrid** (open at a climactic moment, then flash-forward or flashback); Oberg adds **semi-cold start** (The Walking Dead). (For the four-type definition table, the example list, the classification of Sopranos, and the classification of 47 series, see reference.md section 4.)

**38. Two hard rules of entry**: Oberg 3.2 — in a premise pilot, **don't save the inciting incident for the end of the episode**, "You can't afford to have a pilot that's just a long first dramatic act."; Calvisi §6 — **"Enter the action of your story as late as possible in the world but as soon as possible in the script."** (In the original Scandal script the client doesn't walk in until page 12; in the finished cut it is moved up to **minute 6**).

**39. Three network strategies and the "premise pilot trap"** (Blum ch. 17, in the 1999–2001 context; **the network labels are dated, but the mechanism still holds**): the original wording is that a premise pilot "may well be **a poor barometer** of how a series will develop"; **Smith ch. 20 agrees (for half-hour comedy)** — **"most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot"**; **Landau's different position**: it is not a trap; he only asks that the pitch state which type it is. (For the original text of the three strategies and Smith's argument, see reference.md section 4.)
→ **Four criteria**: ① a conservative mass-audience network or a half-hour comedy → typical or half-premise; ② a complex world with rules that must be explained → premise (Lost, Stranger Things, TWD); ③ the character web is the engine → midstream (Fleabag, The Office, Succession); ④ you need both → the Breaking Bad way: first half establishes, second half runs the story pattern once. **Priority: all four can hit at once (③ the character web is the engine + ② the world's rules need explaining is the most common pair); when two or more hit, always go with ④** — ④ is the only shape that delivers both the setup and "what it looks like every week" in a single episode, instead of choosing between ② and ③. Only when a single criterion hits do you follow that one.

**40. Three ways to land the ending**: ① **"The end of your pilot is the beginning of your series."** (Landau); ② **Stick the landing**; ③ **a cliffhanger is not mandatory** (Miller ch. 6). (For the original text of all three and Calvisi's balancing sentence, see reference.md section 4.)

**41. Douglas's four steps of a pilot** (3e ch. 4): ① **Create the World** (Lindelof: **"the franchise is the world."**) ② **Find the Story Springboards** ③ **Populate the World** ④ **Make a Plan**. At its core is **secrecy**, deep enough to take 88 or 100 hours to reveal; **a character's growth arc is usually not on the agenda on day one**; **the first ten pages decide life or death**. (For the full text of the four steps and the quotations, see reference.md section 4.)

**42. Ten anxious questions** (Jeffries, who teaches the pilot course at USC): **by the end of the pilot, list at least ten questions that keep the audience anxious**; those ten questions are also used to derive **ten short synopses for future episodes**; **hook the executive first while keeping cards up your sleeve.** (For the source archive and that eternal note, see reference.md section 4.)

**43. Sopranos's "don't explain the mechanism"** (Chase's introduction p0012 + pilot): the pilot deliberately does not explain Tony's official position or how "waste management" operates, on the principle that **"rarely is anything black and white in life"**; **this is also why the pilot doesn't end on a cliffhanger.** (For the full case and the real reason a major network passed on it, see reference.md section 5.)

## V. Series Typology and Story-Type (Primarily Oberg)

**44. Eight series types** (Oberg 1.2): procedural (closed-ended) / serial / serial-procedural hybrid (three forms) / sitcom / mini-series and limited / anthology / mini-series series / web series. The unwritten rule of sitcom is **"characters don't change, and it's fine"**; limited (4–8 episodes) is **the easiest first step from film into series**. (For the definitions of all eight, their audience implications, and the full table of the three hybrid forms, see reference.md section 3.)

**45. Two additional rules for the mini-series series** (Rabkin ch. 12): the concept cannot be "defined by execution" (all the information in American Crime Story is in the public domain); **the first episode of each season of an anthology or mini-series series must re-supply "an immediate narrative hook connecting the audience to the protagonist."** **Landau 2e ch. 1 (2022)**: **limited series are harder to sell**, and **10 episodes per season is the benchmark for a new series**. (For the full text and exceptions, see reference.md section 3.)

**46. Determining story-type** (Oberg 2.2): ask **"Where does the main problem in the story come from, at season level?"** — external → **plot-led**; internal → **character-led** (which does not need an antagonist but needs a **catalyst character**); social/story world → **theme-led**; plus **hybrid** and **exception**. (For examples of the three and the exception mechanism, see reference.md section 3.)

**47. The reverse check specific to series** (same section): **series almost always have multiple threads, so check in reverse** — is there one protagonist and one main dramatic action/evolution taking shape at the season level? If yes, it is not theme-led. (For the second prompt and the determination of Chernobyl, see reference.md section 3.)

**48. Choose the wrong type and you are locked in** (Oberg's introduction and 3.1, question 9): **"The wrong combination of series type and story-type is the root cause of many series failures"**; the most common box-in is **a character-led show that completely resolves the protagonist's internal problem in season one (movie thinking)** → season two then has to switch story-type. (For three examples and the balancing model, see reference.md section 3.)

**49. Maslow and the M-Factor** (Oberg 2.3, 2.6): **"the lower in the pyramid, the larger the potential audience"**, and **you must explore vertically**. **M-Factor** = the position of audience, genre, and story-type relative to Maslow + whether the budget is coherent (1–5 score). (For Occupied / Money Heist / sitcom examples of exploring up and down, the whodunit warning, and the rooting of Breaking Bad's motivation, see reference.md section 3.)

**50. What's at stake?** (Oberg glossary) = "the question development executives ask most often when they don't care about the story": what happens if the main problem isn't solved, and it must make people **sad/anxious/afraid**; the bottom line is that no episode may lack a statable stake, and multiple threads must connect. (For the original text of the two bottom lines, Landau's two, and the half-hour/relationship-drama versions, see reference.md section 7.)

**51. The inherent problem of an arena** (Miller ch. 1, 2015 American broadcast perspective): High school / Family / Relationship / News / Anthology each has its inherent weaknesses; the core is **"A character's agency completes the story engine"** — a journalist can "meddle" but has **no license to intervene**. (For each of the five arenas, the solutions, and notes on scope, see reference.md section 3.)

## VI. Documents: From Logline to Bible

**52. Three levels of documents** (Blum ch. 17): **the concept** → **the series presentation** → **the series bible**. (For the original definitions of the three levels and what each must contain, see reference.md section 1.)

**53. Page-count specifications at a glance**: bible **10–85 pages** / mini-bible **about 5 pages**; written pitch bible **10–30 pages** (sweet spot around 15); treatment **10–15 pages for drama / 8–10 for comedy**; half-hour comedy series format **10–15 pages**; oral pitch **20 minutes is the sweet spot**, and always time it; mythology document + origin story **one page maximum combined**. (For the full table, sources, and Douglas's three bible tiers, see reference.md section 1; for Oberg's item-by-item specifications for the twelve bible components and the difference between bible / pitch deck / format, see reference-samples.md section 10.)

**54. Four sets of field lists** (pick one): **Landau 2e ch. 1 Essential Guideposts** (about 25 fields, also usable as a bible table of contents; **the Logline "Must contain the word 'but'"**; **a Central Question is about the future / a Central Mystery is about the past**) / **the 11-item pitch recipe in Landau 2e ch. 14** / **Miller's 10 elements of a treatment + six big questions** / **Calvisi's 17-column Basic Story Map** (**the same set of columns is filled in twice, once at the "episode" level and once at the "series" level**). (For the full text of all four sets, see reference.md section 1; for the cross-reference matrix of Miller's 10 elements against the six student treatments, see reference-samples.md section 12.)

**55. The five places most often written wrong**: ① **the Overview is not a pilot synopsis** (Douglas names this a "common mistake": it must describe the world and quest of the whole series and **give the springboards inside the Overview itself**); ② **don't list characters as a roster**; ③ **don't say everything you know** (being pressed with questions is success); ④ **don't over-plan**; ⑤ **the prose style must match the genre of the show**. (For the original wording of the five, supplements from each book, and Oberg/Landau/Smith's hard rules, see reference.md section 1.)

**56. The skeleton of a story-line document (devised by this pack, not belonging to any single book)**. Every book supplies a document for "the whole series," **but none supplies a document for "one story line"**; the ten-field table (line name / purpose / driver / stakes / entry into this episode / exit from this episode / cross-episode beats / relationship to the controlling idea / causal relation to other lines, including **the pull-the-thread test**) is in reference.md section 2 — use it to write each line as one page and align them horizontally before breaking the season.

**Landau's story tentacles (2e ch. 4) are the positive version**: when breaking story, **"choose the plotline that leads to more story"** — look for fertile ground rather than one-off dead ends.

## VII. General Principles for Adapting into a Series

(For the five-source typology of adaptation in the Chinese system and episode-by-episode, scene-by-scene adaptation examples, see `sw-chinese-series-practice`; for staging details, see `sw-format-adaptation`. Except where noted, the following is from Landau 2e ch. 2.)

1. **What can be adapted**: epic-length novels make bad films, but **streaming is the ideal medium for long-form storytelling across decades and large ensembles** (GoT, My Brilliant Friend, Bridgerton; "Russian literature is ideal for the ongoing TV series format"); comics are naturally suited to episodic adaptation.
2. **Chain of Title**: without a clear, lawful chain of authorship you cannot start production. Two caveats: **structure is not protected by copyright** (Rashomon, Memento, Groundhog Day, Russian Doll); **scène à faire** does not constitute infringement. Smith's hard rule: **"You can't sell what you don't own."**
3. **Timing is everything**: what is taboo on the page may be unbearable on screen (the novel Room has a childlike innocence; the film is "unbearably gruesome").
4. **There is no formula**: "The best approach to any adaptation is bespoke."; a multi-year epic can **map one period to each season**; **one overarching theme per season**, changing the theme at a pivot (example: The Wire).
5. **Changing characters, eras, and places is allowed, but it needs a reason**: Euphoria changed almost everything and kept only the essence (reckless, pleasure-seeking, psychologically damaged teenagers) — the original has no Rue and no Jules; Unorthodox's entire Berlin storyline is invented, and **the invented characters are deliberately kept at a distance from the real people** ("we made her a musician," out of consideration for the original author's privacy).
6. **Honoring the spirit without being married to it**: you can merge characters into a composite and cut subplots; "the moving image supersedes the written word." Elementary's formula: "Sherlock Holmes in repair" — a man who was ten steps ahead is now only two steps ahead.
7. **Examples of choosing a starting point**: an adaptation should choose **a starting point that is more dynamic, establishes the gap immediately, and whose very gap can trigger flashbacks**. (For a comparison of the starting points of the Queen's Gambit novel and the Netflix version, see reference.md section 5.)
8. **Finding the (emotional) core**: **"It's not solely who does what when, but who knows what when and the corresponding emotional fallout."**; "When in doubt, simplify."
9. **Externalizing inner psychology**: omniscient narration/interior monologue → multiple POVs or a mysterious narrator (Fleabag's aside makes us her confidante, but she is an **unreliable narrator** crushed by guilt over Boo's death). Walter Mosley: **"Plot Is Revelation."**
10. **When taking on a proposition or IP, first distinguish what you have in hand** (Saracho on Vida, 2e ch. 6): Vida also had a **world-building document** called a "short story" — it contained two bars and a pair of sisters, **but it was not a story**. → **First determine whether what you have is a world-building document or a story; the former supplies only a world.** The reverse method for issue dramas: **"if I just embody gentrification in the two sisters… The characters just embody the issue."**

|---|---|---|---|

| Whether you know the season ending before entering the room | Armstrong: **knew the season ending while writing episode 1**; Gomorrah's full-season outline was completed first | Gould (BCS): "feeling our way through it rather than having a master plan"; Feldman did not know Steve would die at the end of S1 | Ensemble multi-thread shows lean A; shows that evolve on cast chemistry lean B |
| Write the script first or pitch first | Landau: "write your pilot scripts on spec" | Aranya: "**a script is also 60 pages of reasons to say no**"; Your Honor sold on an oral pitch + three pages | No track record → A (Oberg agrees); with a track record or packaging → B |
| Premise or typical pilot | Blum: "a poor barometer"; Smith: most executives prefer typical | Landau: not a trap, as long as you say which one it is | See the four criteria in rule 39 |

## IX. Diagnostic Checklist

**Engine**: ① Is the theme a debatable oppositional proposition rather than a single word? ② Has the theme landed as conflict between two characters, and has internal conflict been externalized? ③ Can the meshing test produce a second and a sixth story? ④ Is the central question a process sentence or an answer sentence? ⑤ Can the story pattern be written in one sentence and run once in the second half of the pilot? ⑥ Do five or six subsequent stories come from the same source and end in the same shape? ⑦ Can this set of conflicts sustain 100 episodes? (**Closed-ended long-form series, limited series, and miniseries skip the "can it run 100 episodes / five seasons" question** and run the engine pressure-loss calculation from rule 10 instead: work out at which episode the material engine loses pressure, and that episode is where it should end. The main text already divides it this way; this checklist is synchronized with it.) ⑧ Can you state each episode's stake, which level of Maslow it sits at, and whether each line probes downward? ⑨ Is the protagonist a participant or an observer — do they have license/agency? ⑩ The marketing intern test: can a one-sentence pitch say nothing more than "it's really good"?

**Character web**: ⑪ Does each major character carry one subtext landmine (at the character level, not a plot revelation), and in which episode is it expected to detonate? ⑫ Do each person's two attributes interfere with each other? ⑬ Have you mistaken suffering for conflict, and does the character have an action they can take? ⑭ Is the nature of the opposing force defined — and only then do you decide whether it needs a face? ⑮ Is the villain the hero of their own story? ⑯ Have you written out the relationship vector for every pair of characters (which button gets pressed, who envies whom, who can't stand whom)? ⑰ Has every new character answered "how does their arrival change the regular characters"? ⑱ Is the protagonist's grounded superpower also a liability? ⑲ Is there one place where "the same backstory is stated twice"? ⑳ Does the series end once the main goal is achieved — is there a next overarching mission?

**Pilot and documents** (**series level: run only when setting up a project, writing a pilot, or preparing a pitch; when revising a single episode of an already-airing show, skip the whole block**): ㉑ Is the pilot premise / non-premise / hybrid, and why? ㉒ Has the central mystery been staged? ㉓ Does the ending leave ten anxious questions and yield ten future episodes? ㉔ Does the pilot demonstrate the franchise (has the heist started? has the therapy started?) ㉕ Is the Overview written as a pilot synopsis, and does it give springboards? ㉖ Are characters introduced embedded in the story, or as a list? ㉗ Does the prose style match the genre? ㉘ Have you said everything you know (being pressed with questions is success)? ㉙ Is season five written too rigidly? ㉚ Has a character-led show resolved its internal problem cleanly in season one (the most common box-in)?

**The document's reader**: ㉛ **Who is this page for?** Working documents (the landmine table, the button table, the binding objects, the story-line document) and submission documents (logline, overview, characters, pilot synopsis, future stories) are two different things; don't mix them into one and hand it over. A platform reader will not look at your tool tables; and a "pitch" with the tool tables stripped out will leave you gasping for air by episode 14. **Write both; submit only one.** Test: if a page contains this skill's terminology (franchise, landmine, unity of opposites, story pattern), it is for your own eyes.

**Cost and segmentation**: ㉜ Does everything an episode "resolves" leave an account to be settled in a later episode? (See section II, 2.8.) ㉝ Is the basis for segmenting the whole series "the nth use of the same skill set by the protagonist," or merely chronological order? (See 2.9.)

## X. Workflow: From Idea to Bible

1. **Research the arena** (Miller ch. 1): choose the arena → read books and articles from different positions, watch documentaries, interview experts, ride along. While researching, answer three questions (What is the world of this arena? What kind of people live in it? **How do new characters intersect with the people in this world?**). "I don't create the show before I do my research."
2. **Answer the three Whys** (Landau 2e ch. 1): Why this idea / Why you / Why now (**don't chase the platform's current mandate**; by the time you finish, it will have changed).
3. **Mine the world** (Douglas, step 1 of four): if you can't name three, four, or five protagonists, go back to this step.
4. **Find the engine**: define the franchise type and the **springboard**; an arena with no ready-made springboard needs a designed "personal inciting incident" as the per-episode launcher. **Focus verbs** (Landau 2e ch. 4): investigating, diagnosing, litigating, battling, competing, saving, confronting, stealing, avenging — the verb is the micro mission.
5. **Walk Rabkin's three-element derivation** (ch. 7, NASCAR case, copyable): research → **flip the question** (the protagonist must be an outsider like the audience) → **intensify the conflict** ("doesn't understand it" is too weak → "hates it but is trapped in this world") → **characters are a clash of equals** (drawn from the opposition; reject the observer protagonist) → situation (the only way out is to do this thing) → **conflict must be intrinsic to character and concept** (rule out frame-ups, villainous bosses, and other "plot devices that can't power a series through a hundred episodes") → distill the theme (an evergreen argument of the era, and one **you personally care about**) → **use the theme to revise the concept in turn** ("individual vs. community" needs a community → a racing team → a group of misfits as stubborn as the boss).
6. **Define the type** (Oberg): format → series type → season-level story-type → per-episode story-type → genre → M-Factor check. Format self-check: can it go from 10×60 to 8×45? Is the first third backstory? **If it only gets good at episode 3, compress or cut the first two episodes** — "starting the story too early is a common flaw."
7. **Build the character web**: familial role → the positive and negative vector of every pair → division of expertise → one landmine per person → the nature of the opposing force → check whether the attributes interfere with each other.
8. **Write Calvisi's 17-column one-pager** as the foundation for every document that follows.
9. **Write the treatment (Miller's 10 elements) or the presentation (Blum's four sections)**: a serialized show gets a three-season arc; a stand-alone show gets 10 episode loglines. (For the six filled-in treatment samples, the Egri three-dimensional + signature trait form, the ability-growth table, and how to write the Markets section, see reference-samples.md section 12.)
10. **Generate and test 5–6 subsequent stories** (sources and ending shapes must all differ); if they are too similar, go back to step 4.
11. **Write a one-page story-line document for each line** (rule 56), align them into a "characters × episodes" master table, and run **the pull-the-thread test** on each.
12. **Design and write the pilot**: choose the type (rule 39's four criteria) → write a "first-ten-pages plan" → run the story pattern in the second half → plant landmines → write the ten questions for the ending → write the script (for beats and acts, see `sw-series-structure`).
13. **Update it into a pitch document / mini-bible** (Landau's 11 items or Oberg's 12 components; for item-by-item specifications, see reference-samples.md section 10; for a real mini-bible given to outside writers, see reference-samples.md section 11), with a mythology document + origin story (≤1 page).
14. **Time and rehearse the pitch** (20 minutes), and prepare at least three examples of future episodes; for room etiquette, see `sw-writers-room`.
15. **After the sale, write the production bible** and let it become a living document that is continually updated ("facts to wax").

## XI. Exercises

1. **Reverse-engineer the engine** (Goldberg & Rabkin ch. 2/9/10): watch TV for an entire evening; after each episode, answer "what is this series about?" in ≤4 lines (no Law & Order / CSI / Star Trek — that's cheating); then break down one title sequence shot by shot (format / mood / character sequence), and answer "Does your story fulfill what the main title promises?"
2. **What three episodes of the same show have in common**: watch three episodes of a show and ask what they share and **why they couldn't be told on another show** — that is its franchise.
3. **99 stories**: in 30 minutes, write 20 episode loglines; each must touch both concept and conflict; if you can't write six, go back and fix the engine.
4. **Rewrite as process sentences**: write the central question of five shows you like as one process sentence each; if your own project can only be written as an answer sentence, change the container (profession/place/era).
5. **Landmine table**: one row per major character — "unspoken fear/desire/contradiction + expected detonation episode."
6. **Character-web vector table**: pair every major character with every other; in each cell write "which of the other person's buttons they press in this relationship"; if more than a third of the cells are blank, the ensemble has not yet become a web.
7. **Blum's five-story test**: write 5 subsequent stories, labeling **source** and **ending shape**; if they are too similar, start over.
8. **Fill in the 17-column form at two levels**: use the Basic Story Map on your project twice — once for "this episode," once for "the whole series."
9. **The six-episode test + ten questions**: write the ten anxious questions at the end of the pilot, then expand each into a logline for a future episode (Jeffries's classroom assignment).
10. **Adaptation exercise**: choose a long novel and write the time period covered by each season, each season's overarching theme, and the choice of the pilot's starting point with three reasons (following the argument format of Queen's Gambit).
11. **Engine pressure-loss self-check** (Succession S4 introduction): write down this series' "material plot engine" (deals, empire, positions, sources of cases) and ask: in which season will it begin to lose pressure? After that, what engine replaces it, or does the series end?
