## I. Document Hierarchy and Template Fields (Blum / Landau / Miller / Calvisi / Oberg / Douglas / Smith)

**50. The three tiers of documents** (Blum ch17, as written): **creative concept** = "the blueprint of the arena/setting, the characters, and the conflict" → **series presentation** = "the concept plus the arena/setting, the characters, a story draft (the pilot's story premise), and sample storylines (future stories)" → **series bible** = "the completed sample script (the pilot script), specific character arcs for each major character, and a step-by-step story outline."

| Document | Source | Specification |
|---|---|---|
| bible / mini-bible | Landau 2e ch1 | **10–85 pages** / **about 5 pages**; start writing as soon as the pilot first draft is finished |
| mythology document | Landau 2e ch14 | **including the origin story, one page total, maximum**. "Keep it simple." |
| Oral pitch | Landau 2e ch14 / Oberg 3 | **20-minute sweet spot**, always time it / Oberg: within 15–20 minutes |
| Written pitch bible | Oberg 3 | **10–30 pages (excluding the script), sweet spot about 15 pages**; also called format, book, pitch document, proposal, TV treatment |
| treatment | Miller Intro | **drama 10–15 pages / comedy 8–10 pages**; a bible is 70–100 pages, a WGA MBA–covered item, usually written only after a sale |
| series format (half-hour comedy) | Smith ch20 | **10–15 pages, with plenty of white space** |
| Douglas's three bible tiers | Douglas 3e ch2 | ① full version: **Star Trek: TNG at about 100 pages** (including **each crew member's relationship history with every other crew member**); ② a few-page version; ③ **most shows never make a bible at all**. Accumulative model: Northern Exposure's **"facts to wax,"** "amassed rather than generated" |

**52. Four field checklists** (pick one): **Landau 2e ch1 Essential Guideposts** (about 25 fields, also usable as a bible table of contents; **Logline "Must contain the word 'but'"**; **Central Question is about the future / Central Mystery is about the past**); **Landau 2e ch14's 11-item pitch recipe** (Why this idea / Why you / Why now / tone = genre / pitch the teaser first / pitch the whole pilot episode / **embed the characters in the story** / mythology ≤1 page / **at least three examples of future episodes** / Stick the landing); **Miller's 10 treatment elements** (Title / Type of show / Idea / **Logline ≤30 words** / World / Characters / Story engine / Themes / Tone / **Season arcs for three seasons or ten episode loglines**, plus six big questions: relevance / difference / envelope / research / influences / **why me**); **Calvisi's 17-column Basic Story Map** (including **COMPELLING CRISIS**, **WEEK-TO-WEEK**, DEFINING CHARACTERISTIC, MISBEHAVIOR, ACHILLES HEEL, DYNAMIC ALLY, SHADOW, PILOT ARC—**the same set of columns is filled out once at the "episode" level and once at the "series" level**; PILOT ARC is written as a three-part rather than a two-point structure).

**53. The five places writers most easily go wrong**
1. **The Overview is not a pilot synopsis** (Douglas 3e ch2 calls this a "common mistake"): it must describe the world and quest of the whole series and **give the springboards right inside the Overview**—"make it soar." Oberg adds one more: **do not use terms like story-type in the bible.**
2. **Do not list the characters**: Landau—"on a need-to-know basis within the context of the story vs. as a laundry list"; Douglas—one page per character, and only a few characters (once you go past the third or fourth, your listener's eyes glaze over); Oberg—3–5 leads at most one page each; Smith—a detailed character description gets its own section.
3. **Do not tell everything you know**: **"there is no need to tell the execs everything you know."** The measure of success is **"An effective pitch will entice your listeners to ask you questions."**
4. **Do not over-plan**: Oberg—**"Put 90% of your energy into making the best first season … and 90% of that energy into making your pilot the most exciting episode."**; "Showing rigidity regarding what might happen in Season 5 … is more likely to trigger alarm bells." Landau 2e: **the bible is a selling tool, not a road map the writers' room must follow.**
5. **The prose style must match the show's genre**: Landau—"If it's a comedy, your pitch had better be funny."; Oberg—"Sell the sizzle, not the sausage."; Smith—"you can't sell a sitcom with a document that reads like an annual report."

## II. The Storyline Document Skeleton (One Page per Line, Devised for This Pack)

| Field | What to fill in | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Line name** | A driver + a verb ("Joyce brings Will home," "Greg finds a master") | If it can only be named with an abstract noun, there is no want yet. |
| **Purpose** | The one thing it does for the whole series (serves the engine / descends Maslow's hierarchy / carries the counter-theme / relieves pressure on the ensemble) | If you cannot say it, cut it. |
| **Driver** | This line's protagonist = **the person who experiences the most conflict** (Oberg's definition), not necessarily the main character | If they share the same goal with someone else and are triggered by the same event, this is really a strand of the A story (A1/A2/A3). |
| **Stakes** | The concrete consequence of failure + where it sits on Maslow's hierarchy | If you cannot say "what happens if it fails," cut it. |
| **Entry point in this episode** | Which scene it enters from and what triggers it | A line that enters later than the episode's midpoint should not be in this episode. |
| **Exit point in this episode** | What state it leaves in (success / failure / suspended), **who now knows what** | The exit must change someone's state of knowledge. |
| **Cross-episode beats** | The 3–8 beats it gets this season, with the episodes marked | A line with no beat for two consecutive episodes will be forgotten. |
| **Relationship to the controlling idea** | Which end of the theme's opposing proposition it stands on, and when it switches sides | Test it with Rabkin's theme filter (TWD's "survival or civilization defines humanity?" instantly kills ideas like "a birthday party mistaken for a murder")—**"When you have your theme … you're halfway to knowing what an episode looks like."** |
| **Causality with other lines** | Whom it affects and who affects it (at least one) | **The line-removal test**: if removing it changes nothing, cut it or add causality through setup/payoff. |

## III. Series Type, Story-Type, Maslow, and Arena (Oberg / Miller)

**42. The eight series types** (Oberg 1.2): procedural (closed-ended) / serial / serial-procedural hybrid (three forms) / sitcom / mini-series and limited / anthology / mini-series series / web series. Key points: a procedural is plot-led in every episode, "like a mini-feature film lasting under an hour," can be watched out of order, and its main problem sits at the bottom of Maslow's hierarchy → the widest audience, at the cost of little room for character development; a serial can be any story-type at the season/series level, **but each episode usually still has a plot-led main line**; the three hybrid forms = alternating mythology episodes (The X-Files, Lost) / a procedural element expanded to a whole season (The Wire's five seasons = drugs / the docks / politics / schools and the mayoral election / the media; Broadchurch; Stranger Things) / a procedural with strong serial elements (Sex Education); the sitcom's unwritten rule is **"characters don't change, and it's fine"**; limited (4–8 episodes) is **the easiest first step from film into series**.

**44. Determining the story-type** (Oberg 2.2): ask **"Where does the main problem in the story come from, at season level?"**—external → **plot-led** (Stranger Things, TWD); internal → **character-led** (Sex Education, After Life; no antagonist is needed, but a **catalyst character** is, such as Maeve); social/story world → **theme-led** (Big Little Lies, Succession, GoT, Deadwood: no overall three-act structure, but each line can be plot-led or character-led in its own right); plus **hybrid** (Breaking Bad shifts from plot-led to character-led) and **exception** (Fleabag, Mr. Robot, Happy Valley, True Detective S1: held up by managing information).

**45. The reverse diagnostic specific to series** (same section): **almost every series is multi-line, so work backward**—is there one protagonist and one main dramatic action/evolution taking shape at the season level? If so, it is not theme-led. Second clue: do the protagonists of each line **share the same goal and get triggered by the same inciting incident**? If yes → probably plot-led. **Chernobyl** looks theme-led, but all its lines share the same inciting event and each protagonist has a conscious goal tied to the main problem → it is actually plot-led, "like many disaster series."

**47. Maslow and the M-Factor** (Oberg 2.3, 2.6): **"the lower in the pyramid, the larger the potential audience"** (TWD, Lost, and Squid Game all sit at the bottom). **You must explore vertically**: Occupied's main problem is at the top (geopolitics); if its lines do not descend to family protection and survival, "it's unlikely it would have met such commercial success"; Money Heist is the reverse (the main problem sits on levels one and two, and each line explores upward into relationships, friendship, and morality); sitcom explores the middle levels and uses food/sex/excretion gags to descend to the bottom—**"If you go low with gags, you can set the main problem as high as you want."** Warning: a murder that has already happened + a whodunit = a top-level problem-solving story, "primarily intellectual … not a thriller at all." **M-Factor** = whether the audience, the genre, and the story-type are positioned coherently relative to Maslow and to the budget (scored 1–5). **Grounding motivation at the bottom makes a morally complex protagonist sympathetic**: Breaking Bad's main problem is ego/pride (the esteem level), yet its motivation is rooted in "security of family, of health."

**49. The inherent problems of an arena** (Miller ch1, 2015 American broadcast network perspective): **High school/college** has a "biological clock" (after graduation Glee was forced to become a two-track show and never recovered its ratings); **Family shows** get asked "What's episode 44?", and the solution is a family drama + a strong engine (Empire = King Lear + a hip-hop music company + drug money and crime; Sons of Anarchy = Hamlet + a motorcycle gang); **Relationship shows** can bring in only a limited number of new characters (Jane the Virgin kept its engine alive by adding crime elements); on **News shows** reporters can "meddle" but have **no license to intervene**—"A character's agency completes the story engine," which aligns with Rabkin ("A reporter is an observer, and a series demands a protagonist who is a participant"); **Anthology** resets every season, giving viewers a chance to drop the show. Note: this is a judgment about a specific market and year—Downton, Succession, and Shtisel show that a family ensemble's engine can come from institutional friction (inheritance, class, religion).

**43. Two additional rules for the mini-series series** (Rabkin ch12): American Crime Story opened the floodgates because it had a concept **"not defined by execution"** (the information was all in the public domain; anyone could legally imitate it tomorrow); **the first episode of every season of an anthology or mini-series series must supply anew "an immediate narrative hook that connects the audience to the protagonist"** (True Detective S1's interrogation-room device); you cannot assume that carrying over mood/tone/style will bring viewers along (Better Call Saul is the exception—the protagonist is already familiar). **Landau 2e ch1 (2022)**: **limited series are harder to sell**; platforms still prefer sustainable franchises; Chernobyl and Unorthodox are "awards bait," and a short episode count generally does not make money; **new shows use 10 episodes per season as the benchmark**.

**46. Choosing the wrong type locks you in** (Oberg's introduction and 3.1, question 9): **"The wrong combination of series type and story-type is the root cause of many series failures."** The most common box-in: **a character-led show completely resolves the protagonist's internal problem in Season 1 (film thinking)** → Season 2 must change story-type → the engine changes, and "This almost never works, unless it's an anthological series or maybe a serial-procedural hybrid." Mare of Easttown is fine as a limited series; a sequel could hardly avoid breaking the engine; Sex Education and Stranger Things are models of balance: the season resolves part of the main problem, and what remains is enough for the next season without changing the engine.

## IV. The Pilot Type Table, Network Strategies, and Douglas's Four Pilot Steps

| Type | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **premise pilot** | What happens in episode 1 is necessary "to start the series from day one"; Douglas: push the protagonist's quest forward from "before" | Lost, Barry, Grey's Anatomy, The Killing, The X-Files, Homeland, The Good Wife, Northern Exposure; **FNL** is counted by some as a hybrid, by Landau as a premise pilot (it ends with Jason Street's crippling injury, which affects all of Dillon) |
| **non-premise / ongoing / midstream** | "It may be the audience's first episode, but it's not day one for the characters."; the difficulty is **having no outsider to guide the audience in** | Succession, WandaVision, Mad Men, GoT, Downton, E.R., The West Wing, The Wire, The Office, Fleabag, Scandal |
| **hybrid** | Open at a climactic moment and then flash-forward (Watchmen, Mrs. Maisel) or flashback (Breaking Bad, BCS, Chernobyl); Douglas: use an external event to push forward a relationship that already exists | **The Sopranos is a hybrid—the crime world is already in motion, but it is day one of Tony and Melfi's therapy**; Boardwalk Empire (the day Prohibition takes effect) |
| **semi-cold start** (Oberg) | A short teaser sets the tone → about 10 minutes of flashback establish the backstory → the protagonist is shot at minute 12 → wakes at minute 15, **the protagonist and the audience equally ignorant** | The Walking Dead |

**37. Three network strategies and the "premise pilot trap"** (Blum ch17, 1999–2001 context, **the networks are outdated, the mechanism still holds**): the original line is that a premise pilot "is likely to be **a poor barometer** of how the series will develop." ① **NBC and ABC**—"the first half of the first episode should complete the setup of the premise, and the remaining part should reflect a 'representative' storyline"; ② **CBS**—"almost always rejects the 'premise pilot' in favor of the 'midstream pilot'"; ③ a pure premise pilot repeats poorly. **Smith ch20 agrees (half-hour comedy)**: a premise pilot cannot demonstrate an ordinary episode—"that Martian won't crash-land in someone's yard every week"; **"most network executives prefer to see a typical-episode pilot."** **Landau's different position**: it is not a trap; you only need to say in the pitch which kind you are pitching.

**39. Douglas's four pilot steps** (3e ch4): "In the beginning is the world."—the core is a **secret**, deep enough to take 88 or 100 hours to reveal; **a character's growth arc is usually not on the agenda on day one**. ① **Create the World** (Lindelof: **"the franchise is the world."**; "The question is who's going to be interacting with that world?"—Grey's and E.R. are "exactly the same show… only because of the people"); ② **Find the Story Springboards** ("This potential for future stories is the essential that differentiates writing a pilot from writing anything else on screen."); ③ **Populate the World** (use "draw out" rather than "introduce"; if you cannot name three, four, or five protagonists, go back to step one; **beginners should not attempt a large ensemble**); ④ **Make a Plan** (**the first ten pages decide life or death**; accelerate the action until we think she is about to succeed → **twist it and pull the rug out**; **"do not describe the world in prose"**—"If you have to explain it, something's not alive here.").

**38. Three ways to end**: ① **"The end of your pilot is the beginning of your series."** (Landau)—"If you're still only talking about plot, you're already off-base."; ② **Stick the landing**—"End your pitch of the pilot episode on a high note that portends future conflicts versus too much resolution and harmony."; ③ **a cliffhanger is not mandatory** (Miller ch6)—"Other shows work better with some resolution and acclimation to the hero's new life." (Masters of Sex vs GoT / 24 / The Affair). Calvisi's balancing sentence: the ending cannot pay off everything, or it will "feel too complete or 'closed-ended'—like a feature."

**40. Ten anxious questions** (Jeffries, Guest Speaker in the same chapter, teaches the pilot course at USC): **by the end of the pilot, list at least ten questions that will keep the audience anxious**—if viewers think they know the answers, they will not come back for episode two; those ten questions are also used to generate **ten short synopses for future episodes**. For each pilot she builds a research file three to four inches thick; the first note she always receives is "bring us into the heightened drama earlier"—**hook the executives first while keeping cards up your sleeve**.

## V. Case-by-Case Engine Breakdowns (Sopranos / Breaking Bad / Torchwood / Downton / Succession / Sakamoto)

**7. story pattern = the shape repeated in every episode** (Rabkin ch8). Sons of Anarchy is not "about a motorcycle gang"; it is about Jax protecting **three families**: perceive a threat → act to protect → the blowback endangers another family. Breaking Bad: "Walt has a problem. His solution works, but puts him in direct conflict with people more evil than him… it requires him to become more evil than his enemies." (S5E5 "Dead Freight" confirms it; S1E4 "Cancerman" breaks the pattern and therefore "feels wrong – slow, dull, disjointed.") The line the author repeats seven times: **"Every episode feels like it's moving a story forward, but it's really just repeating a pattern that was set in the pilot."**
→ Write the pattern in one sentence, then run it through **the second half of the pilot** in full (the first half only sets up and triggers). The pattern has two layers: structural constants (Martial Law has three big and two small fight sequences per episode; Law & Order switches from the police to the prosecutors at the midpoint) and character patterns (the more important layer).

**9. The strength of a concept does not determine sustainability: the Torchwood counterexample** (Rabkin ch9). Doctor Who "Rose" (2005) and the Torchwood pilot are almost identical in structure; by concept you would certainly bet on Torchwood ("alien crime police procedural," infinite cases), yet the former ran to season ten and the latter to four. The root cause: the disagreement between Torchwood's two leads is "she thinks Torchwood can help people, and he **sort of agrees**"; the alien threat is only **premises/pretexts, not stories**—"The story is what happens between the characters when they set out to deal with the alien menace."; and Doctor Who's conflict is alive in the pilot's scenes (**he looks at the macro, she looks at the individual**). → **The disease behind "I can't think of three episodes after the pilot" lies in the character conflict, not the concept.**

**26. Sakamoto-style dual-protagonist misalignment** (《花束般的恋爱》 (*We Made a Beautiful Bouquet*)): it relies not on opposition but on **consideration that produces the value turn** (in scene 85, while drying her hair, she says, "I've decided to look for a job"); each of the two gets a voice-over, and the episode ends with a strictly symmetrical pair of monologues; the mirror characters are arranged in four pairs (the one who breaks first, the one who marries first, the protagonist's past, the protagonist's replacement), and **the largest dialogue budget goes to the "protagonist's past" pair** (36 lines for a neighboring couple who appear only in the final scene, seven times the five lines of the "romantic rival").

**34. The three-piece kit for a sustainable conflict web** (Blum ch17, test three): **"If all the series' protagonists loved one another, all they could do is smile and agree with one another."** The engine = **a plot pattern of "group/family bonds" + character traits pre-installed in each character that necessarily cause friction with the others + a coincidence/situation that prevents them from leaving one another**; without all three, the show cannot last three to five years. **Downton Abbey** is exactly this (Calvisi's Compelling Crisis: "the British aristocracy struggles to preserve the estate and the old order under the pressures of the twentieth century, and the family is torn apart in the process")—class and the inheritance system themselves supply an inexhaustible friction surface. **Succession** puts seven or eight wants in one room and no longer needs to design conflict separately, with three devices: **humiliation transmitted downward** (Logan→Roman→Greg; this one makes office politics generate scenes automatically); **give everyone a small want achievable within the episode that directly conflicts with someone else's**; **compress the whole family into one building and then cut off communication** (phone-collection bags, the phone check desk at the castle). The S4 contrast: **Tom is the only person in the whole show whose script gives him no need—"which is exactly why he can win."**

**41. The Sopranos' "no-explanation mechanism"** (Chase foreword p0012 + pilot): the pilot deliberately does not explain Tony's official identity (he only says "The guy who says how things go is who I think I am"; Jackie Aprile never appears at all) or how "waste management" operates ("you don't need to know the details"). The basis: **"rarely is anything black and white in life,"** and he admired the foreign films he loved as a young man "for not having the endings spelled out or telling the audience what to think or feel." **This is also why the pilot does not end on a cliffhanger**: every line is "resolved," but the Junior/Livia conspiracy is just beginning, and the final shot is a pool with no duck. The major network's reason for rejecting it was not nudity, profanity, or violence but "the details, the complexity, the different pacing"—**"network television is about people saying exactly what's on their minds. This show is about people acting in a passive-aggressive—or aggressive—way."**

**An example of choosing a starting point (Landau 2e ch2, item 7)**: the novel *Queen's Gambit* begins with eight-year-old Beth already in the orphanage; the Netflix version begins with **adult Beth waking hungover in a Paris hotel bathtub**—more dynamic, immediately establishing the gap between "damaged girl" and "international champion," a gap that triggers the first flashback; in the flashback it also **keeps young Beth present to witness her mother's death** in order to reinforce empathy.

## VI. The Character Web: Character Library, Design Menu, and Examples

**19. Every series is a family drama** (Landau 2e ch6): **"Every television series is about a family – whether they're related by blood or not."** Citing the opening line of Tolstoy's 《安娜·卡列尼娜》 (Anna Karenina), Landau draws the corollary: **every TV family must be imperfect and, at some level, discontented**. Six kinds: the literal family drama (Succession, The Crown) / the workplace "family" (The Office, Ted Lasso) / the friends "family" (Friends, Insecure) / the hybrid (Better Call Saul, Pose) / the extended family (Mare of Easttown, Gomorrah, Shtisel) / **chosen family** (Pose, Vida).

**20. The familial role assignment library** (same chapter): authority figures, siblings (rivalry and alliance), spouses, wise elders, prodigal sons and daughters, black-sheep outcasts, **characters who behave like children**. You do not need to cover every gender and age; you need to determine "how each character will treat the others in both positive and negative directions." Law-enforcement partners (The Wire, True Detective S1) **inevitably sometimes act like a bickering married couple**; workplace colleagues compete for the boss's favor and must pass through the jealousy of sibling rivalry.

**23. Division of labor by expertise** (Landau 1e Tip 20): different skill sets are used to **create conflict, present multiple POVs, and help the audience tell characters apart**. Breaking Bad's division of labor: Walt (chemistry = cooking meth) / Jesse (small-time dealer = distribution) / Saul (drug lawyer = money laundering) / Mike (former cop = cleaner) / Skyler (bookkeeping = accounting) / Lydia (corporate executive = global operations, solely to stay alive); Grey's Anatomy **defines characters by specialty**; Mad Men's Harry Crane becomes irreplaceable by "becoming the television expert before television became a necessity." Two companion principles: **House's principle of contrarianism** (deliberately surrounding himself with doctors who disagree with him; in S4 he rejects an applicant precisely because the applicant thinks too much like him); **the Out of Their Element generator** (24's analyst Chloe is forced into field work; The Wire's Prez, unfamiliar with field work, fails to identify himself and shoots an undercover officer).

**25. Complementary dual protagonists** (Landau 1e Tip 9): **"two incomplete people together you get one whole."** (Mulder/Scully: belief and doubt; Booth/Brennan: law and science; The Americans' Elizabeth: loyal / Philip: wavering.) **The Hacks template** (2e ch9): **"their antagonistic relationship is rooted in the fact that they need one another. What's keeping them from admitting their mutual need is their individual shame."**—the two's shared behavioral mode is telling jokes instead of dealing with shame. **Gomorrah's logline generator**: "but **what if your family is also the danger?**"

**27. Shame and the void** (Landau 2e ch7, citing Brené Brown's *Daring Greatly*): **shame = our fear of disconnection**; the chain is "to connect you must be vulnerable → people tend to hide vulnerability → trauma creates **emotional voids** → voids produce coping mechanisms that avoid vulnerability." **Three kinds of defense (design menu)**: walling off (Shtisel, The Crown, Ozark) / numbing self-medication (Euphoria, Queen's Gambit, Succession) / defensive or self-destructive coping mechanisms (Fleabag, Barry). **"Anyone who challenges any of these constructs becomes an antagonist."**

**29. Two tellings of the same backstory** (Landau 2e ch7, Mare of Easttown): in E3 she tells a visiting professor about her father: "He was my best friend. He passed away when I was thirteen. He was a detective..."; only in E6 does she tell the therapist the truth—"Well, he shot himself. I was thirteen years old." → **give the polished version first, then the true version several episodes later in a safer or more forced setting; the gap is the character's shame.** Ensemble balance: the outsider is also defined by shame (the cold case Zabel "solved" was actually someone else's credit)—**"In this town, everyone is atoning for something."**

**31. Define the nature of the opposing force first, then decide whether it needs a face** (Landau 2e ch9): Chernobyl = the Soviet power structure; Fauda = **depends on POV**; Le Bureau = "the past"; **Succession = greed**; Barry = PTSD; **Mare of Easttown and Fleabag = grief**; Hacks = fear of obscurity; Pose = transphobia. The general principle (footnote attributed to UCLA professor emeritus Howard Suber): **"All villains are antagonists, but not all antagonists are villains."** Oberg 3.1 adds: a plot-led show's antagonist must be strong, memorable, and charismatic (Negan, Gus Fring), but **the villain's dangerousness cannot be a mystery**; only their essential nature can be.

**32. The villain is the hero of their own story** (same chapter): **"Every villain is the hero of his/her/their own story."**—"they usually feel justified, entitled, and downright righteous" (Handmaid's Tale's Aunt Lydia genuinely believes she is helping "her girls" find salvation). Four corollaries: the antagonist usually wants what the protagonist has; **almost always tries to subvert the truth** in order to maintain the status quo (in Mare of Easttown the whole town conspires to keep the killer's secret); if the tension is insufficient, **strengthen the antagonist** (the more Barry resists the killer's daily life, the more bodies pile up); the most iconic antagonists combine positive and negative traits (Hannibal Lecter = gourmet + outstanding psychiatrist; Euphoria's Nate—"Hurt people hurt people."). Structural judgment: **"as soon as that primary goal is successfully attained, the series is usually over – unless it pivots to another overarching mission or goal."** The misalignment case: Rue treats her mother as the antagonist, "in truth her mother is her chief ally… **Her addiction is her antagonist.**"

**33. Do not set up straw men** (the Kings, Landau 2e ch4): "98 percent of TV is setting up straw men and straw women to be knocked down because everybody would rather enjoy propaganda than drama. **Propaganda is confirming what you already believe.**" What is more valuable is to let **regular characters we like disagree with one another, with each of them having a point** (The Good Wife S4E14 "Red Team, Blue Team" stages a mock trial inside the firm).

**22. Three scene-level rules** (same chapter): **"the people who love you most know how to hurt you the most."** → Before writing a two-person scene, determine "which button they know the other has," then decide whether to push it; the new-character test—"ask yourself not only who this new character is, but also **how the arrival of this new character might impact your regular characters**"; the subtext formula—**"The subtext of virtually any scene is familial power dynamics, psychological wounds from the past, and subtle movements that reaffirm clan position."**

**24. The rational axis** (Rabkin appendix, Mooresville pitch): the female lead loathes all authority, and everyone around her indulges her as a willful child (love without respect); the male lead treats her as a CEO and argues when he disagrees, but **"start from the position that they are both rational, intelligent adults"**—"When Marshall treats her like an adult, she starts to act like one." → A relationship drama's mutual attraction needs a **structural reason**, not just chemistry.

**30. The pace at which a flaw is revealed** (Landau 1e Tip 8): a flaw must originate in backstory if it is to run long, but in the pilot the backstory is only **obliquely hinted at**—"what we don't know about a character is often more compelling than what we do know." Don Draper is a womanizer in the pilot, and **only in S6 do we learn his mother was a prostitute**; Scandal only reveals in episode 6 that Fitz's marriage is a political convenience. Counterexample: at the end of Girls S2, Hannah's OCD "seemed to emerge from out of nowhere," and audiences rebelled.

**28. A grounded superpower is also a liability** (Landau 2e ch5, Gilligan's character-building method): Walt's superpower is "the ability to lie to himself"; Olivia Pope can solve everyone's problems except her own; Beth Harmon = chess god + addict. The boxed text of 1e Tip 8: **"when a character's greatest strength also becomes his or her greatest liability… The trick to dramatic sustainability is keeping your main character in this form of limbo."**

## VII. Long Quotations and the Original Criteria

**12. Extant's threefold error** (same chapter, textbook case of the premise trap): ① every problem reduces to "how did she get pregnant," and at most nine months later there is an answer; ② **the actions required to answer these questions cannot generate interesting stories**; ③ the pilot **actually shows the sex scene**, so the only suspense left is "when will the lie be exposed." Verdict: **"execution is not the problem here. Conception is."** → Do not dramatize the core mystery in the pilot.

**13. Story landmines** (Rabkin ch9) = character conflicts planted in the pilot's **subtext** and detonated several episodes later. The reasons: 60 pages cannot hold everything; the audience must not be distracted; **the series needs room to grow**—"if there is nothing in episode 35 that wasn't already fully explored in episode one, why would anyone bother with the 33 in between?" Positive examples: Doctor Who S1E6, the Dalek in the museum (Rose sees it as a suffering individual, the Doctor sees it as the last remnant of the most evil race); Crazy Ex-Girlfriend's Rebecca realizes at the end of the pilot that "giving up everything for love would be crazy" → the fear that she really is crazy. → Plant one in each major character (an unspoken fear/desire/contradiction) and write down the episode in which you expect it to detonate; it must operate at the character level—"she is a detective's daughter" does not count.

**15. sweet spot** (Landau 1e Tip 16, p0228 boxed text): **"the intersection between the show's genre, tone, theme, central conflict, franchise, and central question/mystery"**; the one-hour version is **"a series must deliver the promise of its premise every week"**—"This is the tacit contract the showrunner is making with the audience." TWD straddles the fence between horror and family drama ("Remove the blood and guts and the show collapses. But if there were only gore, the show would also falter."); Homeland = duality. The formula from 2e ch4: **sweet spot = character development × franchise**. Companion concept **currency**: Homeland's currency = not knowing whom to trust, TWD's = the survival of the core group, "spend it wisely."

**16. The marketing intern test** (Rabkin ch10): imagine being a junior marketing staffer trying to persuade an *Entertainment Weekly* editor to give you a few inches of coverage. "a caring doctor who heals patients by really listening"—"Um, well, it's really good…"; "King Arthur resurrected in modern-day London to battle Morgana Le Fay"—"I'll get that disc in the mail right away!" **Bigger is not a bigger budget; it is the scale of the conflict that defines the show** (the Ironside remake died after three episodes; its competition that season was The Blacklist).

**17. "Big does not have to mean shallow."** Three ways to make it bigger (same chapter): **change the profession**—the Sopranos originated with Chase wanting to write about a **TV writer** ravaged by the network, tormented by a monstrous mother, who ends up in a psychiatrist's office after a panic attack; nobody wanted to watch it, **one change: the profession**; **change the place**—The Bridge is a traditional two-detective partnership; the key is the U.S.–Mexico border, a world that had never been on television; **change the era**—after rejecting the present day and his own adolescence for the same theme, he landed on a family drama of Cain and Abel ("that's when you know you've hit the thing you need to write"). Conclusion: **"the way to write for the market is to write the things that are most personal and most specifically unique to you."** The opposing position (Miller ch1–2): **"having great characters trumps high concept every time"** (Modern Family's premise is ordinary and rests entirely on its characters; Gotham is high concept yet mediocre), and he opposes "this-meets-that" pitches.

**48. What's at stake?** (Oberg glossary) = "the question developers ask most often when they don't care about the story": what happens if the main problem is not solved, and it must make people **sad/anxious/afraid**. Two bottom lines: **"What you really want to avoid is the situation where we don't know what's at stake in any given episode"**; multiple storylines must connect (theme-led through theme, plot-led through plot). Landau 1e Tip 11: **"Determine what they value most and put it at risk… a series with no suspense will be D.O.A."** Half-hour shows and relationship dramas (2e ch9): **"the stakes aren't life or death, but they will ideally feel like life and death to the characters."** (Ted discovers his fiancée has never seen *Star Wars*—"If Stella doesn't like this movie, I can't marry her."—a trivial problem hung on a deeper fear of intimacy.)

**8. Five tests for whether a show has legs**

| Criterion | Source | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 99 stories | Rabkin ch6 | If you cannot think of a second one, it does not hold up |
| **The six-episode test** | Landau 1e Tip 7 (p0122) | "at least **six** future episode ideas beyond the pilot"; if you cannot, "re-tool, revise, rejigger, and then re-launch" |
| Three ready for a pitch / five or six for a presentation | Landau 2e ch14 / Blum ch17 | "at least **three** examples" / "usually includes roughly **five or six** ideas for new stories" |
| **100 episodes and the four questions** | Goldberg & Rabkin ch17 | What is the franchise? What are the central conflicts at the heart of the franchise? Are those conflicts complex and interesting enough to drive **100 or more episodes**? Is it fresh and different, and yet familiar enough to fit into mainstream television? |
| **The five-story test** | Blum ch17 | If five or six follow-up stories are too similar in **source** (the protagonist's personal life / technology / paid commissions / a past institution / public issues) and **ending shape** (rescue / persuasion / prevention / compromise / incomplete victory), the engine does not hold up |

**14. Suffering is not conflict; two attributes of one person must obstruct each other** (same chapter, counterexample Feed the Beast): **"suffering isn't conflict, and so suffering alone can't drive story"** (the wife dies in a drunk-driving accident, and Tommy can only mope; if she were missing, she could be searched for; if she were dying, there could be a fight; if she were murdered, there could be an investigation); Dion is a chef and also an addict, but the two do not conflict (compare Vinyl's Richie, whose addiction fuels his creativity). What the show later adds is all **plot landmines rather than character landmines** (the lawyer is the detective's daughter; a pregnancy) → it degenerates into a soap opera.
