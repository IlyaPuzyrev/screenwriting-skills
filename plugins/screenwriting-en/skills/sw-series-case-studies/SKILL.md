## I. West Wing: The Anchor Text for the Broadcast Four-Act + Teaser

Sorkin's *The West Wing Script Book* (Newmarket, 2002) collects **six teleplays written solely by Sorkin** (S1E01 Pilot, S1E03 A Proportional Response, S2E01–02 In the Shadow of Two Gunmen I/II, S2E18 17 People, S2E22 Two Cathedrals), each with a one-page author's preface. These are **shooting scripts**, with their own `TEASER` / `ACT ONE–FOUR` / `END OF SHOW` markings and scene-numbered pages — **the act boundaries are documentary fact, not inference**. That makes this the only four-act sample from a 1999–2001 NBC one-hour show (42–44 minutes of main program) that can be quantified page by page.

- **Act-page table (PDF pages)**: Pilot **8 + 19/18/12/11 = 68**; A Proportional Response **4 + 14/14/18/19 = 69**; Two Gunmen I **7 + 21/14/11/14 = 67**; II **2 + 11/14/16/18 = 61**; 17 People **6 + 14/10/10/18 = 58**; Two Cathedrals **3 + 13/13/12/17 = 58**.
- **Teasers run 2–8 pages (median 5)**, and their length is inversely proportional to how much backstory the audience needs: the premise pilot is longest, the second half of the two-parter is shortest (2 pages), and the season finale is 3 pages with a scene heading reading `— CONTINUOUS` that runs straight out of the previous episode's final scene.
- **Act length comes in only two shapes**: front-loaded only in the Pilot (it needs two acts to build the world); **the other five are back-loaded, with Act Four the longest or second-longest**. Acts Two and Three compress most easily (17 People 10/10); **Act Four is never shorter than 11 pages**; total page count declines across the season (68/69 → 67/61/58/58).
- **Four to seven storylines, four as the standard**; the bottle episode has five and the season finale has seven, the most — **when there is no location work to shoot, the number of threads is where pace comes from**.
- **Each episode has one "procedural line"** (the Cuban raft / the retaliatory strike / the FAA terrorist attack / the Haitian embassy), which **carries no act out at all**; it supplies interruptions, deadlines, and the final number handed to the protagonist (Pilot's four acts: 1,200 depart / 700 turn back / 350 missing / 137 detained).
- **Eight types of act out**: the suspenseful question / the reversal line / the emotional revelation / wordless action / the frozen prop / a repeated line whose meaning is rewritten / a setup cut off / a gesture that pays off a plant. The first two episodes use traditional hooks; the last four lean increasingly toward the emotional type.
- **The bottle episode was forced by budget**: Warner ordered "no guest cast / no locations / no new sets / no extras / as little film as possible"; Sorkin's transformation was "I can write a stage play now; and it's time the President came clean about his condition" — **the restriction became the trigger for "time to push the main line"**.
- **A wordless act out must first teach its grammar**: in Act Three a secondary character recites the tells one by one (hands in pockets / looks away / smiles = he has decided); in Act Four at the press conference he repeats those three actions without a word, `just as he smiles we FADE TO BLACK`. **The corridor walk-and-talk and the gesture that pays off a plant are the two most copyable things in these six episodes.**

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Start each episode with four threads** (A main / B comedy or relationship / C emotional / D seeding characters for the season arc); when resources are tight, **add threads rather than lengthen scenes**, and pair them with a procedural line as a metronome.
2. **The corridor is a free set**: one stretch of dialogue crosses three or four `INT. X — CONTINUOUS` headings while delivering exposition + planting a seed + landing a joke.
3. **Turn a secret into a countable number** (15 → 16 → **17**; "the **35th** in the last two hours"), and the audience gets a progress bar.
4. **A button should say less**: an answer can go unspoken, a joke can be left unfinished, an emotion can be given as a single action; **cut to black mid-action**.

> Leave the disagreement on the record: Sorkin writes plainly, "I put no organization to the writing process. The writing is done on the fly." — which conflicts with the outline/bible camp (see `sw-writers-room`). The irony: the only pre-planned two-part structure in the book is the most orderly.

## II. Sopranos: The Engine and the Pilot's Art of Omission

Chase et al., *The Sopranos: Selected Scripts from Three Seasons* (Warner Books, 2002): five **final shooting scripts** (Pilot, College, The Happy Wanderer, The Knight in White Satin Armor, Pine Barrens) plus a four-page introduction by Chase. HBO scripts have **no ACT or teaser markings**, so every "segment" in this section is **an inference judged from the therapy-room frame and the turning points**; the only documentary facts are the scene numbers (the Pilot runs 67 pages, sc. 1–81).

- **About 35 beats per episode** (Chase's own words): "**thirty-five beats**—**thirteen** or so in the A and B stories, usually **five to six** in the C story, and sometimes a D story with just a few beats." **A and B carry equal weight, C is secondary, and D is usually a comic runner.**
- **The season theme is not pre-set**: at the start of a season he writes only arcs/touchstones and is not bound by them; S1's theme (and the incapacitated mother) **"organically emerged" only in retrospect at the end of the season**.
- **The pilot carries twice as many threads**: A (panic attack → therapy → the ducks = fear of losing family) / B (the uncle wants to kill a man in a friend's restaurant) / C (the garbage contract and debts → insurance fraud) / D (mother–daughter conflict) + E (season-arc seeds) + three runners.
- **The therapy room is a device, not an explainer**: the Pilot has **11 therapy scenes** (sc. 1/13/15/18/22/24/44/48/52 empty chair/70/74), whose functions are narrative frame + source of V.O. + theme namer + plot. **The audience always knows more than the therapist**: having just watched him hit a man with a car and beat someone, he says "Nothing. We had coffee."
- **The confidant device can change hands**: College has **zero therapy scenes**, replaced by the wife confessing to a priest; Chase chose it because it is "self-contained" and could stand as a feature — it is also the only one of the five with **just two threads, A and B**.
- **The pilot's ending does not detonate**: after the uncle sounds her out, the script says "She says nothing… **He has her blessing.**" Then the family goes inside, `PAN to the still and silent pool.` **Close on an absence (no ducks), not an event.**
- **A bottle episode is not a single thread**: Pine Barrens traps the two men in the woods overnight (A story), **while B/C/D continue as usual**; three therapy scenes form the B-story spine and never intersect the woods line; the Russian's fate is never resolved in the series, **and the episode's final emotional landing is given to the therapy room**.

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Allocate threads by 35 beats**, list beats by thread first and then **cut them apart and reorder**; **name the theme in retrospect at season's end**, never manufacture story to fit it.
2. **The irony formula of the double life**: first let the audience see the violence, then let the character say something respectable and false to someone else ("We had coffee." / "Talking helps.").
3. **The B-story wish is fulfilled by the A-story disaster** (the daughter gets the solo because her classmate's father was "eaten" by the protagonist) — find the point of contact that makes the audience happy and uncomfortable at the same time.
4. **Refuse three times, then agree**, and on agreement use a rule to transfer responsibility to the other party ("don't say short if you don't mean short").

## III. Downton Abbey Season Two: Weaving 8–12 Threads in a British Ensemble

Fellowes, *Downton Abbey: The Complete Scripts, Season Two*: **a complete shooting-script collection with 419 author's footnotes**, Ep1–8 plus the Christmas special. **It has ACT markings for ITV's commercial breaks**, with varying act counts: **Ep1 six acts, Ep2–5 four, Ep6 five, Ep7 five, Ep8 six, special seven**. The footnotes are scarcer than the scripts themselves — the only text in the whole batch where the author annotates his own work.

- **Scene counts**: Ep1 Sc 1–71 / Ep3 1–73 / Ep4 1–60 / Ep5 1–61 / Ep6 1–64A / Ep7 1–56 / Ep8 1–77 / **special 1–110**. The season's scripts were "too long", ITV allowed 70–75 minutes, which meant an extra commercial break and viewer complaints, and **Season Three returned to a strict hour** (period: the 2011–12 ITV schedule).
- **A grid of 19 threads** (full table in reference.md): running simultaneously — Ep1 ten, Ep5 twelve, Ep6 eleven, Ep8 eleven, special nine.
- **Ensemble specification (footnote 326)**: lunch, dinner, and drawing-room scenes "always have to **advance about six or seven stories at the same time, often by no more than a line**".
- **Only four types of act out**: the transgressive request (bribing the maid) / one line that states a hidden fact ("He is standing.") / the Dowager-style button ("I have a cold.") / a bodily fact (a corpse, handcuffs, collapsing in tears, reading the lesson in public).
- **The rule for killing characters**: at least one regular has to die; the victim must be familiar and beloved — "**It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time.**"; and **the audience is deliberately misled into guessing the wrong victim**.
- **Obstruction comes from institutions**: written evidence, the illegitimate child having no legal claim on the father, buying off a scandal, the Decree Nisi revoked for collusion, brachial plexus (chosen because it is "serious but needs no special effects") — **the antagonist is simply the person who knows the rules best**.

**Transferable techniques**
1. **"The Downton dilemma = both sides are right"** (the most frequent note in the footnotes): "a typical Downton argument, where hopefully you're on both sides." **If one side is obviously wrong, the thread fails.**
2. **Make a thread table for each episode**, giving each thread its **entry scene number and exit scene number**; 8–12 is the ceiling, and 1–2 of them are unrelated to the main crisis.
3. **Run the upstairs and downstairs emotional lines in parallel, equal weight, cross-cut**, saying the same theme in completely different registers.

## IV. Fleabag: A Six-Episode Half-Hour Season Template, Where the Device Is the Season Arc

Waller-Bridge, *Fleabag: The Scriptures* (Sceptre, 2019): **12 shooting scripts across two seasons** (about 25 minutes each) plus a creative memoir at the end of the volume. **A half-hour show has no ACT markings** — the segmentation is **an inference from locations and time blocks**; the documentary facts are the fixed device and the roughly **2,900–3,600 lines** of script per episode in S1. The only sample that designs the fourth wall **as a season arc**.

- **Episode skeleton**: `cold open (1–3 minutes, carrying a joke or a mystery) → TITLES: FLEABAG → 3–7 segments → button (≤1 page, often wordless)`; **the sole exception, S2E6**, swaps in `TITLE CARD: THE WEDDING`.
- **The positional function of the six episodes (strictly symmetrical across both seasons, copyable)**: 1 spreads out all the characters + the season's mystery / 2 converts emotion into a followable desire / 3 a family occasion + the first laying down of cards / 4 **the bottle episode** (retreat / confessional: a closed space + one prohibition) / 5 the high point of false victory / 6 everyone recalled to one closed social occasion (an exhibition / a wedding), where every thread closes.
- **Neither season is a premise pilot**: episode 1 does not push the protagonist into a new situation through an event; it relies on **a web of relationships that is already wounding itself = the engine**; on each character's first appearance the protagonist nails them with a single line to camera.
- **Five-stage reversal of the device = the season arc**: in S1 the camera is an accomplice → S1E6 **the camera chases her into a corner and forces out the flashback** → S2E3 the priest sees it for the first time ("What was that? Where did you just go?") → S2E5 she yells "STOP THAT" and pushes the camera away → S2E6 she asks the camera not to follow, turns and waves from a distance, and the last line is the stage direction "Goodbye."
- **The whole season gives only one long monologue not addressed to camera** (S2E4, the confessional) = **the emotional high point of the entire show**.
- **"Visual Sentences"**, the image the director supplied: **Boo standing across the street, traffic sweeping past** — inserted whenever she laughs, has sex, or "feels fine", and the audience knows **she isn't OK**.
- **The device was tested before it was written**: the dramaturg filmed her on a phone in the kitchen, making tea and talking to camera; five minutes later she put the phone down: "**It works.**" **The only criterion for a further season**: "**If she has something else to learn, then we have more story to tell.**"

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Lay out a six-episode season as 1 spread / 2 desire / 3 cards down / 4 bottle / 5 false victory / 6 closed occasion and settlement.**
2. **The device is itself the season arc**: in Season Two, let **one character see it** and escalate episode by episode (discovery → challenge → order to stop → she pushes it away → she asks it to leave). When that road has been walked, the show is over.
3. **The protagonist tells jokes only to camera and the truth only to people**; one long monologue not to camera in the entire season.
4. **Write roles for specific actors and give the actors veto power** (the Godmother was created after meeting the actress and "cracking open the pilot script to rewrite the ending"; the closing "I love you too" was added at the actress's insistence).

> Joke mechanics (two-part setup, punch word at the end, topper, three to-camera beats plus a fourth flipped by someone else) belong to `sw-sitcom-comedy`.

## V. Calvisi's Eight Pilot Minute-by-Minute Beat Sheets

> The beat-by-beat tables for the eight pilots (beat name → minute mark) are in section 5 of [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md).

Calvisi's *Story Maps: TV Drama*, CASE STUDIES: beat sheets for eight pilots, **with the number before each beat being the minute mark**. The author's own stated methodological limits must be quoted alongside: based on **transcribing the finished episode beat by beat** ("transcribed as I watched them"), **one minute ≈ one page**, and mostly only landmark beats kept. So it is **a measurement of the finished structure, not script fact**; in shows without act markings the act boundaries are **his own inserted inference** — "I've inserted my own act breaks… in what feel like the natural positions."

**Three cross-case rulers (aggregated across eight examples, directly checkable)**: Midpoint falls almost always at **24–30 minutes**; Assumption of Power always at **36–48 (mostly 37–40)**; Declaration of War / All Is Lost always at **about four-fifths of the running time (43–52)**.

- **Scandal (2012, 45′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C/D)**: **the shortest of the eight**, "front-loaded, with both **Catalyst and Inciting Incident occurring in the six minute Teaser**", launching three threads in six minutes. In an early draft the blood-covered man did not enter until page **12**; the rewrite moved him earlier. **The theme only tightens at 41–42 minutes**. The four thread exits are staggered: 39–40 (D) → 42 (A) → 43–44 (B) → 44–45 (C).
- **Mr. Robot (2015, 64′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C)**: the closest fit to the template. **The Midpoint is stretched into a single 25–30 block**; **AOP is "proposed but refused"** (42–48), with the real action held back for DOW (50–54). Exits 56–58 (B) → 60 (C) → **62 (A) last**, the reverse of Scandal.
- **True Detective (2014, 58′, only A/B threads)**: **no Teaser**; the book is candid about its structure — "my original map was only four acts in total, but I chose to define a short **Act Four as a 'bridge act'** since this section feels like a separate movement." The A story is the relationship and the 2012 interrogation, the B story the 1995 case; **AOP is split into B at 40′ and A at 48′**. Four **long-line plants** are explicitly listed (the first victim / the killer's scar / the Yellow King / the break-up event).
- **The Walking Dead (2010, 66′ — an extended premiere slot, Teaser + 5, A/B/C)**: **the model premise pilot**, where the Week-to-Week series **does not yet exist** in this episode. **Two major characters never appear again in the rest of the season**, and the author gives a threefold defense: they are vehicles for delivering the world's rules / **the protagonist is in every scene, so a B story is needed for the audience to breathe** / they stand in for family members who have been separated. The network act-design principle: **each act brings a new movement and an independent engine, and closes clearly at the act break, leaving only the hint of "more to come".** Plants come in pairs: the walker mob 43′ → 60′.
- **Game of Thrones (2011, 60′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C/D)**: **multiple protagonists**, and of the three co-protagonists **only one actually makes a decision, and offscreen**; the defense is that the pilot's job is **world-building**, selling "the promise of high adventure" rather than paying it off. **The Inciting Incident arrives 18 minutes late, in Act Two**. **Act heads rely on location TITLE cards** (Winterfell / King's Landing / Pentos). **The beats run once per thread**: Shadow Showdown three times (32 / 38–39 / 41–43), AOP twice and neither belongs to the protagonist (40 / 46). **Thread letters are assigned by screen time**: Dany, who only appears in Act Three, is the B story. Long plants: the dragon eggs planted at 53–54 and hatched only at season's end; Bran climbing the tower planted at 23–24 and paid off at 58–60.
- **Breaking Bad (2008, 57′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C) — the golden reference for the template**: built from a number of **2-minute scenes**, where **scene length itself is a pacing tool**; several scenes were cut before broadcast, and the result became "**a nearly perfect example of the template**". **AOP is split into two adjacent beats** (A at 36–37 / B at 38–39); **Act Five returns to the Teaser's image to close the loop** (49–51 against 1–3). **Positions in the pilot will flip**: the brother-in-law is essentially an ally in the pilot, the partner a Shadow.
- **House of Cards (2013, 54′, A/B/C/D)**: written like a feature screenplay, **with no act markings**; Calvisi inserts Teaser + 5 himself. Three deviations: **FT/FC falls in Act One (14–15), the only exception among the eight**; **Act Two has only three beats and a gap from 19′ to 25′**; **the CLIMAX closes C → B → A, with the A story kept for the last scene**, closing the loop with the Teaser's dog-hit at 52–54.
- **Mad Men (2007, 48′ finished / 52-page script, four acts with no Teaser — the fewest acts among the eight)**: the reverse-engineering process is worth copying: the script has only **three FADE OUTs**, but the finished structure does not match the script, and he **iterated several times** before settling on "four acts, no teaser". **The A story closes at the end of Act Two** (27–32), with Acts Three and Four handed to the character lines; **ALL IS LOST is split into one each for A/B/C**; **the BIG ENDING is a single scene that turns on information**. **The pilot opens with "the louder theme"** ("It's a man's world" first, the real theme taking over later).

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Move the Catalyst and Inciting Incident earlier** (Scandal from page 12 to the teaser's sixth minute).
2. **Give every thread its own exit beat and stagger the closes**; the default is **secondary threads close first and the protagonist's thread is left hanging** — doing the reverse must be deliberate.
3. **When the protagonist is in every scene, there must be a B story to let the audience breathe** — even if that thread's characters never appear again this season.
4. **Pair every plant with a payoff and mark them as pairs**; **list long-line plants recovered in later episodes separately** (follow True Detective's four).

## VI. How to Read Landau's Structure Master Table

> The full seven-column table of 47 shows is in section 6 of [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md).

Landau's *The TV Showrunner's Roadmap* (first edition), appendix **"Top Television Series by Structure"**: **47 shows**, seven columns = title | year | format | network | structure | pilot type (premise / non-premise / hybrid) | showrunner. **A warning about the text's identity**: the scan of that page was rotated and garbled by OCR, the notes reconstruct the legible parts, and **blank cells in the table mean "unreadable", not "absent from the book"**. Full table in reference-pilots.md.

- **Act-count distribution**: `teaser + 4 acts` is the mode for one-hour shows (Breaking Bad, CSI, Law & Order, Mad Men, The West Wing, The X-Files, ER, etc.); `teaser + 5 acts` (Friday Night Lights, Parenthood, Touch; Justified adds a tag); **6 acts with no teaser** (Grey's Anatomy, Once Upon a Time, Revenge, Rizzoli & Isles); **Scandal is "6 acts, with the short first act serving as teaser, then the title card"**; **Royal Pains has 7 acts** (the most in the table); premium cable and streaming have many **"no act break"** shows (Deadwood, Dexter, Downton Abbey, Game of Thrones, Homeland, House of Cards, Weeds, The Sopranos, etc.). Sitcoms: `cold open + 2 acts + tag` (Big Bang Theory, Two and a Half Men), `cold open + 2 acts` (The Office), `4 acts with a short first act` (Modern Family).
- **premise vs non-premise is more useful**: those marked **premise** are mostly shows whose "world must be built from zero" (24, American Horror Story, Breaking Bad, Deadwood, Lost, Revenge, The Sopranos, The X-Files + three sitcoms); those marked **non-premise** (typical-episode form) are mostly procedurals and ensembles (CSI, Dexter, **Downton Abbey**, Game of Thrones, Law & Order, Modern Family, The Office, Scandal, **The West Wing**); **only two are marked hybrid**: Damages and The Walking Dead.
- **"No act break" does not mean no acts**: the same book's main text points out that HBO's Deadwood scripts have **clear four acts**, while Showtime's Homeland scripts **do not** — write act breaks that "grab at the plot level and also fit the given slot".
- **Page-count norms (period: around 2013)**: a half-hour multi-camera sitcom in double spacing runs about **50 pages**; a half-hour single-camera / dramedy about **30 pages**; a one-hour show **48–63 pages**, with pilots often longer; Moonlighting, The West Wing, and E.R. could reach **85 pages or more** because of rapid-fire dialogue and still fit a one-hour slot.
- **How to use it**: treat the table as **a sampler of shows of the same type** — before writing a pilot, find the row with the tone and pace you want and ask item by item: is there a teaser? how many act breaks? how many scenes per act? is there a tag? is the pilot a premise or a typical episode? Then write to that row's specification.
- **A show's act count can change**: The Walking Dead is listed as `teaser + 4 acts`, with a note that **Mazzara changed it to teaser + 5 acts after taking over** (AMC wanted one more commercial slot) — **the act count is a function of the commercial slots** (this table reflects the landscape around 2013; the platform map has changed a great deal, and this causality still holds, but check the specific show's act count season by season).

**Transferable techniques**
1. **First set the specification from the row of a show of the same type** (teaser or not / act count / scenes per act / tag), then start writing.
2. **Derive the act count from the commercial slots**; do not mistake streaming's "no act break" for "no structure" — you can still write four clear acts in the script.
3. **Decide the pilot type first**: if the world must be built from zero, write a premise; procedurals and ensembles default to the typical-episode form.

> Leave the disagreement on the record: Landau writes "**No outline, no paycheck.**" (the studio and network need sign-off); in an interview in the same book Mazzara objects: "I don't use outlines… they are used to engage TV and studio executives… None of their notes are applicable to script." The substitute is **a two-to-three-page prose story document + a beat sheet of two or three words per beat** (e.g. "Rick kills Shane."). **Choosing**: if you need platform sign-off, or you are handing the script to a freelancer (the Sopranos rule was that freelancers must follow the outline strictly), write an outline; if you are the showrunner and rewrite every episode yourself, you can use only a beat sheet. See `sw-writers-room`.

## VII. Miller: Two Scenes Broken Beat by Beat, and Four Student Pilot Outlines

> The item-by-item tables for the two escalation scenes and the act/scene counts of the four student outlines are in section 7 of [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md).

Miller's *The Hero Succeeds*, chapter 9, gives **the five-piece scene structure** (hero's want / obstacle / escalations / decision / resolution, adapted from ER writer Lance Gentile) with two item-by-item escalation breakdowns; the appendix contains **four actual student pilot outlines** (numbered scenes, standard scene headings, act markings, character names in full caps, with a thumbnail description at first appearance, almost no dialogue). The two scenes' durations and beat counts are **the author's own counts from watching**; the placement of the six landmarks in the outlines is **inferred by reverse-engineering from the model**.

- **Hannibal pilot, the FBI men's room scene (from about 16:06, 1 minute 57 seconds, 14 escalations)**: want = he wants to understand the killer who baffles him; obstacle = his boss wants to know **now**. The escalation chain (excerpt): asking what he is doing in the bathroom → he deflects with humor ("I like the smell of urine cake.") → the boss swats the humor aside and yells at an agent who comes in, "Use the ladies' room!" → asking whether he respects his own judgment → he admits confusion and says **the killer may not be a textbook psychopath** (new information) → pressing him on what he is hiding → the boss throws down the evidence (no semen, no saliva, the victim still a virgin).
- **Decision (= the scene's climax)**: he defends the killer as if he were the killer — "That isn't how the killer loves them. The killer kills them quickly and mercifully." **Resolution**: the boss believes him ("A sensitive psychopath"). The next scene has the boss asking a psychiatrist to evaluate him (because he has just seen him break down and then break through), and the scene after that has him in the evidence room **trying not to look at the bodies** — **scenes connect by emotional logic, not by plot points**.
- **HTGAWM teaser, the woods scene (teaser starts at about 31 seconds, 1 minute 40 seconds, 11 escalations)**: want = retrieve the body and bury it; obstacle = the two of them must stay where they are. The escalation chain is entirely **reasonable objections**: take the murder weapon back → wipe it clean and "hide it in plain sight" → leave the body where it is → agree only to retrieve the weapon → "don't carry a body across campus on the busiest night of the year" → **the ground is frozen** → we have all night to dig → "this is murder, none of us knows what we're talking about". **Decision**: 2–2, a coin toss. The teaching point = **exposition through conflict**.
- **Hard conditions**: a scene needs at least two characters and two wants, **diametrically opposed is strongest**; **each scene still has only one hero, and "Your hero may change from scene to scene"**; escalations **minimum 3, but more is better**; **prefer action** — "If all of your escalations are oral… Your TV show turns into a radio show."
- **Scene counts of the four outlines**: *Jerusalem, TN* **33** (teaser 6/8/8/6/5); *Girl Out of Water* **44** (9/5/10/9/11); *Minotaur* **43** (3/9/7/**16**/8); *Exit Stage Left* (half-hour single-camera) **27** (8/6/6/4/3). **All four are teaser + four acts**; scene count does not equal duration (comedy scenes run longer).
- **First-and-last mirror written into the outline**: *Girl Out of Water* scene 25 states outright — "As in the first shot of the episode, Sierra is facedown in the water. **This time, she's not moving.**"; and **the B story's hero succeeds at the end of Act Two in the same act in which the A story's hero fails** — "The juxtaposition of landmark scenes makes them pack more of an emotional punch."

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Count your escalations**: fewer than 3 and it is not a scene; make at least half of them action.
2. **Use argument to deliver setup**: every objection must be reasonable, and the information hides inside the objection.
3. **Follow a key scene with two scenes of "consequences in emotional logic"**, not with the next plot point.

## VIII. Goldberg & Rabkin's Appendix Beat Sheets (Actual Artifacts)

> The act-by-act scene tables for the three beat sheets are in section 8 of [reference-pilots.md](reference-pilots.md).

*Successful Television Writing*, appendices D–I: **four actual beat sheets + two leave-behinds**, two of which print **the leave-behind and the beat sheet for the same episode side by side**, so you can read word for word how "a one-page story area" grows into a thirty-scene outline. The header wording is itself the draft stage: `Story Area` → `Rough Beat Sheet` → `First Draft Beat Sheet` → `Revised Outline`. Two industry facts (period: late 1990s–early 2000s): **beat sheets have no uniform format and follow the showrunner's habits**; "you get paid whether the script is shot or not!"

**Martial Law "Sammo Blammo" (action buddy show, a textbook constraint device)**
- **Scene counts**: TEASE **2** / Act One **5** / Act Two **8** / Act Three **5** / Act Four **10** = about **30 scenes**. Against the same book's "6–7 scenes per act, four acts" and "fifteen to twenty-five scenes": **the later acts of an action show visibly fragment**.
- **The rules of a constraint device must be written out in full**: a bomb is strapped to the protagonist's chest and can be disarmed only with a single encrypted mini-disc; opening the casing / freezing / smashing / magnetizing / X-raying all detonate it; it has a motion sensor — **if the protagonist stops moving, it blows**; as a "fair warning" it will **BEEP FOUR TIMES, and the fifth is KA-BLAMMO**.
- **A small trouble planted in Act One is recovered in Act Four**: the street thug who was chased off comes back bandaged, **at the worst possible moment** (the protagonist is about to run into the bank).
- **The Act Two break changes the nature of the story** (the sniper gets to the witness first, and "investigating an old case" becomes "racing to stay alive"); **the Act Three break is "all is lost"** (solving the case makes things worse, because the villain only wants money).
- **Write two versions of a leave-behind**: for the network, write only "the conflict that drives this episode" — "The network takes it on faith that the character conflicts will be spelled out in the beat sheet, **but a producer won't grant a freelancer the same benefit of the doubt.**"

**seaQuest 2032 "Depths of Deceit" (political sci-fi, where every act out is an information event)**
- The roughest acceptable form of a `Rough Beat Sheet`: **locations can be written as `SOMEPLACE –` / `SOMEWHERE –`, with no scene numbers, about 6 pages in total** (the Martial Law one runs about 15 pages with full slugs). It locks in the order of power moves and information reveals first.

**Diagnosis Murder, two scripts (a one-hour whodunit + a two-hour episode)**
- One-hour episode: TEASE **1** scene + Act One **6** / Act Two **7** / Act Three **6** / Act Four **6**, no tag. **The body does not appear until the end of Act Two** — the first two acts are entirely spent building the relationship web in which "everyone has a reason to kill someone"; the Act One break is not a crisis but **a moral misstep**.
- **The procedural's authorization to intervene is written as one line of dialogue**: "if it doesn't impact on his medical condition, **it's none of our business**" — and in the end it is precisely the medical record that pulls the protagonist in.
- **The two-hour episode "The Last Laugh": eight acts, with scene numbers running continuously across the acts from 1 to 44, and three threads**. The author's definition: "The cliffhanger is essentially **a bigger version of the usual second-act break** in a one-hour episode… only on a larger scale." Two functions: hold the audience through the second half; **create a strong enough break that, if the episode is ever split into two for syndication, it can bring the audience back the following week**.
- The cliffhanger at the end of the first hour (scene 27) does three things at once: it overturns every conclusion of the previous hour (the "false solution" at the end of Act Two is wrong); **the protagonist causes the disaster himself** (he lets the real killer go, and double jeopardy means he can never be prosecuted); the question changes from "who did it" to "**what now**". Also: **a first-draft beat sheet can leave blanks** — scene 42 reads in full "**Brilliant farce to be figured out.**" + the outcome.

**Transferable techniques**
1. **Make the countdown an audible warning** (four beeps, the fifth is the explosion).
2. **Three hard rules**: the end of Act One is not the biggest crisis but "the protagonist pushed into a situation with no way back"; the Act Two break must change the nature of the story; the end of Act Three must turn the protagonist's victory into a new defeat.
3. **Self-check the constraint device**: run each restriction (bomb, deadline, identity, illness, prohibition) through every scene and ask "what new trouble does it create here?"; **if you cannot answer for more than two scenes, it is only an opening gimmick**.
4. **Beat-sheet self-check**: at the end of each act, write one sentence for "what the audience currently thinks the story is about"; **if two consecutive acts give the same answer, the Act Two break was not written**.
5. **The ending restores the engine**: a long-running show may not change the power structure; only the characters' understanding of one another changes; **the break of a two-hour episode / two-parter = an enlarged Act Two break**, and it must be able to stand as an episode ending on its own.

## IX. Sakamoto Yuji's 《花束般的恋爱》 (*We Made a Beautiful Bouquet*): Dialogue-Driven Relationship Drama

> Scene-count tables, the five-year timeline, and examples are in section 9 of [reference-asia.md](reference-asia.md).

> **Note the medium: this is a feature screenplay** (released 2021, directed by Doi Nobuhiro; translated by 蕾克 (Lei Ke), 一頁 folio, December 2022). **No episodes, no acts, no act outs**, so it cannot serve as a template for series structure. Why it is in this library: its author is one of Japan's most important TV dramatists: 《东京爱情故事》 (Tokyo Love Story), 《Mother》, 《最高の離婚》 (Saikō no Rikon / The Best Divorce), 《カルテット》 (Quartet), and 《大豆田とわ子と三人の元夫》 (Tawako to San'nin no Moto Otto / My Dear Exes); and this is his most concentrated, most analyzable sample of "dialogue-driven relationship drama". The "invisible three acts" below are **an inference reverse-engineered from length**.

- **159 scenes, 1,822 script lines (excluding blanks), 35,000 characters, about 124 minutes on screen**: an average of **11.5 lines per scene**; **72 scenes (45%) are ≤5 lines**; only 2 scenes in the whole film exceed 100 lines.
- **Page allocation of the year chapters** (seven handwritten time captions divide the film): 2020 prologue 2% / **2015 42% (73 scenes)** / 2016 6% / 2017 10% / 2018 12% / 2019 14% (only 12 scenes, almost all long scenes) / 2020 ending 2%. **The first year of the romance takes 42%, and the following four years together take another 42% — "remembering in detail" is itself the form of the theme.**
- **Date precision = the freshness of the relationship**: 2015 is precise to the day; 2016 keeps only 4/13 and 6/3; after 2017 only months; 2019 only three instances. **Flashbacks are used only in the first quarter**: 10 scenes are marked "flashback" in the whole film, **and none after scene 42**.
- **All the weight rests on four or five long scenes**: scene 147, the break-up (**162 lines = 9% of the film**), scene 28, the culture-checklist comparison on the night they meet (115 lines), scene 121, the big fight + proposal in anger (71 lines), scene 52, the confession (54 lines), scene 103, the first quarrel (45 lines); **the gaps between long scenes grow wider as the film goes on** (28 → 52 → 103 → 121 → 147).
- **Three audio tracks**: `X (monologue)` = the voice-over diary track, **151 entries** (Kinu (绢) 77 / Mugi (麦) 74); `X's voice` = the real voice off-screen in the same scene (sex under the covers, a phone call showing only one side of the frame, replay in a dream); **sound bridges cross** (scenes 133–137, where each protagonist's "advisor" interleaves by voice, five short scenes weaving a double piece of advice).
- **Location reuse replaces new sets**: only seven or eight locations are used repeatedly (the couple's room 25 times; the family restaurant 3 times = the same place for the confession and the break-up), **and the second and third appearances of the same location are the entire explanation of the value turn**; `*` marks internal sections within a scene (32 instances across 17 scenes; scene 28 uses 7 of them to split one night into 8 subsections).
- **The dialogue budget is highly unusual**: the two protagonists account for **83%** of all dialogue (Mugi 295 / Kinu 266); **the supporting character with the most dialogue in the whole film is the "mirror couple" who appear only in the final scene (36 lines), seven times the "romantic rival" (5 lines)** — almost the entire budget goes to the mirror, not the obstacle.

**Disagreements that must be preserved**: ① **the turning point is not an event but a thoughtful gesture** — "while blow-drying her hair, she says she is thinking of looking for a job" (scene 85), "putting on headphones, he says, go ahead and turn the volume up" (scene 107); ② **there is no antagonist and no third party who ever becomes real** (the rival has only 5 lines, the affair never happens), all the pressure comes from each of them meaning well; ③ **death is not the turning point**; the decision to break up happens at a **wedding**; ④ **no reconciliation is written** (neither quarrel has an apology scene).

**Transferable techniques (including conversion to series)**
1. **The "diary" form = a natural episode division for series**: one episode = one period, a handwritten time caption at the head, 3–10-line micro-scenes stacked within the episode, **each episode keeping only one long scene (10–15 minutes) as its core**.
2. **Copy the length curve 42/6/10/12/14**, and let **date precision drop one level per season (day → month → season → none)**, using precision itself in place of a "time has passed" caption.
3. **The same line recurs across years and reverses meaning**: rock-paper-scissors three times (loneliness → being in sync → "because I am a mature adult"); "even after reading Natsuko Imamura's 《野餐》 (*The Picnic*), my heart won't stir" is said by him to her, and four years later she says it back word for word, and he can only fall silent.
4. **Use recurring objects rather than events for act outs**: pick 3–4 objects per season (headphones, curtains, seats, the game console), change their function each time they appear, and end the episode on a held image of the object.
5. **Cast four pairs of mirror couples** (the pair that breaks first / the pair that marries first / the protagonist's past / the protagonist's replacement), **give the largest budget to "the protagonist's past"**, and in the season finale have that pair re-enact the protagonist's first episode on the spot.

## X. Noh Hee-kyung's 《世间最美丽的离别》 (*The Most Beautiful Goodbye*): A Family Hospice Drama

> **Note the identity of the text**: the text at hand is a **novelization** of the drama of the same title by the Korean TV writer Noh Hee-kyung (노희경; Chinese: 卢熙京 Lú Xījīng; *It's Okay, That's Love*, *Worlds Within*), adapted by 李圣淑 (Li Shengshu), translated by 刘悦 (Liu Yue), CITIC Press, April 2016, 244 pages of text; the copyright page reads "Copyright 2010 by NOH HEE KYONG". **It is not a script**: no scene headings, no S# numbering, no action lines — it is a third-person omniscient novel. **The book never mentions the broadcast network, year, or episode count**, so any claim about how many episodes it corresponds to is inference.
> The chapter-by-chapter structural table for the seven chapters, the chapter-opening epigraph device, the four-episode mapping (inferred), the family conflict web, and examples are in section 10 of [reference-asia.md](reference-asia.md).

- **Length curve 33 / 32 / 30 / 26 / 32 / 44 / 19 pages**: chapter 4 (the announcement of terminal illness) is the shortest, chapter 6 (each family member preparing to say goodbye) the longest at 44 pages, chapter 7 (the actual death) the shortest at 19. → **The closer to death, the more economical the writing; the longest chapter is "preparation", not "death".**
- **The chapter-opening epigraph device**: a full page before each chapter's text carries only dialogue, no narration, quoting a passage of conversation that will appear in full later in that chapter; it is always a refusal or an announcement between two or more people, never a lyrical passage. What it gives is the chapter's **value sentence**, not a plot hook — the audience already knows the ending, and the tension comes from "how will this line be said". Converted to series, it becomes a 30-second opening per episode showing only a passage of dialogue from the episode's later section, no image.
- **Two clocks**: the visible clock is "moving into the new house in Ilsan before the harsh winter arrives" (the in-story time from diagnosis to death is about five to six weeks, from November to the first snow to a clear morning); the hidden clock is the soybean-paste jars (chapter 1, drying soybean paste in the sun → chapter 6, the paste that has been aged for thirty-odd years grows white mold for the first time → the father: "you will never taste that paste again"). The son's **college admission results day is a deadline deliberately left unpaid** — the mother does not live to see the results.
- **The patient's self-knowledge does not depend on being told**: the mother infers the conclusion herself from the blood she coughs up and the mold on the paste jars (a folk omen); no one tells her.

- **Character web (illness = a single detonator that lights six threads at once)**: mother (almost always called only "Mom" in the book, in her fifties, wants the whole family moved into the new house in Ilsan before winter) / father (a 60-year-old doctor, retiring next year, wants to complete that operation) / grandmother (in her eighties, dementia + stroke, wants not to be abandoned) / daughter (27, a department-store display contractor, wants her love with a married man acknowledged) / son (21, on his third attempt at the medical school entrance exam, wants his mother to see the admission result) / mother's younger brother (a gambler and drunkard, wants money and to be acknowledged as not a waste of space) + his wife (a market stallholder, unable to have children).
- **One writing discipline that can be reused directly**: the characters in the book **never use the word "cancer"** — the mother "has never once said the word 'cancer' aloud".
- **Law A: death is not a horror event but "the last chance to attain beauty"** (afterword, item 10: both elderly people died of cancer, "death is not a terrible thing; it is the last chance to attain beauty"). The whole drama's task is not to postpone death but to distribute this chance to every family member.
- **Law B: a real goodbye is flat**. The mother at the end "left without a smile and without a painful struggle, very calmly"; "**our parting was not beautiful, and it was not sad.**"; "I am not grateful that you told me you loved me before you died, because I 'always' knew how much you loved your little daughter Hee-kyung." → **Everywhere that would be written as a climax (saying love at the deathbed, the funeral, the announcement of diagnosis) is pushed down one level.**
- **Law C: replace grief with guilt**. "Before my mother died, I never thought she would become my regret. At that time, I was her regret." — the engine of a family drama is not "whom I lost" but "**whose regret I once was**".
- **Law E: every family member's account of the same event has been rewritten in self-protection**. The author puts the mother's "birth story" in the afterword, and then the elder sister tells the author it is not true; the conclusion is "**we simply cannot fully understand each other's minds**" — which directly explains the writing in which the whole family conceals things from one another.

**Transferable techniques**
1. **A single detonator + six pre-existing threads**: do not invent new conflict for the illness; let the diagnosis ignite each person's existing want at the same time.
2. **Ban the name of the disease**: let no one say the word, and the ladder of information asymmetry (who knows first, who is kept in the dark, who pretends not to know) generates the tension by itself.
3. **Push the climax down one level**: the real explosion goes into a meager meal, a cheap performance, a line like "when will I ever receive that kind of love?"
