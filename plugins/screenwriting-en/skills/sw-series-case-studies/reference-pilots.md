## V. Calvisi's Eight Pilot Minute-by-Minute Beat Sheets

Source: the CASE STUDIES in Dan Calvisi's *Story Maps: TV Drama*. **The limits of the method must be quoted alongside them**: these beat sheets are combined with each series' Basic Story Map to form eight Full Story Maps; "most have been **trimmed to focus on the 'signpost' beats** that form the bones of the narrative"; "All of these beat sheets have been made from the completed episodes, **transcribed as I watched them**, either on DVD or streaming"; "The numbers at the start of each beat represent the **time in minutes** the beat occurred, which correspond to the page numbers in a properly formatted script." (one minute ≈ one page); "if you see overlap in numbers, that means that a scene began within the same minute that the previous scene ended."

**So these eight tables are measurements of the finished episode's structure, not script facts**; the act boundaries in series without act markings are the author's own inserted **inferences**—"I've inserted my own act breaks… in what feel like the natural positions."

The author adds two notes: in an early draft the blood-covered man didn't enter until page **12**, and the revision moved it earlier ("the Inciting Incident was moved up so as to get the story moving as soon as possible"); "This is six minutes in and we've already launched the A, B and D story."

### 5.4 Mr. Robot (64′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C)

| Act | Minutes | Beat |
|---|---|---|
| TEASER | 1 | OPENING: Elliot's V.O. over shadowy men in a boardroom—"the top 1% of the top 1%"; on the subway he is watched by a man in a black suit |
| | 2-7 | **CATALYST**: in a coffee shop, he presents evidence of child pornography hacked from a private server; what he wants is justice, not money; the police take the man away |
| ACT ONE | 7 | The DRUNK on the subway (seen again later): "an exciting time in the world right now" |
| | 8 | At Allsafe, his boss Gideon (**"A" story**); childhood friend Angela (**"C" story**) |
| | 9 | He skips Angela's birthday party; a flashback shows him standing outside a bar, afraid. Social anxiety; he doesn't like being touched |
| | 10 | He can't stand Angela's boyfriend Ollie |
| | 11-15 | His therapist Krista (**"B" story**); he has hacked her and found that her boyfriend Michael Hansen has no online footprint at all; an impassioned monologue about hating society **stays only in his head**, unspoken |
| | 15-17 | He turns down Ollie's lunch invitation; he knows Ollie is cheating |
| | 18-19 | **INCITING INCIDENT**: executives from E-Corp (which he calls Evil Corp) visit; CTO Terry Colby; his deputy Tyrell Wellick. **TURN**: Tyrell seems fascinated by Elliot |
| | 20-21 | A bleak, empty apartment where his only companion is his fish Qwerty; he cries and takes a measured dose of morphine; the dealer Shayla delivers |
| ACT TWO | 22-24 | AFTERMATH / **FIRST TRIAL + FIRST CASUALTY**: he follows Michael and Krista on their date and tricks Michael's phone number out of him; he notices Michael is mean to his little dog Flipper (**Dog setup**) |
| | 25-30 | **MIDPOINT**: a large-scale E-Corp breach; he is flown to the E-Corp server farm; a brilliant piece of code saves the day |
| | 30 | **TURN**: the hacker signature is "fsociety," with the message "LEAVE ME HERE." |
| | 31 | **DECISION**: he leaves the malicious code in the mainframe and reconfigures it so that **only he** can access it |
| ACT THREE | 32 | AFTERMATH: on the corporate jet home, Gideon comes out to him; E-Corp is 80% of the business |
| | 35-40 | **SHADOW SHOWDOWN**: the drunk reveals he is **Mr. Robot**; he talks about his own father (a thief, imprisoned, who lost his son's respect); he takes Elliot to a technical room in Coney Island. Elliot doesn't dare join |
| ACT FOUR | 41-42 | Angela comes over to watch a movie and sees Shayla still in his bed |
| | 42-48 | **ASSUMPTION OF POWER**: on the Ferris wheel, Mr. Robot lays out a grand plan—erase all of E-Corp's records and the debts of ordinary people; the first target is Terry Colby. **Elliot doesn't agree** |
| | 50-54 | INTEGRATION (A & C): Colby has Angela, who made a small mistake, removed from the boardroom without a shred of dignity. **DECLARATION OF WAR**: Elliot hands the FBI a report containing Colby's IP ("ALL IN" + "ALL IS LOST") |
| ACT FIVE | 55 | AFTERMATH: nineteen days pass and nothing happens |
| | 56-58 | **CLIMAX ("B")**: he threatens Michael to break up tonight and confess; he takes Flipper (**Dog payoff**). **End "B"** |
| | 60 | **CLIMAX ("C")**: Angela tells him to stop fighting her battles, "Let me lose."; he hugs her. **End "C"** |
| | 62 | **CLIMAX ("A")**: Terry Colby is arrested; Elliot is thrown into a car by men in black. **End "A"** |

The three stories exit 56-58 (B) → 60 (C) → **62 (A) last**, the reverse of Scandal. Author's note: this is an example that "utilizes the 'Teaser plus five' Story Map in every crucial way"; the mysterious boardroom at the open and at the close are bookends.

### 5.5 True Detective (58′, no Teaser, only two stories, A/B)

| Act | Minutes | Beat |
|---|---|---|
| ACT ONE | 1 | OPENING TITLES SEQUENCE |
| | 2-4 | **FRAMING DEVICE (2012 interrogation)** established (**"A" = the Hart–Cohle partnership**): Hart talks about his former partner Rustin Cohle, known as "Tax Man"; after four months together they caught the 1995 Dora Lange case. Cohle looks like absolute hell and refuses to stop smoking; the day they took the case was January 3, 1995, his daughter's birthday |
| | 5-8 | **INCITING INCIDENT (1995)**: a ritualized murder scene, a naked body bound to a tree, wearing antlers, tattooed (**"B" = the Dora Lange murder**); Cohle takes extremely detailed notes in a large ledger |

| | 18-21 | Hart vouches for Cohle to the Captain; tomorrow he will run the press conference |

| | 28 | **MIDPOINT**: identity confirmed—Dora Kelly Lange, a former prostitute |

| ACT THREE | 35-36 | AFTERMATH: Sheriff Tate; a girl chased through the woods by a man says he was a "green-eared spaghetti monster" (**setup for later episodes: the killer's facial scars**) |

| | 48 | **ASSUMPTION OF POWER ("A") (2012)**: Hart reveals that he hasn't spoken to Cohle since 2002, for personal reasons (**setup for later episodes: the falling-out**) |
| ACT FOUR (the author's deliberately designated bridge act) | 49-50 | **DECLARATION OF WAR (1995)**: Cohle hits a colleague, utterly fearless |

The author's structural confession (verbatim): "I should note that **my original map was only four acts in total**, but I chose to define a short **Act Four as a 'bridge act'** since this section feels like a separate movement, followed by a slightly longer Act Five."

**Long-arc plant list** (this is the executable format of a serial pilot): the first victim Marie Fonteneau (30-34) / the killer's facial scars (35-36) / the Yellow King (38) / the reason the two men fell out (48).

### 5.6 The Walking Dead (66′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C)

| Act | Minutes | Beat |
|---|---|---|
| TEASER | 1-5 | OPENING: at an abandoned gas station Rick sees a little girl walking; she turns around—she's a zombie. **CATALYST**: he is forced to shoot her in the head and is disturbed by it (**THEME: the struggle to stay human after the catastrophe**; **"A" = Rick explores the new world and chooses when to kill**) |
| ACT ONE | 5-9 | Rick and his partner Shane share lunch in the patrol car and talk about women; his marriage is in trouble (**"C" = Shane, Lorrie and Carl's survivor camp**); a radio call for a pursuit |
| | 9-12 | A roadblock on a country road |
| | 12 | **INCITING INCIDENT**: Rick is shot and goes down. FADE OUT. |
| | 13 | He wakes in a hospital bed; the hospital is empty; a torn-apart nurse's body on the floor |
| | 18 | **TURN**: a double door chained shut, with "Don't Open. Dead Inside" sprayed on the outside; twisted fingers reach through the gap. **Rick isn't in Kansas anymore.** |
| ACT TWO | 20-23 | AFTERMATH: piles of bodies, the wreckage of a mass evacuation; the torso of a female zombie crawls toward him |
| | 24 | **FIRST TRIAL**: he goes home; the house is empty. He breaks down sobbing |
| | 26 | **FIRST CASUALTY**: a boy, Dwayne, knocks him out with a shovel (taking him for a zombie); the father, Morgan, spares his life (**"B" = Morgan and his wife**) |
| | 28 | NEW COMPLICATIONS: he is tied up and questioned; untied but warned |
| | 30 | **MIDPOINT**: Morgan lays out the rules of the world—call them Walkers, they are more active after dark, they are drawn to sound and light, and a bite or a scratch turns you into the living dead |
| | 34-36 | Morgan's wife is now a Walker in the street outside the door; Dwayne cries; Morgan admits with shame that he has never had the strength to "put her down" (**New Challenge: if Rick's family became Walkers, could he do it?**) |
| ACT THREE | 37 | Partial Integration (A/B/C): Rick is certain his wife and son are alive; Morgan mentions a large survivor camp and a CDC facility outside town |
| | 39 | **ASSUMPTION OF POWER**: he raids the police station's weapon cage and restocks on guns and ammunition; Rick sets out alone |
| | 43 | Morgan warns him about the danger of running into a herd of Walkers (**setup: Walker mob**); Rick executes a colleague who has become a Walker with a merciful shot to the head |
| | 45-48 | **SHADOW SHOWDOWN**: cross-cut acts of mercy—Morgan has his wife in his sights but cannot pull the trigger (**End "B"**); Rick goes back, says "I'm sorry this happened to you" to the half-bodied female zombie, then executes her (Rick goes "all in") |
| ACT FOUR | 50 | AFTERMATH: a CB radio broadcast is picked up by survivors in the woods; Shane is among them |
| | 53 | INTEGRATION (A & C): Shane kisses the woman, and the camera cuts back to Rick looking at a photo of his family—the woman is Lorrie. **End "C"** |
| | 54 | The car runs out of gas; at a country house, a couple who killed themselves with a shotgun; he finds a horse |
| | 57 | **NEW BEGINNING / DECLARATION OF WAR**: he mounts the horse, every inch the sheriff of the Old West, and rides into the ghost city of Atlanta |
| | 60 | **ALL IS LOST**: he crashes into a huge herd of Walkers (**Payoff: Walker mob**); he falls from the horse, and the horse is eaten for lunch |
| ACT FIVE | 60-66 | **CLIMAX ("A")**: he crawls into a tank; over the radio a voice calls him "the dumbass in the tank." FADE OUT, the final image hundreds of zombies converging on the tank. CLIFFHANGER |

The author offers a three-part defense of "making the audience invest in two characters and then throwing them away" (Morgan and Duane do not appear at all for the rest of the season; Morgan doesn't reappear until Season 3): a vehicle for delivering the rules of the world / **because the protagonist is on screen in every scene, a B story is needed to let the show breathe** / a stand-in for the separated family. The broadcast-act design principle: **each act brings a new movement, a standalone engine, and closes clearly at the act's end, leaving only the hint of "more to come."** The typical marker of a premise pilot: the week-to-week series **has not yet come into being** in this episode.

The dialogue at the end of Act Three, quoted in full (the author notes that it delivers the theme and foreshadows at the same time):

```
MAESTER LUWIN: If this news is true and the Lannisters conspire against the
  throne, who but you can protect the throne?
CATELYN STARK: They murdered the last Hand, now you want Ned to take the job?
MAESTER LUWIN: The king rode for a month to ask Lord Stark's help. He's the
  only one he trusts. You swore the king an oath, my Lord.
CATELYN STARK: He spent half his life fighting Robert's wars. He owes him
  nothing. Your father and brother rode south once, on a king's demand.
MAESTER LUWIN: A different time. A different king.
```

Note: the author's comment—"(This discussion touches on **the theme of the first season, the damage caused by the unquenchable thirst for power**, and it **foreshadows how Ned's sense of honor and tradition will do nothing to save him from treachery**.)"

How it violates the template (the author's own list): ① **multiple protagonists**: of the three co-protagonists (Ned Stark, Jon Snow, Daenerys), two are passive; the justification is that the pilot's job is world-building and that it is selling "the promise of high adventure" rather than paying it off; ② **the Inciting Incident arrives late** (at 18 minutes, in Act Two); ③ **the beats run once per story** (FIRST TRIAL/FIRST CASUALTY once on the A story at 25-28/30-31 and once on the B story at 33-35; SHADOW SHOWDOWN three times: 32, 38-39, 41-43; AOP twice, and neither belongs to a protagonist: the C story at 40, the D story at 46); ④ **act openings are carried by location TITLE cards** (Winterfell / King's Landing / Pentos; cross-cutting begins in Act Three); ⑤ **Act Five has only two beats**, and the A story's "decision" happens off screen, disposed of in a single line; ⑥ extremely long setups: the dragon eggs planted at 53-54, hatched at the end of Season 1; Bran's tower climb planted at 23-24, paid off at 58-60; Jaime and Ned's sparring planted at 46, paid off in Episode 5. The story letters are assigned by screen time, not order of appearance: Dany, who doesn't appear until Act Three, is the B story ("because she has the second most screen time behind Ned Stark").

### 5.8 Breaking Bad (57′, Teaser + 5, A/B/C)—The Gold-Standard Reference for the Template

| Act | Minutes | Beat |
|---|---|---|
| TEASER | 1-3 | OPENING: Walter White, in his underwear and a gas mask, drives an RV with three unconscious people inside (**"A" = Walt's drug business**); he crashes the RV and records a message to camera: "No matter how it may look, I only had you in my heart." |
| ACT ONE | 4-6 | A science achievement award on the wall; a 50th-birthday breakfast with veggie bacon and zero cholesterol (**"B" = Walt's family**); his son Walter Jr., who has cerebral palsy |
| | 7-8 | Chemistry class: chemistry is the "study of change," growth… decay… transformation (**foreshadowing the character arc**); he can't control the disruptive Chad |
| | 9-10 | Cashier at the car wash; the owner tells him to go wipe cars; Chad takes a photo of him wiping Chad's car |

Page-count standards (*The Teleplay: Basic Guidelines*, current around 2013):

- Half-hour multi-cam sitcom: **double-spaced sitcom format, about 50 pages**.
- Half-hour single-cam comedy / dramedy: **single-spaced feature screenplay format, about 30 pages**.
- One-hour drama: **48–63 pages**; pilots are often longer and cut down later. Moonlighting, The West Wing, and E.R. can run **over 85 pages** because of their fast pace and rapid-fire dialogue, yet still fit a one-hour slot.
- "Aaron Sorkin gets to break the rules… because he's won Emmys and Oscars and is a genius."

Two disagreements that must be kept side by side:

- Landau (p0200 box): "Writing a teleplay without an outline is like going on a road trip without a map… it's also **compulsory** (for the studio and network to sign off on the script). **No outline, no paycheck.**"
- Mazzara (interview in the same book): "I don't use outlines… I can't shoot an outline… outlines take up a lot of valuable writing time and they are used to engage TV and studio executives… None of their notes are applicable to script." The substitutes are **a two-to-three-page prose story document for the network plus his own beat sheet, with two or three words per beat** (e.g., "Rick kills Shane."). "All script problems, for me on The Walking Dead, come down to **structure problems**… And a beat sheet for me is a piece of scrap paper with ten words."
- The number of acts is a function of ad slots: The Walking Dead's pilot had no act break (standard screenplay format), and later became teaser + 5 acts because AMC wanted one more ad slot (it had been 4 acts). Mazzara's positive assessment of act breaks: "those act breaks are an important part of the viewing process… It forces you to make choices. **It's about compression.**" Also: "I do find that TV shows that embrace the TV form embrace the act break."—a rebuttal to "It's not TV, it's HBO": "No, it's TV and it's very well done."
- "No act break" does not mean no acts: the same book's main text points out that HBO's *Deadwood* scripts have **clear four-act structure**, while Showtime's *Homeland* scripts have **none**; writing act breaks "can hook the audience at the plot level while fitting the given time slot."

## VII. Miller: Two Scene-by-Scene Escalation Tables and Four Student Pilot Outlines

Source: Chapter 9 of Kam Miller's *The Hero Succeeds* (the five-part scene structure, adapted from ER / Third Watch writer and emergency physician Lance Gentile) plus four actual student pilot outlines in the appendix (Outlines section L9624–11367). **The durations and beat counts of the two scenes come from the author's own counts while watching; the placement of the six landmarks in the outlines is an inference worked backward from the model.**

Five components of scene structure (verbatim): **hero's want / obstacle / escalations / decision / resolution**. Definition of escalation: "An escalation escalates; it increases friction, tension, flirtation—whatever's appropriate for the scene—and pushes the story forward." Want and obstacle are established before the scene begins; "The first escalation begins the scene." On quantity: "You can have as few as three escalations in a scene before the hero makes a decision, but it's better to have more." Prefer action—"If all of your escalations are oral, your audience doesn't have to watch your show. **Your TV show turns into a radio show.**"

### 7.1 Hannibal Pilot, FBI Men's Room Scene (from about 16:06, 1 minute 57 seconds, **14 escalations**)

The opening image establishes the mood first: Will dunks his face in a basin of water, blood seeping into it; he looks up, drips, wipes his face—"His mind is flooded by the blood of the victim, Elise Nichols. It's confusing, obscuring, uncomfortable."

- **Hero's want**: Will wants to understand this killer who confuses him.
- **Obstacle**: Jack wants to know what Will knows **right now**.

| # | escalation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Jack wants to know what Will is doing in the bathroom (implying "you're wasting time") |
| 2 | Will tries to deflect with humor—"I like the smell of urine cake." |
| 3 | Jack swats the humor aside and turns to yell at another agent who has come in to relieve himself, "Use the ladies' room!"—he wants Will to know he's furious |
| 4 | Jack asks Will whether he respects his judgment (Will can't answer "no") |
| 5 | Will says he does respect him |
| 6 | Jack wants Will to know that his gift makes it more likely they'll catch the killer (subtext: use your damn gift) |
| 7 | Will, nearly apologizing, admits he's working on it but is confused |
| 8 | Will says he doesn't know this kind of psychopath, hasn't read about this kind, and isn't even sure the killer is a psychopath (**new information: the killer may not be the textbook type**) |
| 9 | Jack presses Will on what he's holding back (because Will said putting Elise back in bed was "an apology") |
| 10 | Will soothes Jack: the killer wants to memorialize / honor the victim |
| 11 | Jack wants to know what kind of crazy this is |
| 12 | Will gives Jack what he wants: the killer wants to prove to her that he loves her |
| 13 | Jack pushes back: what does that mean? |
| 14 | Will wants Jack to stop pushing him: the killer loves one of the victims, and through her, all of them |
| 15 | Jack lays down the evidence: there is no semen or saliva on the victim; Elise Nichols was still a virgin when she died |

**※ The book's count is 14 escalations, while these notes transcribe 15 items. When citing, use the book's "14" as the data; the itemized list follows the table above, and the discrepancy is flagged rather than silently merged or deleted.**

- **Decision (= the scene's climax)**: Will realizes—he **is defending the killer as if he were the killer**—and feels offended: "That isn't how the killer loves them. The killer kills them quickly and mercifully."
- **Resolution**: Jack believes him—"A sensitive psychopath"; Will knows the killer will take another girl soon, because the killer knows he will eventually be caught.
- **Emotional logic between scenes**: in the next scene Jack goes to Dr. Alana Bloom to have Will psychologically evaluated (because he has just seen Will fall apart and then saw Will break through); in the next scene Will **avoids looking at the body as much as possible** in the evidence room (want: control his own emotions), and the moment he looks, he enters the killer's mind. The author's comment: "The Jack-and-Alana-Blum scene and Will-in-the-crime-lab scene are perfect evolutions from the emotional logic and story logic of the men's restroom scene."

### 7.2 How to Get Away with Murder Teaser, Woods Scene (about 31 seconds into the teaser, 1 minute 40 seconds, **11 escalations**)

- **Hero's want**: Wes wants to retrieve the body and bury it so they can escape the crime.
- **Obstacle**: Connor and Michaela want to leave the body where it is (Laurel sides with Wes).
- Principle for handling multiple people with multiple wants: **there is still only one hero per scene**, and "Your hero may change from scene to scene" (in the men's room scene the hero is Will; in the Jack–Alana scene the hero is Jack).

| # | escalation |
|---|---|
| 1 | Wes meets the other three in the woods carrying the murder weapon |
| 2 | Michaela wants Wes to take the weapon back |
| 3 | Laurel wants to wipe the weapon clean and, after burying the body, **hide it in plain sight** |
| 4 | Michaela wants to leave the body where it is |
| 5 | Connor agrees to retrieve the weapon but not the body |
| 6 | Laurel thinks the body will get them caught and wants to bury it |
| 7 | Michaela wants a plan that doesn't involve "carrying a body across campus on the busiest night of the year" (tonight Middleton University is holding a raucous pep rally for the Warrior football team) |
| 8 | Connor points out that even if they could move it out unseen, **the ground is frozen** |
| 9 | Laurel says they have **all night** to dig |
| 10 | Connor asks Laurel what she's talking about |
| 11 | Laurel admits it: this is murder, and **none of them knows what they're talking about** |

- **Decision**: Wes tells them to shut up—it's 2 to 2, so they'll flip a coin; if anyone has a better idea, say it now. No one speaks.
- **Resolution**: Wes tosses the coin.
- Teaching point: every objection is **legitimate**, and the difficulty and the stakes are both communicated through the argument—"Peter Norwalk, the creator, is conveying **exposition through conflict**, which is one of the best, most compelling ways to do so."
- The criterion (repeated throughout the book): "If there isn't a strong character want, it's not a scene. **A moment without a character want is simply a moment, an image.**" How a one-person scene works: the opposing want comes from the world or the environment (Orange Is the New Black, "The Chickening" opening: Piper's want = to enjoy her Sunday-morning ritual, obstacle = prison).
- The summary sentence on structure: "The spine of a scene's structure is the hero's want. The obstacle opposes or interferes with the hero's want… Each escalation makes it harder and harder for the hero to achieve his want until finally the hero makes a decision."

Writing devices most worth copying:

- **Mirroring the open and close, written into the outline** (*Girl Out of Water*, Scene 25, verbatim): "As in the first shot of the episode, Sierra is facedown in the water. **This time, she's not moving.** Again something plunges into the water beside her. **It's a lifeguard.**" (Corresponding to Scene 1 of the teaser: "something is thrown into the water beside her.")
- **The B story's hero succeeds at the end of Act Two in the same act where the A story's hero fails**—compare Chapter 10: "The juxtaposition of landmark scenes makes them pack more of an emotional punch."
- **Every story's cost is grounded in a concrete object**: the torn-up project book (Felicity looks at the pages the scout gave her for a moment, tears them up, and stuffs the pieces under her pillowcase without a line of dialogue), the open cash register, the partner who pulls funding, the smashed phone.
- *Minotaur*'s mirror as thematic payoff: the belt in the DVD → in Act Four Minotaur unbuckles his own belt and whips Marcus. The protagonist uses the abuser's method, answering directly the question the treatment states: "how are we all corrupted?"

## VIII. Goldberg & Rabkin's Actual Beat Sheets

Source: Appendices D–I of Lee Goldberg & William Rabkin's *Successful Television Writing*: four actual beat sheets plus two leave-behinds, with two of them printing **the leave-behind and the beat sheet for the same episode side by side**. The wording in the header is itself the draft stage: `Story Area` → `Rough Beat Sheet` → `First Draft Beat Sheet` → `Revised Outline`. Two industry facts (current in the late 1990s–early 2000s): **beat sheets have no standard format and follow the showrunner's habits**; "you get paid whether the script is shot or not!"

### 8.4 Diagnosis Murder "The Last Laugh" (Appendix I, p0205–p0219, two hours, eight acts, `Rough Beat Sheet – First Draft`)

Format: **continuous scene numbers 1–44 (numbered straight through the acts)**, locations in caps, one paragraph per scene; **eight acts** (ACT ONE–ACT EIGHT), **no TEASE marker**.

The author's introduction (the only passage in the book that discusses long-episode structure): "You'll notice there is a major cliffhanger between the first and second hour." / **"The cliffhanger is essentially a bigger version of the usual second-act break in a one-hour episode. In many ways, it serves the same function as a second-act break, only on a larger scale."** Its two functions: ① keep the audience watching the second half; ② **create a strong enough break that, if this two-hour episode were ever split into two episodes for syndication, it could carry the audience into the following week.**

**The two-hour, eight-act model (ready to copy)**: Act 1 = set up the three stories + the murder (the murder at the end of Act One, not in the teaser) / Act 2 = **the false solution** (arrest the wrong person on a neat medical or environmental clue, and let the protagonist look smug) / Act 3 = the investigation needed to overturn the false solution + the secondary story worsens / Act 4 = **the major cliffhanger: the protagonist realizes he let the real killer go** / Act 5 = the real killer admits it to his face and the law fails (double jeopardy), and a new victim appears / Act 6 = the protagonist is attacked in turn (the killer uses the same method) / Act 7 = a new chain of evidence assembled from trash, the yearbook, and lab tests / Act 8 = **a trap built with the killer's own tools**, with the arrest completed at the dig site.

**Symmetrical planting of clues**: the rose allergy (used in Act 2) / the tide and the surfers (background noise in Act 1, crucial in Act 4) / the morph software (planted in Act 1, used in Act 8). **Every clue is written off as an irrelevant detail the first time it appears.**
