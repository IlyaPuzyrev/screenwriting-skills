## I. The West Wing: A Six-Episode Act-by-Act Page Table and Act-Out List

Source: Aaron Sorkin, *The West Wing Script Book* (Newmarket, 2002), the shooting scripts of six teleplays credited to Sorkin alone. **Act breaks are documented fact** (the scripts themselves carry `TEASER` / `ACT ONE–FOUR` / `END OF SHOW`, scene numbers, and page numbers), so no inference is required. Page numbers are PDF pages (book page = PDF page − 10).

Quantified patterns read from the six-episode sample:

- **Teasers run 2–8 pages, median 5**; length is inversely proportional to how much backstory the audience needs (the premise pilot, at 8 pages, is the longest; the second half of a two-parter, at 2, the shortest).
- **Total pages run 58–69**; Season 1 is fatter than Season 2 (68 / 69 → 67 / 61 / 58 / 58).
- **Two patterns of act length**: front-loaded only in the Pilot (19 / 18 / 12 / 11); the other five are back-loaded, with Act Four either the longest or second-longest. Acts Two and Three compress most easily (17 People: 10 / 10); **Act Four is never shorter than 11 pages.**
- **Four to seven storylines, with four as the standard**; the bottle episode has five, the season finale seven, the most.
- **Teaser act-outs are 100% `SMASH CUT TO: MAIN TITLES`**; every act's act-out is 100% `FADE OUT.` / `FADE TO BLACK` plus `END OF ACT N`. The format is fixed; all variation is in the content.
- **Every episode has at least one "procedural line"** (the Cuban rafters / the military strike / continuity of government / the FAA terrorist threat / the Haiti embassy): it never carries an act out; it supplies interruption, deadline, and the final moral data. The raft numbers across the Pilot's four acts: 1200 set out / 700 turned back / 350 missing / 137 detained.

### 1.3 Dialogue Examples

**Example 1 (S1E01 Pilot, Act One, p0033–p0037): ensemble relay lines**

```
TOBY  What to do when the Nina, the Pinta, and the Get Me the Hell
      Out Of Here hit Miami.
SAM   We can't send 'em back. They'll go to jail if they're lucky.
TOBY  We'll get whacked in--what--at least--
SAM   --three Congressional districts. Dade County--
TOBY  --those seats are gone.
JOSH  Not to mention the fact that it's wrong.
SAM   Plus that.
```

**Note:** One person starts the sentence and another finishes it, the line cut into half-sentences; the moral judgment comes after the political calculation and is pinned down with two words, "Plus that." — comedy and position achieved at once. The stage direction in the same scene reads: "Throughout this scene, LEO will drift in and out of participation... In other words, nothing going on here is an emergency."

**Example 2 (S1E01 Pilot, Act One, p0039–p0041): an action out of character replaces dialogue**

```
JOSH  And when was the last time you brought me a cup of coffee?
      (DONNA thinks...)
JOSH  It was never. You've never brought me a cup of coffee.
DONNA Well, if you're gonna make a big deal out of--
JOSH  Donna, if I get fired I get fired.
DONNA Do you think he's gonna do it?
JOSH  (beat) No.
```

**Note:** The first cup of coffee = "I'm worried about you"; the audience infers the concern; nobody says it aloud.

**Example 3 (S1E03, Act Two, Situation Room, p0113–p0115): the same question three times**

```
BARTLET      What is the virtue of a proportional response?
FITZWALLACE  I'm sorry?
BARTLET      What is the virtue of a proportional response? Why's it good?
             (An awkward silence... They're not geared up for these kinds
             of questions.)
BARTLET      They hit an airplane, so we hit a transmitter, right?
             ... They hit a barracks, we hit two transmitters.
FITZWALLACE  (pause) That's roughly it, yes sir.
```

**Note:** Each repetition of the question is preceded by more setup, leaving the military unable to answer; the answer is withheld until Act Three, when Fitzwallace supplies it: "It isn't virtuous, Mr. President. It's all there is."

**Example 4 (S1E03, Act Two, p0110–p0111): throwing the other person's attack right back**

```
SAM   ...you don't have the guts or the strength or the courage to say
      "We know what's right from wrong and this is none of your damn business."
C.J.  Really.
SAM   Yes.
C.J.  Strength, guts or courage.
SAM   Yes.
C.J.  You just said three things that all mean the same thing.
```

**Note:** The quarrel closes its loop at the level of rhetoric — it does not rebut the content, only points out the other person's rhetorical flaw.

**Example 5 (S2E02, Act Two, the pool, p0252–p0255): the four steps of the recruitment scene**

```
TOBY  Jed Bartlet's very impressed with you. He likes the work you did
      with that girls' group with the stupid name.
C.J.  Emily's List?
...
C.J.  He's never heard of me, has he.
TOBY  No.
...
C.J.  How much does it pay?
TOBY  How much were you making before?
C.J.  550,000 a year.
TOBY  This pays six-hundred dollars a week.
C.J.  So this is less!
```

**Note:** Flattery → puncture → the pay gap → the only real question ("Is Jed Bartlet a good man?"). The numbers — $550,000 a year against $600 a week — are themselves the character's decision.

**Example 6 (S2E18, Act Two, p0322–p0323): turning a secret into a countable list**

```
TOBY  How is it possible that this was kept a secret. First of all, who else knows?
LEO   You're the 16th person.
TOBY  Who else?
LEO   I'll tell you some of them, I won't tell you all of them.
...
TOBY  Nobody lied? Is that what you've been saying to yourself over and over again
      for a year?
```

**Note:** The number of people in the know — 15 → 16 → 17 — is the audience's progress bar, and the episode title is that number (No. 14 is Hoynes, No. 15 is the anesthesiologist Dr. David Lee). Toby's correction in Act Four: "It's 17 people by the way. You knew. We weren't counting you."

**Example 7 (S2E22, Act Three, p0387–p0388): the grammar of a wordless act out must first be spoken aloud**

```
DELORES  ...if you think we're right and you won't speak up 'cause you can't
         be bothered, then God, Jed, I don't even want to know you.
         (beat) Come on, I'll get you a triple-A.
JED      Miss Mueller only gets half as much to teach music as Mr. Ryan gets
         to coach crew?
DELORES  (pause) You're gonna do it.
JED      Now I didn't say that.
DELORES  Yes you did.
JED      When?
DELORES  Just then. You stuck your hands in your pockets, you looked away and
         smiled. That means you made up your mind.
...
DELORES  And looked away and smiled. We're in.
```

**Note:** The three constituent actions (hands in pockets / looking away / smiling) are spoken aloud one by one by the character, so that the silent act out in Act Four becomes legible.

**Example 8 (S2E22, Act Four button, p0411–p0412): cutting to black halfway through an action**

```
BARTLET   Yes. Sandy.
REPORTER  Mr. President, can you tell us right now if you'll be seeking a
          second term?
And BARTLET doesn't answer... the staff is getting curious...
BARTLET   (pause) I'm sorry, Sandy, there was a bit of noise there, could you
          repeat the question.
The staff is thinking "Please, God, come to me papa..."
SANDY     Can you tell us right now if you'll be seeking a second term?
Again BARTLET is silent...
LEO       (to TOBY) Watch this.
And BARTLET thinks... looks away... sticks his left hand in his pocket...
and just as he smiles we FADE TO BLACK.
```

**Note:** Three layers — the character deliberately creates delay, an insider gives the audience its cue ("Watch this."), and the cut to black comes halfway through the completed action ("just as he smiles"). The answer is never spoken.

## II. The Sopranos: A Five-Episode Structure Table (Segment Divisions Inferred) and the Pilot's Deliberate Blanks

All the "segments" in the table below are inferences judged by the analyst from the therapy-room frame and the turning points; the only documented facts are scene numbers, page counts, and revision numbers.**

Several beats, then he shakes his head. THE END" |

**The formula Chase gives in his foreword (his words)**: "Each script has approximately **thirty-five beats**—**thirteen** or so in the A and B stories, usually **five to six** in the C story, and sometimes a D story with just a few beats." A and B carry equal weight, C is secondary, and D is usually a comic runner. The process: list beats on the whiteboard, story line by story line → an assistant types them up in separate A/B/C columns → **cut the strips apart with scissors and tape them back together, "married," in episode order** (A scene → B scene → A → C...) → when satisfied, retype, and that is the episode outline. Freelance writers had to write strictly to the outline. The season theme was not predetermined: Season 1's "Tony and his incapacitated mother" was left to "organically emerge" in hindsight at the season's end.

### 2.3 What the Pilot Establishes / What It Deliberately Does Not Explain

**Establishes**

- The world: northern New Jersey; the garbage business "was" the bread and butter; RICO and informants; "things are trending downward"; HMO fraud as the new revenue stream (p0049).
- The nuclear family: Carmela (coffee-can cash, the AK-47, Father Phil, "goomar"), Meadow (15, smart, lies, Aspen), AJ (13, his birthday).
- The work family: Christopher (eager for approval, reckless, wants to sell his life to Hollywood), Big Pussy, Paulie, Silvio, Hesh (his father's Jewish adviser), Dick Barone; Artie / Charmaine are the mirror of the legitimate world.
- Therapy: Melfi's rules (a murder mentioned means calling the police, "Technically"), her curiosity and fear, Tony's resistance (Gary Cooper) and dependence ("Talking helps").
- Livia: rejects all kindness, "girls take better care of their mothers than sons," reads concern as a murder plot ("locked away in a nursing home I'll die faster").
- Junior: an uncle kept under his nephew's thumb, "Something may have to be done."
- Thematic images: the ducks (flying away = the family scattering), the cathedral (what the ancestors built by hand vs. today, when "you can't find two guys who can caulk a bathtub"), the "sad clown."

**Deliberately does not explain**

- Tony's official position ("The guy who says how things go is who I think I am" — he never says boss or capo; Jackie Aprile never appears at all); how "waste management" actually works ("On this here also you don't need to know the details").
- Christopher's exact kinship with Tony (called nephew and also "my cousin Anthony"); Christopher's drug habit is mentioned only in one line by Paulie.
- Who blew up Vesuvio — the audience sees Silvio walk away, Artie does not know, and **no one in the show ever explains it**.
- Junior's "New York" structure ("He's got their okay on the hit"); how Tony's father died.
- What Junior and Livia's understanding will lead to — left entirely blank.
- How much Meadow actually knows (her "cynical look") — held over to College.
- The two deleted scenes (Tony kissing Melfi, Meadow's burning-at-the-stake fantasy) show that the pilot was also a process of trial and error: too-early romantic progress and an over-stylized fantasy were both cut, **but the script book preserves both scenes** for comparison.

### 2.4 Examples

**Example 1 (Pilot, p0025–p0026, sc.18): establishing the doctor–patient conspiracy**

```
MELFI: ...if I was, for example, to hear that a... say a... murder?...
       was about to take place—
       ...I'm supposed to go to the authorities. Technically.
TONY (long beat): Oh.
MELFI: I don't know what happened with this Mahaffey fellow. I'm just saying.
TONY: I see. (beat) Nothing. We had coffee.
```

**Note:** The audience has just watched the hit-and-run and the beating; "We had coffee." is the ironic formula of a double life, and every later session is built on this boundary between the sayable and the unsayable.

**Example 2 (Pilot, p0046, sc.44): resistance and admission in a single scene**

```
TONY: Whatever happened to Gary Cooper? The strong silent type. That was an
American. He wasn't in touch with his feelings. He just did what he had to
do... once they got Gary Cooper in touch with his feelings, they wouldn't be
able to shut him up! Dysfunction this! Dysfunction that!
MELFI: Do you feel depressed?
TONY: Since the ducks left, I guess.
```

**Note:** After a long anti-intellectual monologue, the most ordinary question returns him to his true shape; "Since the ducks left" is the first naming of the theme.

**Example 3 (College, p0091–p0092, sc.3): denial and half-admission**

```
TONY: I'm in the waste management business. Everybody immediately assumes
you're mobbed up. It's a stereotype. And it's offensive.
...There is no mafia.
...Look, Mead'—you're right, you're almost a grown woman—some of my money,
yeah, comes from illegal gambling. And whatnot.
MEADOW: Well... at least you didn't keep denying it, Like Mom.
```

**Note:** A three-part movement (the official line → total denial → yielding an inch); the price of the exchange is the daughter's subsequent confession that she tried speed — a reciprocal structure of information exchange that also sets up the betrayal in sc.60.

**Example 4 (College, p0123–p0124, sc.37): the confidant device changes hands — the wife's confession replaces the therapy room**

```
CARMELA: I have forsaken what is right for what is easy. For a life of
comforts I have turned a deaf ear and blind eye, allowing what I know is
evil in my house... I have committed the sin of omission, because I wanted
things for them, a better life, good schools... I'm so ashamed.
...You know all about him Father Phil, you know! AND I'M THE SAME!
```

**Note:** This episode has zero therapy scenes; the confession (seated back to back) takes on the therapy room's entire function — the confidant device inside the engine can change person and change religion.

**Example 5 (College, p0137, sc.59A): the execution**

```
PETERS: I could have killed you last night. Outside the motel. Your daughter
was drunk... I said to myself, 'He's just a dad this time.'
TONY: One thing about us wiseguys—the hustle never ends.
...Jimmy says hello from Hell.
```

**Note:** His fatherhood had saved him once the night before; in this scene he himself voids that identity; after the strangling, "a few wild ducks in a V winging toward somewhere" — the Pilot's image recovered across episodes, without a line.

**Example 6 (The Happy Wanderer, p0173, sc.13): the concession after three refusals**

```
TONY: David, don't say short if you don't mean short. All kidding aside.
      Do you understand what I'm sayin'?
DAVID: Of course. Hey, you don't have to explain business to me.
(sc.14) TONY: Give him five boxes of ziti. (to David) Good luck.
```

**Note:** Three refusals let the protagonist keep his moral cover; when he agrees, one rule transfers the responsibility to the victim.

**Example 7 (The Happy Wanderer, p0215–p0216, sc.37): the B story's wish fulfilled by the A story's catastrophe**

```
MALE VOICE: A program note—'Sun/Moon' to be performed by Eric Scatino and
Meadow Soprano has been canceled... Instead Miss Soprano will be singing
'My Heart Will Go On.'
CARMELA: Oh, that's a lucky break. I wonder what happened.
```

**Note:** The result is announced over the school PA, not by any character; one oblivious "that's a lucky break" completes the irony. The daughter gets the solo because her classmate's father was eaten alive by her father.

**Example 8 (Pine Barrens, p0312–p0314, sc.34–35): the chain of information decay**

```
TONY: He killed sixteen Chechen rebels single-handed!... He was with the
Interior Ministry. Guy's like a Russian green beret.
→ PAULIE: He killed sixteen Czechoslovakians. Guy was an interior decorator.
CHRISTOPHER: His house looked like shit.
```

**Note:** Key intelligence is corrupted by two levels of retelling plus phone noise (static, echo, crossed lines), and the characters act on it — the comedy and the structural device of "everyone holds only half the intelligence" are the same thing.

**Example 9 (Pine Barrens, p0343–p0345, sc.69–71): an ending that does not answer**

```
TONY: You're a captain, what do you wanna do?
PAULIE: Fuck it, let's go home.
TONY: If this cocksucker ever crawls out from under a rock, this is your
      problem, not mine.
...(a long silence in the car) TONY: You got mayonnaise on your chin.
...(sc.71) MELFI: And Irina before her? Depressive personality, unstable,
impossible to please... Does that remind you of any other woman?
```

**Note:** The Russian's fate is unknown and the A story gives no answer; the episode's final emotional landing is handed to a single question in the therapy room.

## III. Downton Abbey Season Two: A 19-Line × 9-Episode Grid and Fellowes's Rules of Craft

Source: Julian Fellowes, *Downton Abbey: The Complete Scripts, Season Two*, the complete shooting scripts with 419 author's footnotes, Ep1–8 plus the Christmas Special (January 1920). **The scripts carry ACT markers for ITV's commercial breaks, and the number of acts varies**: Ep1 six acts, Ep2–5 four acts, Ep6 five acts, Ep7 five acts, Ep8 six acts, the Special seven acts. This season, Ep4 was co-written with Shelagh Stephenson and Ep6 with Tina Pepler.

**Scene counts (documented fact)**: Ep1 Sc 1–71 / Ep2 1–64 / Ep3 1–73 / Ep4 1–60 / Ep5 1–61 / Ep6 1–64A / Ep7 1–56 / Ep8 1–77 / **Special 1–110**.

### 3.1 The Season-Wide Line Grid

Legend: **●** the line advances this episode　**◎** the line turns this episode (its direction changes)　**★** this episode is the line's high point　**✕** the line closes / a character exits　**(blank)** not in this episode.

| # | Line (characters tied to it) | E1 | E2 | E3 | E4 | E5 | E6 | E7 | E8 | CS |

How to read it: **lines running concurrently** — Ep1 ten, Ep5 twelve, Ep6 eleven, Ep8 eleven, the Special nine. **Lines are layered by length**: season-spanning main lines (1, 2, 4) + single-season arcs (6, 11, 13) + guest lines running one to three episodes (16, 17, 19). Every episode has at least one line completely unrelated to that episode's main crisis, to let the audience breathe (the Ethel line against the war in Ep3–5, the Bryant line against the flu in Ep8). **Retention mechanisms**: popular regulars do not leave; "bankruptcy" (Thomas, Ep7), "a change of mind" (Carson, Ep7; Anna, CS), and "a commuted sentence" (Bates, CS) pull back people already pushed to the door; the ones who can actually be killed are outsiders (Courtenay, Lang, William, Lavinia, Vera, Bryant).

### 3.3 Fellowes's Rules of Craft (Distilled from 419 Footnotes)

1. **"A Downton dilemma = both sides are right"** (the note that recurs most often): "a typical Downton argument, where hopefully you're on both sides." / "A Downton dilemma is when you see both sides." Where it applies: Robert vs. Cora on whether Matthew should be sent home; Clarkson on whether to make an exception for the great house; Ethel on whether to give up her child; Daisy on whether to tell a dying man the truth; Strallan's self-cancellation vs. Edith's desire.
2. **No character is entirely unsympathetic**: "There are no entirely unsympathetic characters in Downton. But there is always a sub-text, or at least I try to suggest one." (O'Brien's penance; Bryant "afraid of his own grief"; Carlisle "he always tells the truth with Mary.")
3. **Recap the lines in motion with two or three lines of dialogue at the top of every episode**: "never assume that they will remember every detail of an earlier episode, because they won't." (Example: Ep7 Sc2–3 uses "should we call him Mr or Captain now" and "any news about Bates" to hook one line each.)
4. **The specification for ensemble scenes (footnote 326)**: "the luncheons, the dinners, the drawing-room scenes… they always have to **advance about six or seven stories at the same time, often by no more than a line**. So the audience has to store it all and then put the plots together at the end."
5. **Let the audience know more than the characters**: "you engineer uncomfortable meetings, and then you let the audience fill in the detail." / "the audience always likes knowing things that the people on screen don't know."
6. **Once the audience knows, you need not show them**: Matthew is given no dialogue when he hears the truth; sex and violence are left to the audience's imagination, which "owes more to old Hollywood than modern television."
7. **Upstairs and downstairs emotional stories must carry equal weight**: "giving them both the same attention" (Ep5's Mary/Matthew and Daisy/William run in parallel), with mirrored pairings so that the same thing is said once in each register (Carson/Hughes against Violet/Isobel).
8. **Deliberately create scenes between two people who would never otherwise meet**: "there is no greater distance at Downton than that between the Dowager Countess and Daisy" — which is why a little institutional disorder is permitted (stoking the fire during the day ought to be a footman's job).
9. **Staging techniques**: everyone rising at the end of dinner can regroup the character pairings; the shape of a hunting day (walking to the first drive, soup midway, lunch in the barn) "can bring characters together or push them apart"; passage acting in corridors is used to run several lines in parallel.
10. **The retention premise of workplace drama**: "One of the basic questions of any drama is: why do these men and women stay?" — Downton's answer is "this is their livelihood," so every so often the audience must be reminded that this is a workplace with a budget, wages, and a hierarchy.
11. **"Truth reminders"**: every so often, plant a line that pierces the warmth (the master does not know the cook's name: "Who is Daisy?"), so the ensemble does not turn into a love-in.
12. **The rule for killing characters**: "it wouldn't be right if no one from our running cast died" (footnote 120, discussed repeatedly by the three producers); the dead must be familiar and beloved — "It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time."; and the audience is **deliberately misdirected to suspect Cora** (which incidentally invites speculation about the possible plot of "finding Robert a new wife to produce a son").
13. **The season ending must give a reason to come back**: the last scene is pure pay-off plus "streamers" — "we won't resolve this tonight, but if you keep watching, at some point we will."
14. **Judging the moment to bring lovers together**: "Any writer or producer of a series always dreads allowing lovers finally to come together… So you keep them apart and keep them apart, but… there comes a time when the audience thinks, oh, for Christ's sake, are they going to get together or not?"
15. **The logic drama requires outranks the logic of fact**: "drama requires a logic not always supplied by truth." — but historical and medical facts must be verified (the decree nisi set aside for collusion, spinal shock vs. transection, the brachial plexus, Spanish flu killing the young), and he prefers references that "look baffling but check out completely." Footnote 306: Strallan's injury was designed — serious but requiring no special effects (no missing limb; too expensive and too much trouble), so a medical adviser supplied the brachial plexus; the explanation was then cut because it "undermined the truth of the situation and turned it into a sort of stage convention."
16. **Do not give period-drama characters modern attitudes**: "It always slightly annoys me when characters in period drama, in order to make them sympathetic, are given modern attitudes and modern choices." (Sybil's premarital chastity and her politics do not conflict.)
17. **Put it in without explaining it**: "the style of Downton… refer to people and events… without making a point of explaining them."
18. **Production realities rewrite the script**: rolling up the carpets at Highclere requires the owner's approval; there is a ceiling on dining-room scenes (Carson and the footmen cannot stand there); a ceilingless studio bedroom is kept ready for reconfiguration (walls can be moved, which allows overhead shots); actor availability means cutting scenes or writing exits; **if an actor is good enough, cut the dialogue** (Thomas's motive, Jane's lines) — "A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue."
19. **Editing is a decision by three, and the author often loses and admits he was wrong**: "the consensus went against me… I think I was wrong and they were right." (Vera's corpse as the ending); what is lost is usually explanatory dialogue and small "new colour" bits of business, and the author notes the loss every time.
20. **American broadcast has no concept of a "special"**: the Christmas Special must "play either as the end of a series, or as a stand-alone."
21. **Write the opposing force as an institution, not as malice**: Bryant demands written proof; an illegitimate child has no legal claim on his father; Carlisle buys up the scandal; the decree nisi is set aside — the resistance comes from rules, and the antagonist is simply the person who knows the rules best.
22. **Joke and power in the same line**: the Dowager's lines are often both scene button and an exercise of power ("I hope it is not vulgar in me to suggest that you find some way to overcome your scruples." / "I have a cold.").

**Four kinds of act out (the season uses only these four)**: (a) someone oversteps by making a request (Carlisle bribing Anna); (b) a hidden fact is spoken in one line ("He is standing."); (c) a single Dowager-style button ("Worse luck." / "I have a cold."); (d) a physical fact (a corpse, handcuffs, a breakdown in tears, reading the lesson from the pulpit).

### 3.4 Examples

**Example 1 (Ep5 Sc58): a Dowager-style button**

```
VIOLET: I have a cold.
```

**Note:** At the wedding she quietly dabs her tears, Edith takes her arm, and she gives only these four words. Footnote 157 records the source as a true story about the author's wife's grandmother, Lady Broome, and adds a judgment: crying at a funeral is "definitively middle class." One line is at once joke, character, class statement, and scene button.

**Example 2 (Ep5 Sc41): a downstairs Downton dilemma — both sides are right**

```
DAISY: All the more reason. I can't lie to him at the end. Don't make me be
       false to a dying man.
MRS PATMORE: What matters now is the poor lad knows some peace and some
       happiness before he goes.
DAISY: I can't.
```

**Note:** One side is "you can't lie to a dying man," the other is "let him have peace in his last few days" — the audience nods at both, and only then does the line qualify.

**Example 3 (Ep5 Sc52): the institution supplies the resistance**

```
VIOLET: Finally, I would point out your living is in Lord Grantham's gift,
your house is on Lord Grantham's land and the very flowers in your church
are from Lord Grantham's garden. I hope it is not vulgar in me to suggest
that you find some way to overcome your scruples.
```

**Note:** Three concrete property claims (the living, the house, the flowers) come before the polite threat; the one applying pressure is simply the person who knows the rules best.

**Example 4 (Ep7 Sc22–23): an act out in which a hidden fact is spoken in one line**

```
LAVINIA: Heavens, that was a near thing.
She has brought her eyes up to Matthew. She stops.
LAVINIA (CONT'D): My God.
He is as stunned as she is. He is standing.
...(Sc23) ROBERT: Everyone, come at once! … Come and see this!
As Sybil and Edith also hurry down, he has run into the library, and now they
follow him, to find Matthew in his chair, which is a slight let-down.
```

**Note:** First the viewer's gaze, then the cause; the reveal is completed by a stage direction ("He is standing."), and no character explains a word. The ensemble reaction scene that immediately follows first lets the family assume it is something else (he has sat back down, "a slight let-down") as a buffer.

**Example 5 (Christmas Special Sc69, Sc110): not letting the heroine romanticize her fault / expressing forgiveness through "I haven't forgiven you"**

```
MARY: It was lust, Matthew, or a need for excitement or something in him
that I — Oh, God, what difference does it make? I'm Tess of the
d'Urbervilles to your Angel Clare. I have fallen. I am impure.
MATTHEW: Don't joke. Don't make it little. Not when I'm trying to understand.
...(Sc110)
MATTHEW: I haven't forgiven you because I don't believe you need my
forgiveness. You've lived your life and I've lived mine. And now it's time
we lived them together.
MARY: You must say it properly. I won't answer unless you kneel down and
everything.
```

**Note:** What he forgives must be what actually happened, not the version he would like to hear; and after the most elaborate fulfillment (snow, kneeling), the answer is compressed to the shortest possible.

## IV. Fleabag: Two Seasons, 12 Episodes — Tables and Examples

Source: Phoebe Waller-Bridge, *Fleabag: The Scriptures* (Sceptre, 2019), the shooting scripts for two seasons of 12 episodes (about 25 minutes each) + a short foreword + the closing pieces "Who is Fleabag?" and "Post-script" (about 190 lines, sectioned by collaborator). The digital edition has no page numbers, so locations are given by txt line number, `L`. **A half-hour show has no ACT markers — the "segment" divisions in the table below are inferences based on location and time blocks; the documented facts are the fixed segment devices and the roughly 2,900–3,600 lines per Season 1 episode script.**

The skeleton of every episode (strictly followed in both seasons): `cold open (1–3 minutes, carrying one joke or one mystery) → TITLES: FLEABAG → 3–7 segments → button (≤1 page, often wordless)`. **The only exception is S2E6**, which replaces the title sequence with `TITLE CARD: THE WEDDING`.

### 4.2 The Symmetrical Structure of the Two Seasons (a Skeleton a Six-Episode Season Can Copy Directly)

| Position | Series One | Series Two | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Episode 1 | Lays out all the characters + the debt + the mystery of Boo within one day (a typical episode pilot, **not a premise pilot**) | One engagement dinner (a single location) lays out all the characters + the new character + the season's suspense | Engine demonstration: **the web of relationships is itself the engine**, not events |
| Episode 2 | The ex recycled (Harry) + Hilary enters | Church / coffee (the two alone) + the therapist's office | Converts Episode 1's emotion into a desire one can follow |
| Episode 3 | A family occasion (Claire's birthday) + the season arc shows its hand | A family occasion (an awards ceremony) + the device is seen for the first time | Episode 3 plays the first "hole card" |
| Episode 4 | Bottle episode (a silent retreat: enclosed space, no speaking) | Bottle episode (the confessional: enclosed space, a game of rules) | A two-hander deep dive away from the usual locations; the purest single-episode theme |
| Episode 5 | The high point of false victory (the sisters allied, the money obtained) | The high point of false victory (sex, reconciliation with her sister) | Gives "the best day" and stores energy for the collapse in Episode 6 |
| Episode 6 | Everyone recalled to one space (the Sexhibition) + the truth + the camera chasing her | Everyone recalled to one space (the wedding) + every object settled + she dismisses the camera | Season finale = one enclosed social occasion where every line closes |

The season-finale buttons are not plot: S1E6 is "People make mistakes." + pencil and eraser; S2E6 is the fox, the statue, the wave. **The last image of a half-hour show resolves the theme, not the plot.**

Four quotations from the closing commentary (criteria for writing):

- The moment the device passed the test (Vicky Jones, L43484): "When trying to crack the pilot episode, Vicky filmed me on her phone as I walked around my kitchen. I was making tea and experimenting talking to the camera. After five minutes she pulled the phone down and grinned: 'It works.'"
- Visual Sentences (Harry Bradbeer, L43507): "Harry described the image of Boo standing across the street with cars zooming past her. He said that was all we needed. … She has such a formidable armour of wit and self-awareness, but drop that in at any point – her laughing at a party, her during sex, her walking down the street feeling 'great' – and we'd know that she isn't OK."
- The only criterion for a further season (L43511): "'If she has something else to learn, then we have more story to tell'. Harry was convinced that the greatest love story we had to tell was that between Fleabag and herself."
- The actor's veto and added lines (Sian Clifford, L43537; Andrew Scott, L43562–43564): "There were a few times I tried to change the lines on set, and she would quietly stop me. 'It's good. Stop it.'" / "He even insisted on the character saying 'I love you too' to Fleabag at the end, and thank God he did."

### 4.3 Examples

**Example 1 (S1E4, the silent retreat, around L11900): a supporting character hands over a list of wants; the protagonist answers with a single line**

```
BANK MANAGER  They keep asking me. What do you want from this? What do you
              want? (beat) I'm not telling them what I want ...
Beat. A beautiful track starts playing quietly under his next dialogue.
BANK MANAGER  I want to move back home, I want to hug my wife, I want to
              protect my children ... I want to take clean cups out of the
              dishwasher ... and put them in the cupboard ... At home. And
              the next morning I want to watch my wife drink from them.

FLEABAG       I just want to cry ... all the time.
He looks at her. His smile falls slightly. ... She zips her mouth shut.
He zips her mouth shut. They both look out.
```

**Note:** The supporting character's want runs from large to small (home → cups), while the protagonist answers in a single short sentence; the music cue is written into the script.

**Example 2 (S1E5, the dinner table, from L14900): the same question put to two groups of people**

```
GODMOTHER  Gooooosh! You really are ... So how did you two meet?

DAD        How's work, Claire?
```

**Note:** The to-camera version and the "official" version are set side by side; Dad changing the subject = the action of avoidance. PRIEST   There. There. Where did you just go?
```
FLEABAG  Nowhere.
```

**Note:** The Season 1 narrative device becomes a plot event here; "panic" is written for the camera, and the audience becomes the one caught out.

**Example 5 (S2E4, the confessional, L35811–36456): the series' only long monologue not addressed to camera**

```
FLEABAG  No, I want someone to tell me what to wear every morning … I want
         someone to tell me what to eat, what to like, what to hate, what to
         rage about, what to listen to, what band to like, what to buy tickets
         for, what to joke about, what not to joke about. I want someone to
         tell me what to believe in, who to vote for, who to love and how to …
         tell them.
         (beat)
         I just think I want someone to tell me how to live my life, Father,
         because so far, I think I've been getting it wrong. … Just fucking
         tell me what to do, Father.
Silence. She looks to the grate. She can't make out his expression. A long pause.
PRIEST (O.S.)  Kneel.
FLEABAG  What?
PRIEST (O.S.)  Kneel. … (gently) Just kneel.
```

**Note:** Twelve what/who items in a list-like parallel construction turn "I don't know how to live" into a comic rhythm; the other person answers with a single word, and that word is at once a religious command and an erotic one; the grille replaces the reaction shot. She cannot see his expression, and neither can the audience.

**Example 6 (S2E6, the bus stop button, L43118–43434): a seven-beat anti-climax ending**

```
"You nailed it." → the display says 46 minutes "They always lie. It'll
magically come in a minute." → "It's God, isn't it?" "Yeah." → "That I
fucking love you. I love you." → "No no don't, let's just leave that out
there for a second on its own." → "I love you." → he takes her hand "It'll
pass." → he leaves → "I love you too. (beat) Ok." → the bus display reads
Cancelled → the fox passes → "He went that way." → she takes out the statue
→ she gets up to go, the camera follows two steps → she turns back, shakes
her head almost imperceptibly, asking us not to follow → walks into the
distance, turns and waves → "Goodbye." / THE END
```

**Note:** The love confession is permitted three times (the second time she herself asks to "just leave that out there for a second on its own"), and the answer is only two words; the ending does not rely on dialogue but on a **shake of the head** to complete the device's farewell, and the series' last line is a stage direction written to the camera, "Goodbye."
