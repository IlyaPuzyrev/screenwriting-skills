# Qupai-System Case Studies (曲牌体全本语料库)

> **The output language is the language of the question**; always anchor terms back to their originals, see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md), and invent no new translations. Opera terms (折 zhé — act; 齣 chū — scene; 楔子 xiēzi — wedge; 宫调 gōngdiào — mode; 套数 tàoshù — suite; 曲牌 qǔpái — tune pattern; 前腔 qiánqiāng — repeated tune; 幺篇 yāopiān — repeated tune in Northern drama; 合头 hétóu — chorus tag; 集曲 jíqǔ — composite aria; 集唐 jítáng — Tang-assembled lines; 副末开场 fùmò kāichǎng — the fumo's opening; 题目正名 tímù zhèngmíng — title and name; 帮腔 bāngqiāng — offstage answering chorus; 宾白 bīnbái — spoken dialogue; 科介 kējiè — stage-action cues) have no English equivalents; in any language, keep the original term and add a one-sentence explanation.

**This is a corpus, not a method book.** Method statements are never rewritten; only pointers are given: see the thirteen sections of [sw-chinese-opera-qupai](../sw-chinese-opera-qupai/SKILL.md) (position／zaju branch／chuanqi branch／tune-filling (填词, tiáncí)／modes and suites／staging (排场, páichǎng)／the spoken-to-sung seam／offstage chorus／adaptation and revision／modern new writing／famous works that break the rules／boundaries／diagnosis). **Every case has five fixed items**: ① work and edition (including collation status) ② conclusions at the structural layer ③ conclusions at the aria/qupai layer ④ the convention layer ⑤ transferable techniques; each ends with a one-line "where the famous work breaks the rules" pointing back to qupai Section 11.

**The main text keeps only conclusion sentences and techniques; tables, act-by-act data, source examples, and full quotations all live in the reference files**, and every place something was moved is marked "see reference-xxx.md §N": [zaju](reference-zaju.md), [mudanting](reference-mudanting.md), [taohuashan](reference-taohuashan.md), [changshengdian](reference-changshengdian.md), [bashan](reference-bashan.md). **The main text sets the direction; before you write, pull up the corresponding reference table and fill it in.**

## I. Three Yuan Zaju: Hard Evidence from Twelve Acts

### 1.1 Works and Editions

Three plays by 关汉卿 (Guan Hanqing): 《感天动地窦娥冤》 (The Injustice to Dou E That Touched Heaven and Earth), 《赵盼儿风月救风尘》 (Zhao Pan'er Rescues a Courtesan), and 《杜蕊娘智赏金线池》 (Du Ruiniang at the Golden Thread Pool), **from the Yuanqu xuan edition on Wikisource** (retrieved 2026-09-12; the page names no copy-text, but its textual features agree with the Anthology of Yuan Drama (《元曲选》) system of 臧晋叔 (Zang Jinshu)). **Quotations come from the original text and are not marked as pending verification**; inferences are separately marked 〔按〕 (àn — my note). 《望江亭》 (The Riverside Pavilion) has no complete text, so the third play was changed to 《金线池》, which serves as a **third independent sample** for testing the conclusions drawn from the first two; the 1984 vernacular adaptation 《关汉卿戏剧故事选》 (Selected Stories from Guan Hanqing's Plays) has **no qupai, no act divisions, no role types (行当, hángdang)**, so it cannot be used to judge structure.

### 1.2 Conclusions at the Structural Layer

**The act-by-act structural table for all twelve acts, the sung/spoken ratio table, the combined table for the three plays, the act-by-act comparison with the Northern-suite formats of 王季烈 (Wang Jilie), and the rhyme survey are in [reference-zaju.md](reference-zaju.md) §1, §3, and §4.** The conclusions: **the wedge is optional** (it uses only two tunes in the Xianlü mode, 〔賞花時〕 and 〔端正好〕, may include a 幺篇, runs to at most two tunes, and the singer is not fixed); **in the four acts the singer is always the zhengdan (正旦 — principal female role) and no one else (123/123 tunes)**; **Act One is always in Xianlü and Act Four always in Shuangdiao**; **each act holds one rhyme throughout and the four acts do not repeat a rhyme; rhyme category is a property of the act and may recur across plays**; **nowhere in the three plays do 前腔 or 换头 appear**; **the final act's close may be handed over to the official verdict and the judgment verse**; the title-and-name tag has as many lines as the play has plot threads; **the distribution of the aria-to-speech ratio within an act is an indicator of the major scene (重场戏, zhòngchǎngxì — the scene with the greatest value reversal)** (the execution-ground act is densest at 1:1.1; the wedge is 1:18.4; the unusually even ratio across the four acts, with no lyrical peak, is the structural consequence of a plot-driven scheme play; Act One at 1:0.7 is the only act with more song than speech); **11 of 12 acts match the suite format** (the one that does not matches under a loose standard and fails under a strict one; **both readings stand side by side**), and there is a ready-made example of "**two formats linked into a long suite**."

### 1.3 Conclusions at the Aria/Qupai Layer

Together the three plays contain **126 qupai entries** (41/44/41); **the full table is in [reference-zaju.md](reference-zaju.md) §2.** Five conclusions: **the right to sing is the right to be the protagonist**—all 44 tunes go to Zhao Pan'er, who comes to the rescue, while the person in trouble sings not one; the play's title follows, and **structure, singing rights, and title are one and the same decision** (banqiang opera—板腔体 bǎnqiāngtǐ—has no equivalent); **the person who sings and the person who drives the action can be separated**; **the descending order of the closing 煞 (shà) is a fixed pattern** (one tune, one vow, each carrying one allusion); **a 幺篇 must advance the action one step** (**self-check**: delete the 幺篇—does the plot still move forward?); **the rhyme category agrees with the manner of the close** (the Yu-mu rhyme, closed in the mouth, ends crisply; the Jie-lai rhyme, open, ends expansively).

### 1.4 The Convention Layer

Seven items of the convention layer (程式, chéngshì); **all entries, source examples, and the seven representative aria texts are in [reference-zaju.md](reference-zaju.md) §5 and §6**: entrance poem + self-introduction (**two reverse samples: the corrupt official's introduction reads as a confession; the honest official describes only the state of his jurisdiction**); **the lead singer does not recite an entrance poem—the character's establishment (定场, dìngchǎng) comes from the first aria**; the split-line exit poem's **last line must carry a turn or an irony**; 〔帶云〕 (dàiyún — spoken words inside a song) and 〔背唱〕 (bèichàng — aside sung to the audience); **〔換科〕 (huànkē — swap in full view): the switch is made in front of the audience, and a "wrong question" must follow to cash in the gap**; the official verdict + 〔詞云〕 (cíyún — rhymed spoken coda) (**not exclusive to crime plays**); the empty exit (虚下, xūxià) and the old stage door (古门, gǔmén) (**one line saves a character an entrance and an exit**).

### 1.5 Transferable Techniques

**The full evidence, self-checks, and failure conditions for each item are in [reference-zaju.md](reference-zaju.md) §7.**

1. **First decide who sings; the title comes out of that character's action** (**does not hold** for chuanqi or banqiang opera).
2. **Under single-role singing there are only two ways to write a crowd scene**: turn the other party's words into interjections (插白, chābái), and have the lead singer's next line **pick up the exact words of that speech**.
3. **A tune cut into two or three pieces by stage business or speech is the norm, not a breach** (if not one tune is cut, the aria is not growing out of the action).
4. **A scheme must be laid out in full before it is carried out, in a block of spoken dialogue, in front of the audience; and the trap must be half seen through midway before it is steered back** (**does not hold** for the general layer's view of suspense: Chinese opera does not keep secrets from the audience).
5. **An argument stated three times in the same syntax becomes a skeleton; twice is repetition** (the third occurrence must land where it has actual consequences).
6. **Use a non-singing supporting character to recite the exit poem and mark the section** (otherwise the protagonist turns into a narrator).

**Where a famous work breaks the rules**: Act Four of 《金线池》 has **no coda**—a third exit from the coda, standing alongside "four tunes of the same pattern + a changed opening may omit the coda"; see qupai Section 11.

## II. The Peony Pavilion: The Top-Tier Sample of the Fifty-Five-Scene Southern Chuanqi

### 2.1 Work and Edition

汤显祖 (Tang Xianzu) completed 《牡丹亭还魂记》 (The Peony Pavilion: The Return of the Soul) in the twenty-sixth year of the Wanli reign (1598). Copy-text: the People's Literature Publishing House edition annotated by 徐朔方 (Xu Shuofang) and 杨笑梅 (Yang Xiaomei) (first edition 1963, 24th printing 2014); the epub has no page numbers, so **all references are located by scene title**. **Collation status: 83 items pending verification have been checked, 4 corrected, 9 variant readings retained, 25 impossible to verify** (the list is in [reference-mudanting.md](reference-mudanting.md) §7; all 25 are notes and prefatory matter in the annotated edition, cited as "according to Xu Shuofang's note"). Two hard constraints: **not one 〔拟〕 (nǐ — "inferred") has been removed from a mode attribution** (the Wikisource complete text marks no modes) → **any entry whose mode column carries 〔拟〕 can serve only as an example, not as evidence**; discussion of modes rests only on the three Northern suites (〈冥判〉 "The Infernal Judgment," 〈硬拷〉 "The Harsh Interrogation," 〈圆驾〉 "The Imperial Decree"), and removing 〔拟〕 requires consulting the paper edition of 《纳书楹曲谱》 (Nashuying's Collection of Tune Patterns); **the "thirty-nine kinds of flowers" in 〈冥判〉 is doubtful**; the self-check derived from that number is **suspended and does not enter the skill**.

### 2.2 Conclusions at the Structural Layer

**The scene-by-scene structural table for all 55 scenes is in [reference-mudanting.md](reference-mudanting.md) §1.** The conclusions: **55 scenes and about 420 qupai**; the longest scene has 20 tunes, the shortest 2; **aria density equals the emotional weight of the scene**; **a long scene must be followed by a short one** (**self-check**: plot the qupai count scene by scene; if two consecutive scenes exceed 10 tunes, insert a short scene); **the first scene, 〈标目〉 ("Setting the Title"), does not dramatize action**; it gives only the author's statement, a plot summary, and **a four-line minimalist outline** (not jitang) in which each line takes charge of one thread—**the main line is one person and one event; a subplot hangs from a single sentence**; **the sheng and dan are the play's main brain and must appear in the second and third scenes**, as 王季烈 (Wang Jilie) puts it and as is general in chuanqi; **one scene can hold two suites** (in 〈闹殇〉 "Tumult at the Deathbed," the first suite writes the death, the second writes the reactions of those left behind; **without this** the play would drop the curtain the moment the protagonist dies); **subplots are the first place to cut** (the Shuoyuan (硕园) recension cuts the play to 43 scenes; the Qianlong recension cuts precisely two scenes of foreign kings and bandit rebels); **scene count and prosody are two independent axes of revision**.

### 2.3 Conclusions at the Aria/Qupai Layer

**The scene-by-scene evidence for the six items, the tune-by-tune arrangement of the composite suite in 〈圆驾〉, the list of composite arias, and the sources for long-distance echoes of the same tune are in [reference-mudanting.md](reference-mudanting.md) §2 and §3; the full table of 425 aria entries is in `_notes/tang-mudanting/01–07`.** Six items: **the division of labor between Northern and Southern tunes**—Northern tunes for foreign kings, bandits and rebels, ghosts and judges, courtrooms, and the court; Southern tunes for the boudoir, the family, and scholars; in a Northern suite **one person sings from beginning to end and the others have only speech** (a survival of Yuan zaju's "single-role singing"); **the North-South composite suite writes "one against many" into the prosody itself** (**failure condition**: if several people share the right and wrong equally in the finale, the composite suite will hollow out the protagonist—switch to alternating tunes of the same pattern); **use modes to echo across scenes** (banqiang opera cannot do this), and by extension **choose qupai with the literal sense of their names in mind**; **Tang-assembled exit poems are present in all 55 scenes**—four seven-character lines, with the original poet's name noted under each, performable split among characters or with a choral tag; the final scene's eight lines are the only exception; **when a qupai appears a second time, write it against the reverse of its first appearance** (**failure condition**: within ten scenes, or with the same emotion, it becomes repetition); **composite arias are used for passages where emotion is fragmentary—walking, looking, thinking**.

### 2.4 The Convention Layer

Seven items (the minimum usable set of chuanqi conventions); **scene-by-scene sources and excerpts 1–10 are in [reference-mudanting.md](reference-mudanting.md) §4 and §6**: **the self-introduction has six things** (name／native place／family background／present situation／wish／what one is about to do, closed with "It is precisely…"; **the chou and tie (貼, tiē — secondary female role) may give theirs in verse** and brief the audience); the entrance poem **may borrow an earlier poet's lines or be written by the playwright**; **speech inserted inside a song does not occupy a tune slot** (**self-check**: delete all inserted speech—do the song lines still scan and still connect?); **song for feeling, speech for reasoning** (**failure condition**: when the reasoning itself is an emotional explosion, it may still go into song); **change the scene by means of the Flower Spirit**; related devices are leaving and returning, and a scene with two codas; ghosts enter and exit by ghostly sound, a sleeve across the face, and offstage wind; **use a ready-made convention as it stands, but reverse it once** (the three-call, three-response rule prescribes "each voice higher than the last," but here it is performed "each voice lower," and the ghost-testing convention proves she is a ghost).

### 2.5 Transferable Techniques

**The full evidence, self-checks, and failure conditions for each item are in [reference-mudanting.md](reference-mudanting.md) §8.**

1. **For crowd scenes and transitions, use "repeat the same tune + change the words of the chorus tag" rather than making a new qupai for each group** (the 合头 changes only the last three characters).
2. **For multiple reactions in one scene, use "four 前腔 of one tune + a shared 合头," one emotion per person** (reduce each of the four to a single word; if two words are synonyms, cut one tune).
3. **The number of tunes in a solo scene equals the number of levels in the protagonist's understanding of the same object, not the length of time** (**failure condition**: if another character is present who can take a tune, switch to divided singing).
4. **Cut major information into four slices; between each, insert one probing question and give a piece of stage business to carry the turn** (without this, the opponent becomes a listener).
5. **Write the same space twice—once at the time of the event, once after it** (**failure condition**: with no gap in time or change in character between the two, it is only repetition).
6. **To echo across twenty scenes, use a ready-made line the audience already knows** (does the line still stand outside this play?).
7. **Four moves for adapting an existing story** (the comparison table is in §5): ① **delete the relatives** to isolate the protagonist; ② **change the legal into the illegal** (opening a grave becomes private digging, which violates the Great Ming Code: "whoever opens a coffin and exposes the corpse, principal and accessory alike, shall be beheaded"); ③ **change the manner of meeting**; ④ **keep the original's good lines in the spoken dialogue and rewrite only the arias**. **Self-check**: ask of each move, "did this change create a new opponent or a new legal risk?" Continue with qupai Section 9 and `sw-format-adaptation`.

**Where a famous work breaks the rules**: too many padding characters (衬字, chènzì), to the point of being unpunctuatable; 〈冥誓〉 "The Ghostly Vow" leaves its mode and violates the tune; the staging of all four Dreams lacks judgment—both 吴梅 (Wu Mei) and 王季烈 (Wang Jilie) criticize it; 《紫钗》 (The Purple Hairpin) "is in truth unperformable"; level, rising, and departing rhymes are mixed with entering tones. The handling: "**One should study its writing, not imitate its method.**" See qupai Section 11.

## III. The Peach Blossom Fan: Forty-Four Scenes, Two Intertwined Lines, and the Playwright's Own Rules

### 3.1 Work and Edition

孔尚任 (Kong Shangren) finalized the play in the thirty-eighth year of the Kangxi reign (1699). Copy-text for the main text: an epub (converted with Calibre, no reliable page numbers, so **all references are located by scene title**). The prefatory essays on composition (《小引》 "Short Preface," 《小识》 "Brief Note," 《本末》 "Origin and End," 《凡例》 "General Rules," 《考据》 "Textual Research," 《纲领》 "Outline") **are all missing from the epub and have been supplied from Wikisource**; **the essay 《砌末》 "Props" is still missing**—a known gap, not a case of Kong Shangren not writing it. **Collation status: 322 items pending verification have been checked (643 passages, covering all 44 scenes), 46 corrected, about 56 variant readings retained, 11 impossible to verify** (the list and two doubtful points are in [reference-taohuashan.md](reference-taohuashan.md) §0). **The epub's conversion error rate is an order of magnitude higher than that of The Peony Pavilion, and single-character errors directly affect judgments of rhyme category (韵辙, yùnzhé) and line format**; whenever an aria is used as a rhyme example, use the collated version; the 11 unverifiable items are **all modes**—**mode attributions can serve only as examples, not as evidence**.

### 3.2 Conclusions at the Structural Layer

**The scene-by-scene structural table for all 44 scenes, the master casting table, and the distribution table of the four lines in the upper half are in [reference-taohuashan.md](reference-taohuashan.md) §1, §2, and §8.** The conclusions: **44 scenes = 40 numbered scenes + four unnumbered scenes** (the trial first scene 《先声》 "First Sounds," the intercalary twentieth scene 《闲话》 "Idle Talk," the added twenty-first scene 《孤吟》 "Solitary Chant," and the continued fortieth scene 《馀韵》 "Lingering Resonance"; General Rule 16 states that these are "the beginning, end, and order of the whole forty scenes"); **the upper and lower halves have twenty scenes each and were performed on two nights** (**self-check**: does the last scene of the upper half suspend both lines without resolving them? If the upper half is self-sufficient, the lower half becomes a sequel); **the alternating rhythm of the four lines** follows three arrangements—① **never three scenes of the same line in a row**; ② **whenever the treacherous-faction line gains the upper hand, a scene of the sheng-dan line suffering follows immediately**; ③ the lower-level stitching line is wedged where the two lines are about to connect but do not—**this can be copied directly**; **aria density = weight** (about 300 tunes in the whole play; the densest is 14 tunes in a composite suite; the sparsest is **0 tunes in the intercalary twentieth scene**); **the two "weeping" scenes are symmetrically placed and every item is reversed** (news comes from outside → news is fetched by the self; a crowd weeps → one person weeps; song > speech → song < speech; swearing a pact → a five-level military order)—**when the same play writes "the commander weeps" twice, the second must replace every device of the first**.

### 3.3 Conclusions at the Aria/Qupai Layer

**The scene-by-scene evidence for the nine items, the full text of the four examples of documents set to song, and the sources for recurrences of the same tune are in [reference-taohuashan.md](reference-taohuashan.md) §3.** Nine items: **the two samples of the North-South composite suite divide the work identically—the outsider sings Northern, the home crowd sings Southern, and the ear immediately sorts friend from enemy** (**failure condition**: when the two sides have no opposing positions, the division is wasted); **⚠️ but Northern tunes do not equal "outsider"** (in scene 18 the six Northern tunes all go to the commander who sits still), **what can be remembered as a rule is: Northern tunes = the one person in this scene who must sing from beginning to end**; **reusing the same suite serves as the signal that "this person will sing to the end"**; **three limits on setting official documents to song**—one tune carries one matter (when there are many items, continue with 【前腔】), formulaic language must be cut and only judgment sentences kept, and someone must ask before the song and answer after it; **a recurrence of the same tune creates irony by reversal** (if the first occurrence did not lodge in the audience's memory, it might as well not exist); **praise and blame of loyal and treacherous characters rest on "using the same qupai once for each," not on lines that state a position** (if it also needs another character to point it out, the two tunes have been written flat); **a composite-aria suite**—in one scene each person sings one tune in turn, and the last tune goes to the protagonist; **one qupai to the end writes a great rout** (**the refrain positions stay the same while the content falls all the way down**); **⚠️ the play's only discussion of singing technique—"the character 'si' is the 务头" (wùtóu — the most telling phrase in a tune)—must be quoted together with Wang Jilie's correction**—Kong mistakenly merged "the two 'si' are the 务头" and "the character 'si' must not be voiced aloud" into one matter (qupai Section 4).

### 3.4 The Convention Layer

**The full text of the seven items, the table of the prop's seven transformations, the full ritual text of the sacrifice, the compilation of five ways to close an exit poem, and the details of role-doubling are in [reference-taohuashan.md](reference-taohuashan.md) §2, §4, and §8.** Seven items: **the fumo opening is expanded into a through-line** (the play's greatest structural invention, **and this narrator is also a character in the play**—a narrator who speaks only in the frame has no weight in his commentary); **the push-the-scene method** ("That young gentleman has already entered—pray look, everyone"; no exit poem is used); **"non-plot scenes" get no tunes** (the only witness present／a listener asks on the audience's behalf／emotion is carried by stage business and sound effects. **Failure condition**: if the information can be brought out by a main-line character, do not create a separate scene); **the prop's seven transformations**—**each change of identity must be caused by someone else's action**, and each must sit on a value turn; add one more stroke: **a fake of the same object appears**; **role labels are a casting roster, not character names** (**when you meet "xiaosheng," first check the "playing so-and-so" at the head of the scene**; **the allocation of role types is the implementation of the outline**); **five ways to close an exit poem** (always newly written by the author, **anti-jitang**); **the sacrifice ritual enters the play as it stands**—**the same word "weep" is divided into three layers** (weeping on orders／the commander weeping alone／a bystander unable to hold back); only by setting out the correct ritual do the later two layers have a fall to measure against.

### 3.5 The Sixteen General Rules and the Key Points of the Outline

**The sixteen General Rules are a ready-made checklist of playwriting rules**, standing with 《闲情偶寄》 (Casual Expressions of Idle Feeling) by 李渔 (Li Yu) as one of the two primary sources for qupai-system method. **The original text of the sixteen rules, the full table of the Outline's four colors and four qi (四色四气), and the key points of the Short Preface, Brief Note, Origin and End, and Textual Research are in [reference-taohuashan.md](reference-taohuashan.md) §5–§7.** The five most directly executable:

1. **Quota of tunes (Rule 5)**: the general practice is ten tunes for a long scene and eight for a short one; Kong **sets his own limit of eight for a long scene and six or four for a short one** to prevent actors from cutting at will; the cost is stated alongside—Wu Mei criticizes the quota for making "the writing unable to flow freely."
2. **Song and speech exclude each other (Rule 7)**: **anything already explained in spoken dialogue may not be restated in song, and anything that belongs in speech may not be forced into song**. **Failure condition**: does not hold where repetition is itself rhetoric (垛句 duǒjù — inserted short lines; 帮腔 bāngqiāng; choral lines).
3. **Against the jaded formula (Rule 3)**: "**Whatever situation can be guessed in advance is a jaded formula.**" **Self-check**: before writing the next scene, write one line—"what does the audience expect the next scene to be?"—and if that is exactly what you have written, change it. **Failure condition**: conventional scenes are precisely the ones that should be predictable.
4. **Against jitang (Rule 15)**: entrance and exit poems are "**the beginning-and-end order of a single rhyme**," and must always be newly written. → **The exact opposite of The Peony Pavilion's all-jitang practice; set the two side by side, do not adjudicate.**
5. **Role type ≠ morality (Rule 14)**: "when the proper color is insufficient, borrow from chou and jing." **Self-check**: draw up a "role type—character—good/evil" table; if the columns align perfectly, the casting is lazy.

Three more for reference (Rule 4: no scene may be added or cut; Rule 9: "clarity of meaning comes first"; Rule 12: "the spoken dialogue is so complete that not a word may be added") and the **Outline's four colors and four qi** (they stand **alongside, not in place of,** `sw-character-conflict`; the axis of classification is **function**, not good and evil; "color" governs the separation-and-reunion line, "qi" governs the rise-and-fall line; **frame characters enter the roster and should not be treated as a wedge**) and the Origin and End's "**every time a tune is completed, it must be sung to the beat**" (find a good singer of the day to sing it—**do not read it silently yourself**), and Kong's comment that the happy-ending revision "would inevitably make me look like a boor" (**the happy ending was market pressure; Kong knew it and did not take it**); the full texts are in §5–§7.

### 3.6 Transferable Techniques

**The full evidence, self-checks, and failure conditions for each item are in [reference-taohuashan.md](reference-taohuashan.md) §9.**

1. **A small prop must be able to push, link by link, all the way to the play's theme** (a four-level chain: private object → trace on the body → camp → era. **Failure condition**: if the chain breaks at the second level, it is only a token, not the eye of the theme).
2. **Write the same character action three times without repeating it by distinguishing it with three things**: the rank of the opponent, the magnitude of the cost, and the inversion of the aria-to-speech ratio (if the ratio is the same three times, you have written it twice over).
3. **A group besieging one person: split one 带过曲 (dàiguòqǔ — a short linked tune-sequence) into lines and assign them in order of attack, while the surrounded person uses only spoken dialogue** (if the order can be shuffled at will, the assignment is not done). **The division between song and speech is the division of positions.**
4. **Give the hardest words to spoken dialogue, not to song** (if turning them into song requires adding filler words for level and oblique tones, they were meant to be speech).
5. **A character's professional skill must be a plot mechanism**, and **each entrance should change to a heavier musical form** (if the plot still runs after you replace the craft, the craft was given away for nothing).
6. **A play-within-a-play must do work, not serve as atmosphere** (**let the person about to be painted as a villain himself state the power of the painted face**; "can he sing such-and-such a play?" decides where a character goes).
7. **An anti-happy-ending must push twice**, in this order: **first finish a love song, then look at the fan together once more, and only then tear it** (the second overturn must bring something new).
8. **Write the ending as landscape: walk the whole road first, then speak one line** (one tune, one view, ordered by walking distance; at each place write only "what is missing"; **every location must have been staged earlier**. **Failure condition**: a short play has no old views to recover).

**Where a famous work breaks the rules**: level and oblique tones are often out of tune, and padding characters are not well fitted; the syntax of 〈传歌〉 "Teaching the Song" is wrong (Wu Mei's verdict: "**fine words but no fine tune**"); 〈哭坛〉 "Weeping at the Altar" substitutes the Southern 〔朝天子〕 for a Northern tune. See qupai Section 11. **Kong Shangren's General Rules and the verdicts of Wu Mei and Wang Jilie are two coordinate systems; write both.**

## IV. The Palace of Eternal Life: The Complete Fifty-Scene Staging Table (after Wang Jilie; **no complete text**)

### 4.1 Work and Edition

《长生殿》 (The Palace of Eternal Life) by 洪昇 (Hong Sheng). **There are no complete-text notes for The Palace of Eternal Life in this batch of material**—all data come from the fifty-scene staging table in the 《螾庐曲谈》 (Yinlu's Talks on Song) of 王季烈 (Wang Jilie), volume 2, chapter 4 (printed pp. 0170–0173, read directly from page images, not marked pending verification; printed page number = scan page − 22). **This section can therefore discuss only staging and role allocation, not qupai, lyrics, or the aria-to-speech ratio**; no conclusion touching a specific aria is written. The table is transcribed as it stands, see [reference-changshengdian.md](reference-changshengdian.md) §1.

### 4.2 Conclusions at the Structural Layer

**The full fifty-scene table (scene／title／mode／role／nature of the plot), the twenty-four-category classifier for plot nature, the six-way classification of Southern suites by emotional color, the law matching suites to roles, and the entry on short transition scenes are in [reference-changshengdian.md](reference-changshengdian.md) §1–§3.** Two things read off the table:

- **The three non-repetitions** (p0169): "Apart from the first scene, 〈传概〉 'Setting Out the Outline,' which is the customary opening piece, there are forty-nine scenes in all. **Not only are the qupai not repeated throughout, but the mode of one scene does not repeat the mode of the scene before it; the principal role of one scene never repeats the principal role of the scene before it.**" This is the **only staging table in the whole batch whose forty-nine scenes can be checked one by one**.
- **The law of alternating staging** (p0169, common to both systems): "If the first scene has the sheng sing and the second has the dan sing, then the third must use **a broad-voice role or a lively ensemble play**; if two or three scenes of slow tunes and long suites have passed, a **short transition scene or a comic scene performed by chou or jing** must be inserted." **Self-check**: list the scenes in a column by "lead singer／nature of the music," and see whether three consecutive scenes share the same role and the same nature. **Failure condition**: a play of ten scenes or fewer need only keep "no two consecutive scenes with the same lead singer."

### 4.3 Three Techniques Read Off This Table (Wang Jilie's own account, p0174)

1. **Changing mode and rhyme within one scene is legitimate, but it must be caused by a change of staging or a turn in the plot** (〈定情〉 "Plighting the Troth": the banquet／the whispered exchange; 〈密誓〉 "The Secret Vow": the meeting in heaven／the pact in the palace; 〈埋玉〉 "Burying the Jade": forcing the consort／setting out). "**It is precisely by changing mode and rhyme within a single scene that it fits the plot all the more closely**"—the exact opposite of the "gratuitous modulation" in 《琵琶记·吃糠》 (The Lute · "Eating Bran"). **Self-check**: what happened on stage at the moment of the mode change?
2. **The Northern rule that one scene is limited to one singer can be adapted**: in 〈觅魂〉 "Seeking the Soul," the first half of the suite is sung by the jing and the second by the mo—"**no single performer's strength could possibly carry such a long suite to the end**; the spirit who appears in a different role is still the same person who communes with the dead; though this adapts the ancients' fixed method, it does not go against the ancients."
3. **Overall judgment**: "See to it that **parting and reunion, joy and sorrow are interwoven and cross-hatched, so that the performers are not unevenly worked or rested**." → **"No performer unevenly worked or rested" is the staging table's second function**: it is also the cast's stamina allocation chart.

### 4.4 The Convention Layer (What This Table Can Support)

The full text of the four items is in [reference-changshengdian.md](reference-changshengdian.md) §4 and §5. Key points: **the first scene, 〈传概〉, is the "customary opening piece"** and is not counted in the three non-repetitions; it occupies the same functional position as 〈标目〉 in The Peony Pavilion and the trial first scene in The Peach Blossom Fan; **the final scene, 〈重圆〉 "The Round Reunion," returns to the format of the second scene**, so beginning and end share one shape; **the 赚 (zhuàn — "earn," a linking tune also called 不是路) is used as a joint at changes of staging and shifts of mode, and "in a whole chuanqi the 赚 is best used in one scene; within one scene it is best not to use more than two"** (counterexample: The Purple Hairpin).

### 4.5 Transferable Techniques

1. **Before writing a long play, make a five-column table: scene／mode／role／nature of the music／event**, and look only at whether the "role" and "nature" columns collide in consecutive rows. **Does not hold** for short plays and excerpt scenes; for a text whose modes cannot be verified, only the last two columns can be checked.
2. **The longest emotional suite must fall in the scene with the greatest value turn, and is best sung by sheng and dan** (if the scene it occupies is not what you consider the major scene, the aria has been placed in the wrong spot—the same principle as Yuan zaju's "the act with the smallest aria-to-speech ratio must be the major scene," and isomorphic with `sw-scene-craft`).
3. **Between two big scenes there must be a transition, and the transition may use only short, fast tunes** (misusing a slow tune with 赠板 (zèngbǎn — added beats) means "the plot does not connect and the threads do not join").
4. **The staging table is also a stamina chart**: see who has to sing a big suite in three consecutive scenes. **Failure condition**: a closet drama may skip this—but by skipping it, the script falls back into the category of "beautiful writing that never took hold in the singing theater" (p0166).

**Where a famous work breaks the rules**: 〈打围〉 "The Encircling Hunt" is discussed in qupai Section 11; also, 《幽闺记·请盟》 (The Secluded Boudoir · "Seeking an Alliance") mistakes the Yuediao tune 〔看花回〕 for the Xianlü tune 〔点绛唇〕, and **The Handan Dream and The Palace of Eternal Life repeat the error in a chain**—"predecessors marked it this way" is not evidence.

## V. The Bashan Scholar: What Qupai-System Opera Looked Like in 1983

### 5.1 Work and Edition

《巴山秀才》 (The Bashan Scholar) by 魏明伦 (Wei Minglun) and 南国 (Nan Guo) is a Sichuan opera **high-pitched-style (高腔, gāoqiāng)** play (qupai system, with offstage answering chorus), labeled a "newly written historical play." **Ninth draft, Zigong, 30 July 1983**; first edition, Sichuan Literature and Art Publishing House, January 1985. **The OCR note and the list of historical changes are in [reference-bashan.md](reference-bashan.md) §0.** **Its unique value**: it is the **only modern newly written full-length qupai-system play in this batch**—the first four cases show the qupai system as the Ming and Qing practiced it and as theorists saw it; this one shows **what it was like in 1983**: the qupai names remain, the bangqiang remains, but the suites are gone and the metre is loose. **The scale of historical change can be quantified**: of the prototype "Dongxiang Massacre," **only two real names and two plot incidents are retained**; all other characters and the "poisoned wine" ending are invented.

### 5.2 Conclusions at the Structural Layer

**The scene-by-scene structural table for the eight scenes, the character list, and the inventory of plants and payoffs are in [reference-bashan.md](reference-bashan.md) §1 and §6.** The conclusions: **eight scenes, all with two-character verb-object or modifier-noun titles** (求赈 "Begging Relief"／谎报 "The False Report"／屠城 "The Massacre"／迂告 "The Roundabout Complaint"／智告 "The Clever Complaint"／换札 "Swapping the Letter"／搜店 "Searching the Shop"／揭底 "Exposing the Bottom"), **read in sequence they form one complete plot line**; **the four four-character lines of the prelude and the two choral lines of the final scene form a frame**; **the main line = four levels of complaint = four times being duped = four elevations of understanding**, and **each level of failure must teach the protagonist one more thing about the world, or it is only a repeated defeat**; **all propulsion comes from "other people bearing the consequences for him"**; **the protagonist does not initiate, but after each push his understanding jumps forward one notch** (a "very low starting point" construction); **the second scene, from which the protagonist is absent, is the hinge of the whole play's causality**; scenes four and five are the two major scenes; the first two-thirds of scene eight is entirely spoken, **saving the play's last qupai until just before the character's last breath**.

### 5.3 Conclusions at the Aria/Qupai Layer: Four Questions and Four Answers

The play has **43 qupai/aria entries**; **the full table is in [reference-bashan.md](reference-bashan.md) §2; the tune-to-emotion correspondence table and the page-by-page evidence for the four questions and answers are in §4.** There are 11 high-pitched-style qupai, plus 4 instances of 【昆曲】 (Kunqu), 1 of Peking opera's 【西皮二六】 (xīpí èrliù), and 1 of 【韵白】 (yùnbái — rhymed speech), and **9 aria passages with no tune name marked**. **Question one: no suites—the linking of qupai into suites is abandoned completely** (no modal relationship between tunes; not one mode is marked anywhere in the play; the same tune recurs across scenes and across characters; the chuanqi rules "one mode per scene, one suite to the end, sheng and dan do not share a tune" all cease to apply; the only surviving trace is **continuous singing (接唱, jiēchàng)**, which occupies the position of the 合头 and is used only for confrontational alternation). **Question two: choose tunes like scattered gold and broken jade, on the basis of emotion and the size of the passage; the test is that a choice you can explain answers "how long is this emotion, what key is it in," while a choice you cannot explain answers "what mode was the previous tune."** **Question three: half the rules are kept—one rhyme to the end of a passage is strict, but character count and tonal pattern are completely free** (four-, five-, seven-, and ten-character lines mixed; **heavy use of three-character openings** = a banqiang habit, not qupai metre; line counts far beyond the original tune's capacity; colloquial Sichuan speech and xiehouyu (歇后语 — two-part allegorical sayings) go straight into the song). **Question four: no new tunes are invented, but the tune-name column is expanded into a "singing-method instruction column"**: the parentheses contain four kinds of non-qupai information—**vocal genre** ("Kunqu," with the choice of tune left to the composer), **metrical patterns from other opera genres (板式, bǎnshì)**, **manner of recitation**, and **category of vocal style**. **Addendum: a whole Tang poem is used unchanged as an aria**—**the qupai system already had a jitang tradition; this play moves it from the exit poem into the aria.**

### 5.4 The Convention Layer: Nine Bangqiang Passages

**Bangqiang is set in parallel with the characters' songs; the line begins with the two characters "帮腔," occupying the same position as a character name—it is treated in the script as a role.** **The full text of the nine passages, four examples of bangqiang, and the scene-by-scene sources for the remaining conventions are in [reference-bashan.md](reference-bashan.md) §3, §5, and §7.** **Four functions, one chosen per passage**: narration／commentary／speaking on a character's behalf／emotional amplification; there are also two purely structural uses, **starting the song** and **taking up the next line**. **Three formal rules**: ① **extremely short** (six of the nine are only 3–7 characters), and **bangqiang never carries a full passage of lyric emotion**; ② **always placed where "the character cannot go on singing"**; ③ **the bangqiang can execute the change of tune**, with the character following—**this is the structural power peculiar to bangqiang in the qupai system**. **The boundary**: **accompaniment/chorus is offstage**, handling announcements and the theme. **The solitary exception: a character in the play hears the bangqiang and adopts it** ("Hey, that bangqiang sang it well!"—and immediately acts on it) → the bangqiang turns from "the voice of the crowd" into "the voice of fate"; **at most once in a play**, and it must fall at the point where the character has nowhere left to turn; use it a second time and it degrades into an ordinary supporting role. **The remaining conventions** (examples in §5): the entrance prelude replaces the self-introduction; **the scene-setting poem uses four four-character lines** (reserving irony for the final scene's reversal); **three-person relayed aside singing** (singing the conspiracy to the audience in front of the complainant); double asides on the same stage; **face-changing written at the point of a personality reversal, not as a display of technique**; an improvised prose sacrificial address replaces the exit poem; **hallucination scenes marked by anachronistic costumes**; gong-and-drum cues.

### 5.5 Transferable Techniques

**The full evidence, self-checks, and failure conditions for each item are in [reference-bashan.md](reference-bashan.md) §8.**

1. **Same-tune ring composition**: the same tune and the same bangqiang line appear once before and once after in the play, with the content in symmetrical opposition (counting one to ten forward in the dream of rank and fame／counting ten to one backward in the history of complaints). **Failure condition**: too short an interval makes it repetition; the two occurrences must sit on either side of the character's transformation, and the second must be able to negate the first. **Banqiang opera has no marker for "the same qupai" and cannot achieve anything this clean.**
2. **Write only very short bangqiang lines, and only where the character cannot go on singing; attach one function label to each passage and delete any that will not take one** (if it runs over seven characters, ask "why isn't the character singing this line?").
3. **Use bangqiang for cold commentary, turning grief into comedy within a single line** (**failure condition**: when the style is not a blend of grief and comedy, it looks like mocking the protagonist).
4. **Use vocal style to label characters** (the local official sings high-pitched style, the visiting prince sings Peking opera, the powerful celebrate with Kunqu = refined = false). **Failure condition**: does not hold when the opera genre has only one vocal style.
5. **A whole ready-made poem can serve as an aria without changing a word, but it must fit the onstage situation and carry a second meaning** (ask "what would the audience know less of if it were removed?").
6. **Use the same character flaw twice; the second time it saves a life** (taunting him that he "wouldn't dare fire the cannon" → he fires it, and the offstage flag-gate cannon is the play's most ingenious transition). Cross-reference `sw-character-conflict`.
7. **A prop from a dream pays off exactly as dreamed, and the payoff kills** (the dream of rank and fame's "three cups of imperial wine" → in the final scene three cups are truly bestowed, and the third is poison). Kindred device: a mispronounced character is planted and, four scenes later, used as a weapon by its victim.

**Its place in the method layer**: this play belongs in qupai Section 10, "Modern Newly Written Qupai-System Plays" (not Section 11, "Famous Works That Break the Rules")—together with the Kunqu practice of 翁偶虹 (Weng Ouhong)—no suites, "scattered gold and broken jade" selection of tunes in alternation, on the grounds that "the suite is too constraining"—it is **the same conclusion reached independently by two opera genres, two authors, and two eras**; 《班昭》 (Ban Zhao) by 罗怀臻 (Luo Huaizhen) takes the opposite road (written in vernacular Chinese yet "not a single character breaks the rules," at the cost of "two days to fill one qupai"). **The three stand side by side, in direct conflict with Wu Mei and Wang Jilie's "filling words must follow the manual"; keep both the ancient and the modern.** **The terminological conflict must be flagged**: "**powder applied to the back of the painting**" in this play's composition notes means using the old era to set off the new, while in Weng Ouhong's six methods it means using a contrasting character inside one play to set off the protagonist. **Four items belonging to the general layer** (original text in §9): **the method of eliminating protagonists one by one** to settle on the protagonist, and **the pivot-action method** → `sw-character-conflict`／`sw-story-structure`; **style is decided by the mismatch of "character nature × event nature,"** and **for the theme sentence, look first for a folk saying** → `sw-premise-theme`.

## VII. Comparison with the Banqiang Corpus

**The Bashan Scholar and 《潘金莲》 (Pan Jinlian) form a control pair by the same author across vocal systems**; the two point at each other—do not read only one half:

| | The Bashan Scholar (Section V) | 《潘金莲》 (Pan Jinlian) (`sw-chinese-opera-banqiang-cases`) |
|---|---|---|
| Vocal system | Sichuan opera's **high-pitched style** = qupai system | Sichuan opera's **tanxi／huqin** = banqiang system |
| What the script marks | **Tune names** (including vocal genre, metrical patterns from other opera genres, manner of recitation); no modes | **Metrical patterns**; no tune names |
| Bangqiang | Present, nine passages, written as a role | Traces of bangqiang as an independent role—**a high-pitched-style trace inside the banqiang system** |
| Era and route | 1983 newly written historical play, a comedy with tears | The absurd and the cross-temporal (the commentator's seat) |

**One author, one opera genre, two ways of writing**—the cleanest single piece of evidence for "the axis of division is the vocal system, not the opera genre" (banqiang Section 0.1, the opening of qupai); **Sichuan opera is an opera genre that crosses systems within a single script, and marking the vocal system is itself part of the playwright's job**. Two further cross-library pointers: **contemporary work uses an onstage chorus／offstage chorus + surtitles to replace the self-introduction, scene-setting poem, and exit poem** (banqiang Section 6, "the chorus layer"), whose counterpart here is the Tang-assembled exit poem and the fumo opening; **aria density = the major scene**—the three independent demonstrations in this library and banqiang opera's "save the arias for the major scene" are the same thing.
