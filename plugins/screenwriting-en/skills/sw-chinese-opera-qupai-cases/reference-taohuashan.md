# reference-taohuashan.md — Kong Shangren (孔尚任): The Peach Blossom Fan (《桃花扇》), Forty-Four Scenes

> **What this file contains**: the complete scene-by-scene structural table for all 44 scenes of The Peach Blossom Fan (《桃花扇》); the master table of role-type assignments; the full conclusions at the aria/qupai level; the full conventions section and the table of the prop's seven transformations; the original text of the sixteen General Rules (《凡例》); the complete Outline (《纲领》) table of four colors and four qi; the key points of the Short Preface (《小引》), Brief Note (《小识》), and Origin and End (《本末》); the compilation of exit poems and the distribution of the four lines in the upper volume; and the full text of the transferable techniques.

## §0 Collation Status and Two Uncertain Readings (full text of main-text section 3.1)

**Collation status: 322 points flagged for checking have been checked (expanded into 643 comparable fragments, covering all 44 scenes), 46 corrections made, about 56 variant readings retained, and 11 points unchecked.**

- **The epub conversion error rate is an order of magnitude higher than for The Peony Pavilion (《牡丹亭》)** (46/322 vs. 4/83): 29 are single characters similar in shape or sound (催/摧, 丝/药, 衩/钗, 桃/挑...), and 17 are lost pauses or line breaks inside aria text. **Single-character errors directly affect judgments about rhyme categories (韵辙, yùnzhé) and line patterns**, so whenever lyrics from this play are used as rhyme examples, the collated version is used.
- The 11 unchecked points are **all modes (宫调, gōngdiào)**: both electronic editions give only the qupai name, not the mode. The conclusion is the same as for The Peony Pavilion—**mode labels can serve only as examples, never as evidence**.
- **Two uncertain readings; avoid quoting them**: in Scene 9, "tiger head, swallow **jaw**" (虎头燕**颔**; both electronic editions read 颌, probably a cognate error); in the Scene 12 exit poem, "**close** the heavy gate" (**掩**重门; both electronic editions read 俺重门, which makes no sense).
- Incidental finding: a global search of all 643 fragments **found not one case of a quotation assigned to the wrong scene**.
- The item-by-item list of changes and the table of variant readings are in the final section, "Collation Record," of `_notes/kong-taohuashan/06-chu37-40-xu40-zhiling.md`.

**Two rules that can be read off the table**: (1) **sheng and dan do not double**; everyone else does—the emotional main line must be carried by two fixed performers; (2) **fujing = Ruan Dacheng, chou = Liu Jingting, jing = Su Kunsheng**: these three codes each have one continuous character, exactly matching the two lines in the trial first scene's summary—"the treacherous Ma and Ruan" and "the clever Liu and Su." **The role-type assignment is itself the implementation of the outline.**

### 1.3 Qupai and Modes: How Far These Notes Can Judge

- The script marks only **qupai names** (with 「」 around the tune name), not modes; the mode must be inferred from suite (套数, tàoshù) conventions. What can be determined reliably: the first qupai of a Northern suite determines the mode—【点绛唇】 = Northern Xianlü, 【粉蝶儿】 = Northern Zhonglü, 【新水令】 = Northern Shuangdiao, 【斗鹌鹑】 = Northern Yuediao, 【醉花阴】 = Northern Huangzhong. Southern tunes are not marked piece by piece; these notes give a mode only when it can be established and leave it blank otherwise—**no guessing**.
- **「前腔」 (qiánqiāng) = the same qupai sung again** (in Northern music called "yāopiān" [幺篇]); **「前腔换头」** = the opening line's pattern is varied; **「合」／「合前」** = the last few lines sung by all / repeat the previous piece's chorus; **a qupai name containing "犯" or "带"** = a medley aria (several qupai spliced into one) or a linked pair (two Northern qupai run together). These four marks appear extremely densely in The Peach Blossom Fan and are the entry threshold for reading a qupai-system script.
- **One suite per scene, one mode per suite** is the norm; The Peach Blossom Fan mostly follows it, but preludes (such as the cipai [词牌, cípái — lyric tune patterns] 【蝶恋花】, 【鹧鸪天】, 【西江月】) and codas are not counted within the suite.

## §3 Aria / Qupai Layer (full text of main-text section 3.3)

- **Two North–South composite suite (南北合套, nánběi hétào) samples, same division of labor**: Scene 11, "At the Camp Gate" (the ten-piece Northern Shuangdiao Xinshuiling suite all goes to chou Liu Jingting; the Southern tunes go to the home-side ensemble), and Scene 40, "Entering the Way" (the Northern Huangzhong suite all goes to wai Zhang Wei; the Southern tunes go to the ensemble). **The outsider sings Northern, the home ensemble sings Southern; the ear instantly separates friend from enemy. Failure condition**: when the outsider and the home side have no opposing standpoints (he has only come to deliver a message), the composite suite's division of labor is wasted.
- **⚠️ Guard against over-generalizing: Northern tunes do not equal "outsider."** In Scene 11 the Northern tunes go to Liu Jingting, who has barged in; **in Scene 18, "Fighting for Precedence," all six pieces of the Northern Xianlü suite go to the commander Shi Kefa, who sits still** (the four garrisons do not sing a line). Put the two side by side; you cannot take only one. **What can be remembered as a rule: Northern tunes = the person in this scene who must sing it through alone**, whether he has barged in or sits still is decided by the shape of the play. See qupai, Section 3.
- **The same suite is reused across the play as a marker that "this person will sing it through"**: the Northern Shuangdiao Xinshuiling suite appears three times—Scene 11 gives it to chou (Liu Jingting storming the camp gate), Scene 23 to dan (Xiangjun sending the fan from her sickbed), and the continued Scene 40 to jing (Su Kunsheng's 「哀江南」, mourning the fallen state). **The same suite given to different role types and different occasions, once each**; the audience hears the opening and knows this passage belongs to one singer to the end.
- **Three limits on packing official documents into song** (inferred from four examples): ① **one piece carries one matter** (【一封书】 = the entire letter persuading Zuo Liangyu; 【本序】 = the petition to enthrone; 【高阳台】 plus three repeats = the three great crimes and five reasons against; 【锁南枝】 plus two repeats = the memorial and the proclamation); when the items outnumber the lines, continue with repeats; ② **cut the formulaic phrases of official documents**, keep only the judgment sentences and the items themselves; ③ **someone must ask before the song and someone must answer after it** (after each crime Shi Kefa responds, "This crime is not small," "What is the next crime?"). **Remove any one of the three and packing documents into song turns into reading a file aloud.**
- **Reappearing the same qupai creates irony**: Scene 5, "Visiting Emerald," sheng sings 【雁过声】: "Look closely—the window is bright, the courtyard open"; Scene 28, "Inscribing the Painting," the same man in the same tower sings 【雁过声】: "Desolate—the beauty has gone far"; twenty-three scenes apart. Scene 5's 【锦缠道】 / Scene 38 Shi Kefa's 【锦缠道】; Scene 2 dan learns to sing 【皂罗袍】 and 【好姐姐】 / Scene 39 dan uses the same qupai while sewing the banner. **Self-check**: did the first appearance lodge firmly in the audience's memory (did it have a complete scene)? A qupai that reappears without having lodged is as good as absent.
- **Praise and blame for loyalty and treachery rest on "using the same qupai once each," not on lines that state a position**: in Scene 32, "At the Altar," 【朝天子】 is first sung by Shi Kefa mourning Chongzhen, then by Ma and Ruan discussing the killing of the crown prince; 【普天乐】 has the traitor singing "I've asked for leave to enjoy the spring" and the loyal minister singing "I weep in the wild and pour a cup by the river." **Self-check**: when the same qupai is used twice, can the audience tell the standpoints from content alone? If it still needs a bystander to point them out, the two pieces are written flat.
- **Medley suites (集曲, jíqǔ)**: Scene 17 derives 【锦渔灯】, 【锦上花】, 【锦中拍】, 【锦后拍】 from the parent qupai 【渔灯儿】; **sung in sequence within one scene, one piece per person, the last piece given to the protagonist** (Xiangjun)—one emotional color, one rhyme, four people each stating a position.
- **One qupai to the end writes a great collapse**: Scene 36, "Fleeing the Disaster," uses only 【香柳娘】 for the whole scene, sung eight times in succession to string together eight groups (emperor → prime minister → vice minister of war → two singing together → courtesans → professional guests → governor → Su Kunsheng → Xiangjun). **The refrain position is exactly the same, while the filled content falls all the way from "pearls secretly shed" to "village fists borne together." Self-check**: if the refrain content stays parallel and does not descend, repetition is only repetition.
- **The only place in the play that directly discusses singing technique** (Scene 2, Su Kunsheng teaching a song): "'The character 美 is one beat; the character 奈 is one beat'"; "'The character 丝 is the wùtóu (务头 — the most important and most beautiful phrase), and must be sung inside the throat.'" ⚠️ **Wang Jilie's correction (王季烈) must be quoted along with it**: Kong Shangren **mistakenly combines two separate matters**—"the two 丝 are wùtóu" and "the character 丝 must not be voiced aloud"—into one; 丝 belongs to the Zhi–Shi rhyme and is sung with the sound escaping between the teeth, which has nothing to do with wùtóu. See qupai, Section 4.

## §4 Conventions Layer (程式, chéngshì; full text of main-text section 3.4, including the prop's seven transformations)

- **The fumo's opening (副末开场, fùmò kāichǎng) expanded into a through-line** (the play's greatest structural invention, and what distinguishes it from The Peony Pavilion): **all four unnumbered scenes are presented or presided over by fumo as the Old Master of Ceremonies**—the trial first scene opens the play, the added Scene 21 comments on the upper volume and introduces the lower, the continued Scene 40 closes the play (Zhang Wei in the intercalary Scene 20 is a separately devised eyewitness). **And the Old Master of Ceremonies is simultaneously a character in the play**: in Scene 3, "Riot at the Ding Sacrifice," he actually goes on stage and beats Ruan Dacheng; in Scene 32, "At the Altar," he reads the prayer in his capacity as an usher at the Court of Imperial Sacrifices (**this is the profession he announced in the trial first scene; frame and plot become one**); in Scene 38 he encounters Shi Kefa throwing himself into the river and collects the garments; in the continued Scene 40 he is pursued by a runner. **Self-check**: if a narrator only ever speaks inside the frame, his commentary carries no weight; let him undertake at least one real action in the main line.
- **Stage-pushing device (推场法, tuīchǎngfǎ)**: the trial first scene's last line, "That young gentleman has long since entered—look, everyone"; the added Scene 21's last line, "And here comes Ma Shiying on stage again—look, everyone"—same syntax, no exit poem.
- **"Non-plot scenes" get no qupai**: the intercalary Scene 20, "Idle Talk," **has zero qupai in the whole scene**; three strangers talk under a bean trellis on a rainy night, and the history that cannot be shown directly on stage (the fall of Beijing, the laying out of Chongzhen, the Qing troops entering the pass to repair the tomb) is given to an eyewitness to narrate. Three conditions: ① the speaker must be the only eyewitness present; ② the listeners must ask questions on the audience's behalf, cutting the long narration into segments; ③ emotion must not rely on song but on stage business and sound effects (strong wind and thunder, vengeful ghosts, soft music and the warning cries that clear the way). **Failure condition**: if the information can be brought out naturally by a main-line character, do not create a separate scene—the price of a non-plot scene is broken rhythm.
- **The prop's seven transformations** (if you take only one thing from this play, take this):

| Scene | Identity of the fan | Turning point it triggers |
|---|---|---|
| 5 "Visiting Emerald" | fragrant fan **pendant** (a similar object appears once first) | tossed up to the tower = the opening gambit succeeds |
| 6 "Sleeping with Fragrance" | white silk **palace fan** inscribed with a poem = the love-token document | the marriage is made |
| 7 "Refusing the Dowry" | the lyric names it: "keep it tight in your sleeve" | plants the hint that "the fan will meet danger" |
| 17 "Refusing the Matchmaker" | the **proof** for refusing marriage | the first refusal |
| 22 "Guarding the Tower" | a **weapon** ("it looks like a sword for self-defense") → damaged by blood | the second refusal + disfigurement |
| 23 "Sending the Fan" | blood spots painted into peach blossoms → the name is born → sent as a **letter home** | object and play title become one |
| 27 → 28 | the letter arrives → **relic** ("for the peach blossom a debt of life and death was contracted") | the lovers miss each other |
| 40 "Entering the Way" | **torn to pieces** on the spot by a third party | the love is cut off |

  **Rule**: each change of identity must be caused by **someone else's action** (Hou inscribes the poem, Yang paints the peach blossoms, Su escorts it, Hou receives it, Zhang Wei tears it), not by the owner stroking it repeatedly; each change is pressed onto a turn of value. One more stroke: in Scene 25 the Hongguang Emperor rewards Xiangjun with another **peach-blossom palace fan** to hide her shame—**a fake of the same object appears**; the true fan is moral integrity, the false fan is imperial favor, placed side by side in the same scene.
- **Role-type codes are casting arrangements, not character names**: sheng (Hou Fangyu) and dan (Li Xiangjun) **do not double**, everyone else does—"jing" is Su Kunsheng in Scene 2 and Ma Shiying in Scene 15. **When reading the script and encountering "xiaosheng," first check the "playing so-and-so" at the head of the scene**; this is the greatest reading obstacle between qupai-system convention and the modern script. The three codes fujing = Ruan Dacheng, chou = Liu Jingting, jing = Su Kunsheng each have one continuous character, exactly matching the four summary lines—**role-type assignment is itself the implementation of the outline**.
- **Five ways of closing an exit poem** (下场诗, xiàchǎngshī; this play always writes new poems, **anti-jitang** [集唐, jítáng — assembled Tang lines]): landscape close (Scene 8); verdict close that also hangs a hook (Scene 9's last line, "the tower ships go east—a lifetime's error"); self-praise close later overturned by the plot as irony (Scene 10); leave-someone-behind close (Scene 12: the departing have exited, the poem is finished by those who stay); **after great grief, one person speaks it alone** (Scene 13). The norm is four speakers, with sheng speaking the last line.
- **Sacrificial ritual protocol enters the play as is** (Scene 32, "At the Altar"): the invocations are written out line by line (burying the blood and hair—welcoming the spirits—paying respects—offering silk—reading the prayer—wailing—first, second, and final libations—removing the offerings and sending off the spirits—beholding the burial—rite complete), with the full prayer inserted. **The same word "weep" is divided into three layers**: the officials weep three times by order (required by the protocol), Shi Kefa weeps alone and loudly (outside the protocol), the Old Master of Ceremonies "cannot help but weep a whole bout" (outside both)—only by setting the rigid protocol out as is do the latter two layers have a gap to measure against.

#### 3. The Prop Chain: Seven Identities for One Fan (the play's most directly transferable technique)

| Scene | Identity of the fan | Turning point it triggers |
|---|---|---|

**Rule**: each change of identity must be caused by **someone else's action** (the poem is inscribed by Hou, the peach blossoms painted by Yang Longyou, the fan escorted by Su, received by Hou), not by the owner stroking it repeatedly; and each change is pressed onto a turn of value. In addition, in Scene 25 the Hongguang Emperor rewards Xiangjun with another **peach-blossom palace fan** to hide her shame—**a fake of the same object appears**, placing "the true fan is moral integrity, the false fan is imperial favor" side by side in the same scene; this is the cruelest stroke in the play.

#### 3.2 Xiangjun's Three Refusals: The Same Character Action Written Three Times Without Repetition

| Round | Scene | Who pressures her | Stake | Her means | Cost | Song/speech division |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | 7 "Refusing the Dowry" | Yang Wencong (good intentions) | a dowry of over two hundred taels | pulls out her hairpin, strips off her finery, sings 「川拨棹」 | loses the money | **song** (this qupai; sheng takes the repeat) |
| Second | 17 "Refusing the Matchmaker" + 22 "Guarding the Tower" | six acquaintances + a vicious servant | three hundred taels, the power of the prime minister's household | raises the fan as proof; three escalating spoken passages; flails with the fan; butts her head | disfigurement, loss of her foster mother | **mostly speech**; only one piece each of 「锦后拍」 and 「摊破锦地花」 |
| Third | 24 "Cursing at the Feast" | Ma Shiying, Ruan Dacheng (the holders of power) | her life | enters under a false name, curses three times at the banquet | kicked down, sent to the palace as a slave | **four pieces of song, each harder than the last** |

**Pattern**: the same action written three times is differentiated by three things—**the rank of the opponent** (friend → acquaintance → powerful minister), **the scale of the cost** (money → body and family → freedom and life), **the reversal of the song/speech ratio** (song → speech → song). The second has the lowest song/speech ratio because that is when she **does** the most (striking, butting); the third is the fullest in song because that is when she **has only her mouth left**. This holds for "a character holds the same position three times" in any opera genre and any subject.

## §5 The Sixteen General Rules of The Peach Blossom Fan (《桃花扇凡例》): Item-by-item Transcription of the Original

### 3. Item-by-item Transcription of the General Rules (sixteen items in all)

URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/桃花扇凡例 . Each item begins "一、" in the original; the numbering follows the original order; **no omissions or rewrites have been made**.

1. The play is named The Peach Blossom Fan; then the peach-blossom fan is like the pearl, and the brush that writes The Peach Blossom Fan is like the dragon. It threads through clouds and enters mist, now straight, now oblique, but dragon eyes and dragon claws never leave the pearl. The viewer should use a giant's eye.
2. Court politics, gains and losses, the gatherings and scatterings of literati—all are firmly verified as to time and place, with no borrowing whatsoever. As for the love of young men and women and the banter of guests, though there is a little embellishment, it is not in the same class as things utterly without foundation.
3. The staging has rises and falls, turns and reversals, each opening a realm of its own; it comes abruptly and leaves suddenly, so that the audience cannot predict its situations. Whatever situation can be predicted is a stale formula.
4. Every scene's veins run connected; none can be shifted, none can be reduced. It is not like old plays, dragged east and west to patch together a scene.
5. In the various editions of filled lyrics, every long act (折, zhé) conventionally uses ten tunes and every short act eight. Actors cut the complex and take the simple, singing only five or six; often what they drop and keep is improper, betraying the author's painstaking heart. Now for long acts I fill only eight tunes, and for short acts six or four, so that they cannot cut again.
6. The tune names are not chosen for novelty; their suites are all ones current performers know well, so there is no need to investigate—they become song as they enter the mouth. But the words must be new and striking, not a single one following in another's tooth-marks.
7. The lyrics are never filled in at random. Whenever a feeling in the breast cannot be spoken, or a scene before the eyes cannot be shown, I borrow lyrics to sing it. Again, when one matter is told a second time and has already been given in spoken dialogue, lyrics replace it. But if something ought to be spoken and is put into lyrics, the listener will not understand, and the sequence before and after will break. And when it has already been spoken, why must it enter the lyrics again?
8. Making a tune must have purpose and interest: one piece completes the writing of one piece, one line completes the writing of one line. Set it on a desk or sing it in the theater, it should move and arouse, making people beat time and sigh in admiration—this is what is meant by "song that is good." If it is forced and perfunctory, entirely without meaning, then singer and listener alike are in for misery.
9. For lyrics to enter the mode and fit level and oblique tones, the whole aim is that the meaning of the words be bright and clear. I often see Southern tunes cramped and wrenched, so that people cannot understand them; even if forced to fit the strings and bamboo, they can only serve as gongche notation—how can that be called filling lyrics?
10. The allusions used in the lyrics are picked up at random, without exposing the trace of piled-up platters—the look of undigested allusion-mongering. Transform the stale into the new, turn the rigid into the living. Summoning ghosts and stacking corpses—such things I will not take.
11. Spoken dialogue should be cadenced and ringing, sentences neat and practiced; the comic business and banter all have their own interest. Better to be not quite popular than to injure elegance; this rather captures the intent of the poets.
12. In old editions, spoken dialogue is only three parts; when actors go on stage, they add seven parts of their own; vulgar manners and vicious jokes often turn gold into iron and become a burden on the writing. Now the spoken dialogue is complete and detailed, with no room to add a single word. The somewhat greater length is for this reason.
13. The comic business of laughter, anger, and scolding is like plain-line drawing of figures: every eyebrow and beard appears, and what draws the audience in depends entirely on this. Now all is marked out in detail; face and spirit leap on the page, teeming with life—how much more so when an actor imitates them.
14. Role types exist to distinguish the gentleman from the petty man; sometimes, when the proper color is insufficient, one borrows chou or jing. The clean face and the painted face are like a person's beauty and ugliness: one should appreciate them beyond the distinctions of male and female, black and yellow—looking past surface categories to the real quality.
15. The entrance and exit poems are the beginning and end, the order and coherence, of a single rhyme. If old lines or vulgar lines are used and hastily patched to fill the space, the whole scene loses color. Current editions mostly favor assembling Tang lines, which is also a stale formula. Now I have created new poems for all: they have a beginning when they start and a thread when they end; the meaning of "making the going clear and the returning graceful" can, it seems, be pursued.
16. The whole book is forty scenes. Of these, the upper volume begins with a trial first scene and ends with an intercalary scene; the lower volume begins with an added scene and ends with a continued scene—these too are the beginning and end, the order and coherence, of the forty scenes as a whole. With a beginning and an ending, the qi is full and the spirit complete, and it escapes the worn path of separation, reunion, grief, and joy. Is it not fair to call it a play?
    (The Wikisource text of this item reads "下本首下本首加一出"; the three characters "下本首" are duplicated and are a transcription error on that page; following the sense and the play's actual arrangement, I have corrected it to one occurrence.)

**The sixteen items grouped by function** (not the original grouping; this is the note-taker's arrangement for transfer into the skill):
- Theme and through-line: 1 (pearl and dragon)
- Discipline of historical fact: 2
- Structure: 3 (rise and fall of staging, against "stale formulas"), 4 (each scene's veins cannot be added to or cut), 16 (forty scenes + the four unnumbered trial/intercalary/added/continued scenes as overall order)
- Number and metre of tunes: 5 (long acts eight tunes, short acts six or four), 6 (suites use familiar qupai, words must be new), 9 (mode and tonal pattern subordinate to bright meaning)
- Division of song and speech: 7 (**what should be sung and what should be spoken are mutually exclusive; what has been spoken is not put into song again**), 8 (one piece one composition, one line one composition)
- Rhetoric: 10 (allusions not piled up)
- Spoken dialogue and comic business: 11 (cadenced, better not popular than injuring elegance), 12 (**spoken dialogue complete, no word to be added**, against actors adding seventy percent), 13 (stage business marked in detail)
- Role types: 14 (role types distinguish gentleman and petty man; when proper color is insufficient, borrow chou or jing)
- Conventions: 15 (entrance and exit poems must be newly written, anti-jitang)

## §7 Key Points of the "Short Preface," "Brief Note," and "Origin and End"

### 1. "Short Preface to The Peach Blossom Fan" (《桃花扇小引》)—Genre Positioning and Motive for Writing

Third month of the jimao year of Kangxi (1699), written at random by the Yunting Mountain Man.

- **Chuanqi is a mixed genre that "lacks no form," not a minor path:**
  > Though chuanqi is a minor path, it includes every form—poetry and rhapsody, ci and qu, parallel prose, fiction. As for portraying eyebrows and beards and tinting scenes and objects, it also joins the painter's studio.
- **Its purpose is rooted in the Three Hundred Poems (《三百篇》), its method of judgment takes from the Spring and Autumn Annals (《春秋》), its brushwork from the Zuo Commentary, the Discourses of the States, and the Grand Historian**—this hangs the standard of chuanqi writing directly on historiography:
  > Its purpose is truly rooted in the Three Hundred Poems; its judgment is the Spring and Autumn; its use of brush and composition are Zuo, Guo, and the Grand Historian. Thereby to warn the world and change customs, to praise the sage way and assist kingly transformation—this is the nearest and most urgent. The music of today is still the music of antiquity; how can this not be believed?
  ("甫王化" follows the Wikisource text; current edited editions mostly read "辅王化"—same meaning, a textual variant.)
- **The subject is "new events," and the audience includes people who were there**—this is what determined that the play could not be fiction:
  > The play The Peach Blossom Fan deals entirely with new events of the Southern Dynasty; fathers and elders still survive. Singing and dancing on stage, pointing from outside the action, they know by whom the three-hundred-year foundation was overthrown, by what it was ruined, in what year it perished, in what place it ceased. It not only makes the audience sigh and weep; it can also chasten and move the human heart, and be one rescue for a declining age.
  **"By whom was it overthrown? By what was it ruined? In what year did it perish? In what place did it cease?"—these four questions are the play's problematic**, the interrogative form of its controlling idea.
- Writing intensity and circumstances:
  > I gathered widely the surviving records and put them into music; every line, every word, was wrung from the heart with pain... I regret that though readers are many, not one has ever looked a line or a word through to the end. Each time I stroke my breast and sigh deeply, I am almost ready to commit it to the fire.

### 2. "Brief Note on The Peach Blossom Fan" (《桃花扇小识》)—"Strange Because Not Strange": How the Thematic Eye Grows from a Vulgar Matter

Third month of the wuzi year of Kangxi (1698), written at random by the Yunting Mountain Man. **This is the most important piece of structural theory in the whole book**, explaining how a small prop can carry the play's theme of rise and fall.

- The opening sentence is chuanqi's law of subject selection:
  > Chuanqi transmits the strangeness of its matter; if the matter is not strange, it is not transmitted.
- Then **he asks and answers himself, first degrading the material to the bottom** (a courtesan's fan / a wastrel's inscription / a visitor's painting / slashing the face to swear a vow / blood spots dyeing a flower / a sealed letter sent in secret—each in turn judged "vulgar," "petty," "light," "obscene and not worth mentioning"), then uses a **chain of anadiplosis** to push it up to rise and fall:
  > What is strange in its not being strange is the peach blossom on the fan. The peach blossom is the beauty's bloodstain. The bloodstain is her keeping chastity and awaiting marriage, her head broken and bleeding rather than submit to the powerful traitor. The powerful traitor is the remnant of the Wei eunuch's faction (魏阉, Wèi Yān). The remnant is the one who advanced music and beauty, gathered goods and profit, formed factions and took revenge, and overthrew the three-hundred-year imperial foundation.
- It closes on material evidence:
  > The imperial foundation is gone—where are the powerful traitors? Only the beauty's bloodstain, the peach blossom on the fan, is praised on every tongue and clear before every eye. This is the matter that is strange because it is not strange, that need not be transmitted and yet can be transmitted.
- **Structural lesson**: a small person's private object → a wound on the body → a political camp → a dynasty's foundation: a four-level leap, each level beginning with the noun of the level above (anadiplosis). This is the author's own statement behind "one prop runs through to the end" recorded in the first notebook.
- **Note**: the line "borrow the feeling of separation and reunion to write the sense of rise and fall" is often attributed to the "Short Preface" and "Brief Note"; **its actual source is the fumo's speech in the trial first scene, 《先声》 (Prelude)** ("Yesterday in the Taiping Garden I saw a newly published chuanqi called The Peach Blossom Fan... it borrows the feeling of separation and reunion to write the sense of rise and fall; real events and real people, with evidence and proof"—see https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/桃花扇/先聲 ). The closing passage of the Outline—"Color is the image of separation and reunion... qi is the number of rise and fall"—is its taxonomic version. When quoting, cite the right source.

### 5. "Origin and End of The Peach Blossom Fan" (《桃花扇本末》)—Only the Parts Related to the Writing Process

URL: https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/桃花扇本末 . (The original has twelve paragraphs; those on travel and banquets, the splendor of performances, and the cost of printing, which are unrelated to method, are not recorded.)

- **The chain of source material** (which determines where "verified time and place" comes from): his elder clansman Fangxun (方训) served as a ministry official in the southern capital at the end of Chongzhen; his maternal uncle Qin Guangyi (秦光仪), fleeing disorder, depended on him and lodged there for three years, "learning the surviving affairs of the Hongguang reign in great detail"; after returning home he repeatedly recounted them to Kong Shangren; Kong "checked them against the various unofficial records, and none disagreed—they were a veritable record." **Only the episode of the blood splashed on the fan and Yang Longyou painting it into blossoms came from "Longyou's young scribe telling it to Fangxun"; "though it is not seen in other books, the matter is new and strange and worth transmitting"**—
  > The play The Peach Blossom Fan was made in response to this. The rise and fall of the Southern Dynasty is thus tied to the bottom of the peach-blossom fan.
  That is: the play's only fictional core is the origin of that one prop; everything else is a veritable record.
- **Writing period and three revisions**: before entering office he "only sketched its outline; in truth had not yet adorned its rhetoric and color"; he once boasted to a close friend, "I have a chuanqi, The Peach Blossom Fan, still hidden in my pillow"; more than ten years later, only because Tian Lunxia (田纶霞) asked for the manuscript, did he "trim the lamp and fill in the lyrics,"
  > Three drafts in all before the book was complete; it was the sixth month of the jimao year.
- **The working method of filling lyrics (the most methodologically valuable passage in this piece)**: Kong admits, "Though I know a little about modes, I fear I am not in tune with the singer's mouth"; his earlier play The Little Hulei (《小忽雷》, named for a small pear-shaped lute) was entirely filled in by Gu Tianshi (顾天石) on his behalf; while writing The Peach Blossom Fan he had Wang Shouxi (王寿熙), a man of Wu (a current actor who knew suites thoroughly), with him morning and evening,
  > He showed me the suites of the songbooks, which current actors know thoroughly, and I filled according to the score. Each time a piece was finished, it had to be sung to the beat; if there was the slightest awkward character, it was immediately revised. Hence the whole book has no ailment of a jarring tongue.
  **"Each time a piece was finished, it had to be sung to the beat; if there was the slightest awkward character, it was immediately revised" is the standard verification loop for qupai-system lyric writing**, and it forms an inside-and-outside pair with Item 9 of the General Rules (mode and tonal pattern subordinate to bright meaning).
- **Performance feedback entered the revision**: in the bingxu year he watched performances at Hengshan for two days; "where my intention was not satisfied, I called the troupe head and instructed him on the spot"—the author revised a second time through rehearsal coaching.
- **A revised version exists**: Gu Tianshi "read my The Peach Blossom Fan, extended it, and changed it into The Southern Peach Blossom Fan (《南桃花扇》). He has sheng and dan reunite on stage to please the audience's eyes"; Kong's judgment was "his language is brilliant and striking, following Linchuan (临川 — Tang Xianzu). Though he fills in what I fall short of, he cannot avoid making me look a coarse fellow." **This shows that the reunion ending was market pressure of the time; Kong knew it and did not take it.**
- **The commentary format**: each act's line commentary is in the page header ("each act's line commentary at the top"), the overall commentary at the end of the act, all written casually by readers, and the author kept them all. In current editions, the page-header commentary and the end-of-scene overall commentary **are not Kong Shangren's self-commentary**.

## §8 Distribution of the Four Lines in the Upper Volume's Twenty Scenes and a Compilation of Exit Poems

### 4.2 Distribution of the Four Lines in the Upper Volume's Twenty Scenes (assigned according to the four summary lines of the trial first scene)

The trial first scene gives four outline lines: "Treacherous Ma and Ruan, inside and out, lay a long sword; clever Liu and Su come and go, pulling a secret thread. Young Master Hou cuts off the bond of flowers and moon; Zhang Daoshi sums up the case of rise and fall." Assigning the upper volume's twenty scenes to these four lines reveals Kong Shangren's **rhythm of alternation**:

| Line | Scenes | Count |
|---|---|---|
| sheng–dan separation and reunion (Young Master Hou cuts off the bond of flowers and moon) | 2 Teaching the Song, 5 Visiting Emerald, 6 Sleeping with Fragrance, 7 Refusing the Dowry, 17 Refusing the Matchmaker | 5 |
| Treacherous faction (Treacherous Ma and Ruan, inside and out, lay a long sword) | 3 Riot at the Ding Sacrifice, 4 Spying on the Play, 12 Leaving the Academy, 14 Blocking the Traitor, 15 Welcoming the Imperial Chariot, 16 Holding Court | 6 |
| Lower-order needle-threaders (Clever Liu and Su come and go, pulling a secret thread) | 1 Listening to the Ballad, 10 Writing the Letter, 11 At the Camp Gate | 3 |
| Military and political (trunk of the rise-and-fall line) | 9 Pacifying the Troops, 13 Mourning the Sovereign, 18 Fighting for Precedence, 19 Peace and War, 20 Shifting Defense | 5 |
| Two lines side by side | 8 Tumult at the Pavilion (elegant gathering and Ruan's lantern boat on the same river) | 1 |
| Frame / non-plot | Trial Scene 1 Prelude, Intercalary Scene 20 Idle Talk, Added Scene 21 Solitary Chant | 3 |

**The arrangement that can be read off**: (1) **never three scenes in a row on the same line**—at most two (18 and 19 are consecutive martial scenes; Scene 20 at once turns back to personnel outside Nanjing, where sheng and dan are); (2) **whenever the treacherous-faction line gains power, a scene of the sheng–dan line suffering follows immediately** (15 Welcoming the Imperial Chariot → 16 Holding Court → 17 Refusing the Matchmaker); (3) the three scenes of the lower-order needle-threading line all sit at the point where the two lines are about to join or not join (Scene 1 brings out the protagonists; 10 and 11 connect Nanjing and Wuchang). **This is a long-play staging rhythm table that can be copied directly.**

## §9 Transferable Techniques (full text of examples, self-checks, and failure conditions; complete version of main-text section 3.6)

1. **A small prop must be able to push by anadiplosis all the way to the play's theme.** Check with the four-level chain from the "Brief Note": private object → bodily trace → camp → era ("the peach blossom on the fan / the beauty's bloodstain / refusal to submit to the powerful traitor / overthrow of the three-hundred-year imperial foundation"). **Failure condition**: if the chain breaks at the second level (the prop concerns only two people's love), it is merely a token, not the thematic eye; do not put the play's title on it.
2. **The same character action written three times without repetition is distinguished by three things**: the rank of the opponent, the scale of the cost, the reversal of the song/speech ratio. [Xiangjun's three refusals: Scene 7 "Refusing the Dowry" against a friend, loss of money, **song** (this qupai + sheng taking the repeat); 17 "Refusing the Matchmaker" + 22 "Guarding the Tower" against six acquaintances and a vicious servant, disfigurement and loss of her foster mother, **mostly speech** (that is when she "does" the most: striking, butting); 24 "Cursing at the Feast" against the powerful, freedom and life, **four pieces of song, each harder than the last** (that is when she has only her mouth left)] **Self-check**: if the song/speech ratio is exactly the same all three times, the writing has merely repeated itself.
3. **A crowd besieges one person: break a linked pair into lines, assign them in order of attack, and give the besieged person only spoken dialogue.** [Scene 17, 「北骂玉郎带上小楼」, six people sing in turn: inducement → intimidation → shrewishness → official authority → physical force → collapse; Xiangjun never joins this qupai] **Self-check**: if the order of the divided lines can be arbitrarily swapped, the division is bad. **The division of song and speech is itself the division of standpoints.**
4. **Give the hardest words to spoken dialogue, not song.** [Scene 35: "If the field battle goes badly, defend the city / if defending the city goes badly, fight in the streets / if street fighting goes badly, fight hand to hand / if hand-to-hand fighting goes badly, take your own life"; between each segment a "Yes!" is inserted as a beat; Scene 22: "Then I will wait for him three years, wait for him ten years, wait for him a hundred years—only I will not marry Tian Yang"] **Self-check**: if these lines were rewritten as song, would filler words have to be added for the character count and tonal pattern? If yes, they were meant to be speech.
5. **A character's professional skill must be a plot mechanism, not decoration.** [Liu Jingting's storytelling → delivering the letter (Scene 10) → storming the camp gate (Scene 11) → carrying the proclamation (Scene 31); each time the cost rises a level, **each entrance changes to a heavier musical form** (storytelling chant → two Northern tunes → a full Northern suite); Su Kunsheng's singing → teaching a song (Scene 2) → **singing in curfew as a way to knock on the door** (Scene 31, four pieces on the same qupai = four levels of volume, until he is arrested); Lan Ying's painting → inscribing the painting to save Hou (28 → 30)] **Self-check**: if this person's craft were swapped for another, would the plot still work? If it would, the craft was given for nothing.
6. **A play-within-the-play must do work; it may not be atmosphere.** [Scene 2, teaching The Peony Pavilion = **giving the character something no one else has**; Scene 24, Ma Shiying himself tells how The Crying Phoenix (《鸣凤记》) paints a villain's face = **making the man who will have his face painted describe the power of face-painting**; Scene 25, because she knows only The Peony Pavilion and not The Swallow's Letter, she is assigned the chou role, and then because she sings well is reassigned to zhengdan, rewarded with a fan, and kept in the palace = **"whether she can sing a given play" directly decides the character's fate**] **In The Peach Blossom Fan there is not one play-within-the-play that serves only as atmosphere.**
7. **An anti-reunion ending must be overturned twice.** [Scene 40, the fan is torn and the paths divide (overturning the sheng–dan reunion) + continued Scene 40, the three old men scatter before a runner (overturning the "fisher and woodcutter chat" harbor)] **Self-check**: does the second overturning bring something new (here, "even the bystanders have nowhere to stand")? If it is only another helping of misery, it is a superfluous scene. The order of tearing the fan must also be copied: **first let sheng and dan finish one love song, then let the two look at the fan together once, and only then tear it**—tearing at the fullest point of emotion is the only way it holds.
8. **Landscape at the end: walk a road first, then speak a line.** [Continued Scene 40, seven pieces of 「哀江南」; the first six give one scene each in the order of the journey (city outskirts → Xiaoling [the Ming founder's mausoleum] → imperial city → Qinhuai River → Long Bridge → old quarter), each noting only "what is missing"; the seventh gathers them into three "I have seen with my own eyes"] **Self-check**: have all six places **been staged in earlier scenes**? A place never staged, however well written, will not move anyone. **Failure condition**: a short play has no old scenery to recycle.

**Where the masterpiece breaks its own laws**: level and oblique tones are often off, filler characters are not well placed; the syntax of "Teaching the Song" is wrong ("配他公子千金体" is scanned by the metrical pattern (板式, bǎnshì) as "子千金体," which is not a phrase; Wu Mei (吴梅) therefore judged it "**fine lyrics but no fine tune**"); "Mourning at the Altar" uses a Southern 【朝天子】 in place of a Northern one, "greatly losing the sense of a North–South interleaved suite." See qupai, Section 11. The disagreement is preserved side by side: **Kong Shangren's General Rules and the judgments of Wu Mei and Wang Jilie are two coordinate systems; write both** (end of Section 11).
