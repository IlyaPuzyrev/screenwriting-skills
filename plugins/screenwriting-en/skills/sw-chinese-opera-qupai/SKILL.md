# Chinese Opera Playwriting: The Qupai System (戏曲编剧·曲牌体)

> **The output language is the language of the question**; always anchor terms back to their originals — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md) — and never invent translations. Opera terms (曲牌体 qǔpáitǐ, 宫调 gōngdiào, 套数 tàoshù, 务头 wùtóu, 衬字 chènzì, 前腔 qiánqiāng, 换头 huàntóu, 幺篇 yāopiān, 赠板 zèngbǎn, 帮腔 bāngqiāng, 宾白 bīnbái, 科介 kējiè, 折 zhé, 出 chū, 楔子 xiēzi) have no English equivalents; in any language, keep the original term and add a one-sentence explanation.

This skill is the **qupai-system** cell in the opera row of the stagecraft layer; its paired cell is [sw-chinese-opera-banqiang](../sw-chinese-opera-banqiang/SKILL.md). **The division is by vocal system, not by opera genre**: in the qupai system the lyrics are **filled** into fixed tune patterns (曲牌, qǔpái — named tunes with set character counts, line patterns, and tonal patterns); the units are the act (折) and the scene (出), with one mode per act. In the banqiang system (板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ — the paired-line system) the lyrics are seven- or ten-character **paired lines**, and the aria is sustained by changes of metrical pattern; the unit is the scene (场). For the rationale of the division, see section 0.1 of banqiang.

**The division of labour among the books**: **Li Yu (李渔) supplies the stage rules in 《闲情偶寄》 (Casual Expressions of Idle Feeling)** (he kept his own family troupe, and what he writes is "how a script behaves when it is executed"); **Wu Mei (吴梅) supplies the literary and prosodic rules in 《顾曲麈谈》 (Guqu Zhutan — Jottings on the Study of Song) and 《曲学通论》 (Quxue Tonglun — A General Treatise on the Study of Song)** (the two books are detailed in different places and also contradict each other), and **《中国戏曲概论》 (An Outline of Chinese Opera) supplies the criteria of judgment**; **Wang Jilie (王季烈) supplies the method of staging (排场) in 《螾庐曲谈》 (Yinlu Qutan — Yinlu's Talks on Song)**; **the prefatory rules and outline of Kong Shangren (孔尚任)** stand alongside Li Yu as the two primary sources; **the three extant zaju of Guan Hanqing (关汉卿) supply hard evidence from twelve acts** (according to the Yuanqu xuan edition on Wikisource); **Tang Xianzu (汤显祖), Wei Minglun (魏明伦), Weng Ouhong (翁偶虹), and Luo Huaizhen (罗怀臻) supply the exceptions — adaptation and modern practice**.

For tables, act-by-act and scene-by-scene data, the complete qupai table, and long quotations, see [reference.md](reference.md). **Not in this skill**: everything about the banqiang system → banqiang; items 17–34 of the forty prohibitions (the whole run of clichés, coinages, vulgarisms, obscurity, over-literary phrasing, and pedantic phrasing) are purely verbal → `sw-dialogue`; the four questions of adaptation → `sw-format-adaptation`; props and entrances and exits → `sw-scene-craft`.

## 1. Starting Position: Two Schools, Two Scoring Lines, Two Preconditions

**Read this first**: the rules of the qupai system are things said by two sets of people from two positions; if you do not first distinguish the positions, the rules that follow will fight one another.

**1.1 The stage school vs. the study-and-prosody school (parallel, not adjudicated).** Li Yu is the stage school: "What Jin Shengtan (金圣叹) judged was **the Xixiang (《西厢记》, The Romance of the Western Chamber) that men of letters toy with, not the Xixiang that actors perform**." Wu Mei and Wang Jilie are the study-and-prosody school: they discuss music before playwriting, the exact opposite of Li Yu's "structure first, music third." **Wu Mei's split verdict on Li Yu must be read in both places**: praise — his staging and comic dialogue "**stand alone for all time**"; blame — "**his layout is skilful, but his wording is very clumsy... not fit to enter the critical rankings of the lyric world**." → **When citing Li Yu, mark that he gives stage rules, not literary or prosodic rules.** Wang Jilie occupies a third position: his stance is the stage, his methods are wholly prosody. **The three-way division of labour (製曲 zhìqǔ — writing the lyrics, the playwright's job / 譜曲 pǔqǔ — setting the tune / 度曲 dùqǔ — singing it) and the distinction between the score (曲谱, qǔpǔ) and the mode register (宫谱, gōngpǔ) are in reference-tizhi.md §16** — **if you ask "which Kunqu score do I consult?" ask first whether you need the words or the tune; if you cannot tell them apart, you will be given the wrong book.**

**1.2 The Tang–Shen controversy (three parties in parallel, not adjudicated).** Shen Jing (沈璟): "**better that the music be harmonious though the words are unskilled**" (宁律协而词不工); Tang Xianzu: "**there is no harm in wrenching the throats of everyone under heaven**" (不妨拗折天下人嗓子); Wu Mei refuses to take sides ("make both beautiful") and gives a criterion of choice: "**The rules fixed by Ning'an can be reached by hard study; the verbal craft of Ruoshi cannot be attained by forced effort**" (宁庵定法，可以力学求之；若士修词，不可勉强企及). The third party is Weng Ouhong's middle way, "as long as the words are clear and the meaning flows" (cross-reference banqiang). **This skill takes only "the side that can be learned."**

**1.3 Play (戏) and song (曲) are two independent scoring lines.** 《桃花扇》 (The Peach Blossom Fan) and 《长生殿》 (The Palace of Eternal Life) measure out **opposite rankings** on the ruler of words and the ruler of staging; the periodizing sentence runs: "Above Qianlong there were both play and song; at the Jiaqing–Daoguang turn there was song but no play; after Xianfeng–Tongzhi there was neither play nor song." **Self-check: read the script twice** — once looking only at plot, staging, hot and cold, and role distribution, once looking only at lyrics, modes, rhyme, and the four tones; both tables must have scores.

**1.4 Precondition one: studio song vs. stage song (清曲 vs. 剧曲).** The three rules on repeated rhymes, padding characters, and mode borrowing are all wrong if stated separately; **you must first ask "for the stage or for the study."**

| | **Stage song (剧曲)** — a stage script with dialogue and action; **use me for a script meant to be performed** | **Studio song (清曲)** — a song suite with dialogue and action removed, lyrics only; **use me for a standalone suite for pure singing, or for a character's unaccompanied song within a play** |
|---|---|---|
| Mode borrowing (借宫) | Allowed (Nanlü may borrow from Shang; Zhonglü from Banshe) | Not allowed |
| Repeated rhyme (重韵) | Allowed, and famous authors did not avoid it | Not allowed |
| Padding characters (衬字) | May be many (the Four Dreams of Linchuan [临川四梦] are "the more hazardous, the more marvellous") | Used sparingly |
| Subject and metre (题材／格律) | Love between sheng and dan is the main axis; metre is loose | "**There is no need to write only the speech of boys and girls**"; metre is stricter |

**Failure condition**: modern new writing has loosened both columns; see section 10.

**1.5 Precondition two: first destroy a false premise.** "Ming–Qing chuanqi all observed the modes" is **wrong** — Wu Mei points out that 《琵琶记》 (The Story of the Lute) and 《拜月亭》 (The Pavilion of Moon Worship) are precisely the **source** of the fashion of "**not seeking the mode or tuning the key**" (不寻宫数调). Two companions: ① **the surviving electronic texts of 《牡丹亭》 (The Peony Pavilion) and 《桃花扇》 uniformly omit mode labels** (verified scene by scene) → **any entry whose mode column carries "[provisional]" (〔拟〕) may serve only as an example, never as evidence**; for discussions of mode and rhyme, **take examples from Yuan zaju only**; in chuanqi only the three Northern suites (〈冥判〉 "The Infernal Judgment", 〈硬拷〉 "The Hard Interrogation", 〈圆驾〉 "The Imperial Procession") and places with solid labels may be used. ② **"Former scholars labelled it this way" is not evidence**: first ask whether a given item is a "rule" (格) or an "example" (例); if it is an example, find a second and third example (《幽闺记·请盟》 "The Story of the Secluded Boudoir: Swearing the Pact" mistakenly treats the Yue-mode 〔看花回〕 as the Xianlü-mode 〔点绛唇〕, and 《邯郸记》 [The Handan Dream] and 《长生殿》 repeat the error in a chain).

## 2. The Zaju Branch (Yuan Zaju)

The evidence is taken from Guan Hanqing's three extant zaju (《窦娥冤》 The Injustice to Dou E, 《救风尘》 Saving the Courtesan, 《金线池》 The Golden Thread Pond, **according to the Yuanqu xuan edition on Wikisource**); the twelve-act statistics are in reference-tizhi.md §9.

- **Four acts and one wedge; the wedge is optional** (《救风尘》 has no wedge; its six-month span is covered by characters' self-narration on entrance). **Failure condition**: if the span contains an event that must be shown on stage, a wedge must be established. **A wedge can only use the Xianlü-mode 〔賞花時〕 or 〔端正好〕, at most two tunes**; if it cannot fit, turn it into an act.
- **One mode per act, no repetition across the four acts; the first act is always Xianlü and the fourth always Shuangdiao** (true in all three plays, and more workable than "four acts, four modes"); the two middle acts choose among Nanlü, Zhenggong, Shang, and Zhonglü according to emotion. **Self-check**: are the first four tunes of act one 〔點絳唇〕, 〔混江龍〕, 〔油葫蘆〕, 〔天下樂〕? Is the first tune of act four 〔新水令〕? If not, first check which edition you are relying on.
- **One rhyme throughout each act; four acts, four rhymes, none repeated**; **mode and rhyme category change together** (self-check: copy the last character of every tune in an act into one column; if a different rhyme category has slipped in, the rhyme is broken). **The wedge has its own rhyme** and does not count toward "four acts, no repetition." **Repeated rhymes within a suite are allowed** — but only in stage song (1.4).
- **A single lead singer, exclusive** (all 123 tunes in the three plays' four acts belong to the zhengdan [正旦 — lead female role]); **the wedge's singer is not fixed** (《窦娥冤》 is sung by the chongmo [沖末 — secondary male role], 《金线池》 by the zhengdan). Self-check: copy the role markers (脚色, jiǎosè) into one column; if a second name appears within the four acts, the form is broken. **Writing crowd scenes**: turn the other characters' words into inserted speech (插白), and have the lead singer **pick up the wording of that speech** in the next line; otherwise the supporting characters turn mute.
- **The right to sing = the right to be the protagonist**: in 《救风尘》 all 44 tunes go to Zhao Pan'er (赵盼儿), "the one who comes to the rescue"; the suffering Song Yinzhang (宋引章) does not sing a single tune, and the play's title follows from this — **structure, singing rights, and title are one and the same decision** (the banqiang system has no equivalent; see section 12).
- **The first and last few tunes of a suite are fixed; the middle may be added to, cut, or reordered** (all twelve acts of the three plays fit the patterns; 〔隔尾〕 and the various modes' borrowed 〔黃鐘尾〕 are established zaju practice, not collected in the current Kunqu suite tables — when something does not match the table, first check whether it is a case of "the table omits it"). **A suite should stay within twelve or thirteen tunes**: too few is called "闪赚" (shǎnzhuàn — skimpy), too many "絮叨" (xùdao — garrulous). **The coda of the final act may be omitted**, on condition that the closure is handed over to the official judgment and the 〔詞云〕 (cíyún — verse epilogue); omitting the coda in a middle act is a missing part.
- **The title-and-name couplet (题目正名)**: at the very end of the play, spoken not sung, two or four lines (as many lines as there are plot threads). **The "name" line must embed the full title of the play, and the action in it must actually be performed in the play**; if you cannot write that line, the title was not well chosen.
- **〔换科〕 (huànkē) is the special marker for "switching in full view"** (substituting a divorce letter, switching who is helping whom): not a word of explanation; the person switched does not know, the audience does; self-check: after the switch there must be a "wrong question" line that cashes in the gap (cross-reference the props section of `sw-scene-craft`).
- **Trap-setting plays** (five of Guan Hanqing's eight extant plays are "a woman sets a trap and brings down a powerful man or wastrel"): **the scheme must be explained openly in a full passage of spoken dialogue before it is carried out**, and **the trap must be half-seen through midway and then wound back around**. **This directly conflicts with the general-layer view of suspense** — opera does not keep secrets from the audience; hide the scheme according to the Western method of suspense, and by the third act there is nothing left to watch.

## 3. The Chuanqi Branch (Ming–Qing Chuanqi and Kunqu)

- **Length**: **Ming chuanqi generally took forty scenes as the norm** — Wu Mei held that this length was itself the disease, and **at sixty scenes it inevitably becomes "the main meaning is neglected and the guest drowns the host"**; **side branches may only be carried along in spoken dialogue** (self-check: outside the main action of each scene, how many other matters are touched? more than one, and they should be folded into dialogue). The Qing remedy was the short-play collection in which "**one act exhausts one event**."
- **Division into volumes**: 《桃花扇》 was performed as upper and lower volumes, one night each, joined by an added twenty-first scene in which the fumo returns (self-check: if the upper volume stands by itself, the lower becomes a sequel). **The sequence of each scene "may not be altered, may not be reduced"** (prefatory rule 4); **a long scene has eight tunes, a short scene six or four** (prefatory rule 5, to prevent actors from cutting at random) — **the price is stated alongside**: Wu Mei criticized this quota for making "the writing unable to flow freely," and "**sheng and dan cannot be treated carelessly**."
- **The fumo's opening (副末开场)**: in the first scene the fumo (副末, fùmò — the minor male role who opens the play) states the intention and the outline, without enacting the story; Li Yu splits it into two layers — **first a short entrance tune that "hints" at the intention, then a family-gate passage that "states plainly" the outline**; the family gate (家门, jiāmén — the opening summary of the play's intent and plot) is the hardest to write, and **one may "leave the first piece out for now and insert it at the final scene."** **《桃花扇》 expands it into a through-line**: four unnumbered scenes are all presented by the fumo Old Zhanli (老赞礼), **and Old Zhanli is at the same time a character in the play**. **Self-check**: a narrator who speaks only in the frame carries no weight; make him carry at least one real action in the main plot.
- **Separate singing by sheng and dan, and the refrain (合头)**: **one sheng and one dan are the main brain of the whole play and must appear in the second or third act**; **exceptions coexist**: 《邯郸梦》 and 《钧天乐》 (Music of Heaven) take the laosheng as the lead. **The words of a choral passage (合头) must be claimable by every character on stage** — a scene written as "each confides their own thoughts" must explicitly mark **split singing**, or in rehearsal it will inevitably be merged into chorus.
- **North–South mixed suites (南北合套)**: invented by the Yuan writer Shen He (沈和), defined as "tunes for a scene with rather many transitions" (suite patterns reference-taoshu.md §4). **《牡丹亭·圆驾》 is the supreme example**: **all ten Northern tunes go to Du Liniang (杜丽娘) alone, while the Southern tunes are distributed to everyone else, each with a refrain** — **the mixed suite is not a display of technique; it writes "one person against the crowd" into the prosody**. **Failure condition**: if the final scene divides right and wrong evenly among several people, the mixed suite will leave the protagonist unsupported; use instead alternating singing of the same tune.
- **The division of labour between Northern and Southern tunes — two parallel rules, not one alone**: **Northern tunes for the outsider** (scene 11 of 《桃花扇》, where Liu Jingting, 柳敬亭, storms the commander's gate; in 《牡丹亭》 the barbarian king, bandits, ghost judges, court, and imperial court use Northern suites, with one person singing throughout) · **use me when an outsider breaks into a scene where the situation is already set**; **Northern tunes for the master of the house** (scene 18 of 《桃花扇》 runs the other way; Wang Jilie's general rule: "for sheng, jing, and laosheng in tragic plays, Northern tunes are appropriate") · **use me when the dominant character must hold the stage with virile grief and indignation**. **Common criterion**: whether you want bone and density or long lyrical flow, and whether you want the crowd to answer in song. **Proportion**: chuanqi must be eight or nine parts Southern; **failure condition**: zaju is the reverse and may be entirely Northern.
- **Cross-scene mode echoes (unique to the qupai system; the banqiang system cannot do it)**: Liniang dies in the Southern Shang-mode "ying" suite, and 〈玩真〉 ("Appraising the Portrait"), a scene for Liu Sheng (柳生), still uses the same mode and the same tune group facing the painting. Self-check: before writing a new suite, check whether this mode has been used in the play and on whom. ⚠️ Only the three Northern suites and securely labelled examples can support this — the mode of the "ying" suite example is 〔provisional〕 (1.5).
- **Tang-assembled exit poems (集唐下场诗)**: each of the 55 scenes of 《牡丹亭》 ends with four lines assembled from Tang poems, with the Tang poet's name noted under each line; **they may be spoken by one person, divided among characters, or include a "chorus"**; **they both close the scene and hint at the next** (self-check: read the four lines alone — can you see what will happen in the next scene?); the last scene may break the rule with eight lines. **Kong Shangren objected**: "Tang-assembled lines are also a hackneyed convention. **Now I have made new poems throughout, with a beginning that has a starting point and an end that has a thread**" (prefatory rule 15) — **the two views stand side by side; follow the convention of the script in hand.**
- **Four colours and four qi (四色四气)**: in the "Outline" (纲领) of 《桃花扇》 the **axis of classification is function, not good and evil** — the four colours (正 upright / 间 intervening / 合 combining / 润 moistening) govern the separation-and-union line, the four qi (中 central / 戾 contrary / 馀 surplus / 煞 baleful) govern the rise-and-fall line; the two figures of the 经部 (jīngbù — the "classic" roster section) are frame characters and are listed in a separate section (full table reference-tizhi.md §11), **parallel to, not a substitute for, the three-dimensional characters of `sw-character-conflict`**. **Role assignment is itself an evaluation, but it is not a moral label**: "when the upright colour is insufficient, borrow from chou and jing" (prefatory rule 14); self-check: draw a table of "行当 (hángdang — role type)—character—good and evil"; if the three columns align perfectly, the assignment is lazy.

## 4. The Method of Tune-Filling

**4.1 What "filling" is.** **The score is a pattern book** — "**the clumsy cannot subtract a little, the skilful cannot add a little**," "**the older the better; approach the new even slightly, and a difference of a hair becomes an error of a thousand miles**" (Li Yu). **Li Yu's own tension must be spelled out for him**: this passage says the older the score the better, while the two passages on escaping convention say "change and it is new, do not change and it rots"; the boundary is that **metre (score, rhyme, character patterns) cannot change, while plot, comic business, and fine dialogue must change** (**this formulation is a note-taker's summary, not Li Yu's own words**). **Choosing a qupai = choosing rhythm, length, and performance form at once** (Luo Huaizhen) → the order of writing: **first fix the frame, then establish the idea, then arrange the tunes, and only at the end choose lines and form the piece** (Wu Mei, "above all avoid patching in, above all avoid making do"). **Self-check**: before writing the first line of lyrics, can you list the order of qupai for this act and what each tune carries? If not, it is not yet time to put pen to paper.

**4.2 The three levels: 前腔 / 换头 / 幺篇.** **Qianqiang (前腔)** = several tunes used in succession with **not one character of the line pattern changed**; **huantou (换头)** = changing the head of the preceding tune, adding or subtracting one or two characters or lines; in Northern tunes it is called **yao (幺)**. **Failure to distinguish them makes "huantou" read as "repeat once"** (modern new writing often writes "continue singing" without marking qianqiang, leaving the reader to judge by rhyme and line count). The three levels of amplitude, and the must-check list for same-name-different-tune and same-tune-different-mode, are in reference.md §3.

**4.3 Padding characters (衬字).** **Three hard rules for Southern tunes**: ① **add only where the beat pattern (板式, bǎnshì) is dense, and only at the beginning or middle of a line; never within the last three characters of a line or where the beat pattern is sparse**; ② **at most three characters per place**; ③ **empty words (function words) are preferable to full words**. **Northern padding is entirely unrestricted** (the beat is not fixed; beats may be added on padding) — **when writing a Northern suite, all three rules are lifted**. **Not one character may be added to a chouban tune (抽板曲, chōubǎnqǔ — a tune whose beats are drawn out) or to the first line of a coda**; **anything beyond eight or ten characters is by default padding and may not be given a solid beat** (Tang Xianzu piled on padding until "the singer had no place to phrase or pause," and after his text was revised "**the writing fell ten times below the original**"). **Before filling a Southern tune, first identify the beat pattern**: if the last beat of the upper line and the first beat of the lower line are far apart, not one character may be added at the start of the lower line.

**4.4 Wutou (务头 — the qupai-system version of the "key line"; four parties in parallel, four realizations of the same functional slot: "find that one line first").**

| Who | What wutou is | How to find it |
|---|---|---|
| **Li Yu** (a different reading, stage school) | "Like the eye in a game of go: with it the group lives, without it it dies" | Point to which line, which one or two characters, is the wutou; **if you cannot point to it, it is a dead tune** |
| **Wu Mei** (a tonal definition) | "**The place in the tune where the level, rising, and departing tones are strung together**"; in a seven-character line, characters 3, 4, and 5 must not share a tone | Mark the last line character by character for the four tones and yin/yang, and find where three sounds or two sounds join |
| **Wang Jilie** (adopts Wu Mei, supplies the look-up method) | Same as Wu Mei, adding "**it is mostly at the end of the tune or at a tight place in the middle**" | Look in 《北词广正谱》 (Beici Guangzheng Pu — The Expanded and Corrected Register of Northern Lyrics) for places noted "rising and departing may not be shifted," and in Southern scores for places noted "departing-rising is marvellous" |
| **Lu Jun** (banqiang system, cross-reference) | The key line — the line that embodies the theme, the mood, or a change in relationship | Write the key sung line first, and let it determine the rhyme |

Cross-reference section 2 of banqiang. Two additions: **at the wutou, rather let the four tones and yin/yang yield than let the sense of the text be incoherent** (the only place where metre may be sacrificed for meaning, **not to be generalized**); **the position of the wutou is designated in advance by the author**. ⚠️ **Wang Jilie's correction must be quoted along with it**: Kong Shangren's "'si' is the wutou" **conflates two different things** — "si" belongs to the zhi-shi rhyme category, and when sung the sound comes through the teeth; it has nothing to do with wutou.

**4.5 The forty prohibitions and the four tones.** The forty prohibitions (四十禁) come from 《曲律》 (Rules of Song) by Wang Jide (王骥德), with Wu Mei's commentary (full table reference-jinji.md §12); take only the prosodic layer, **items 1–16 and 35–40**; **items 17–34 → `sw-dialogue`**. **Wu Mei's general note must be quoted together**: "**if you cannot keep them all, examine and remove the worst**," "**the law is extremely harsh**" → **the forty prohibitions are a health-check sheet, not an admission threshold**; if you cannot run all of them, run at least six: repeated rhyme, rhyme encroachment, an awkward throat, excessive padding, piled-up learning, and random use of modes. **Four rules for the four tones** (details and the six classes of rhyme endings in reference-taoshu.md §6): ① **never use four level, four rising, four departing, or four entering tones in a row**; ② **departing-rising in succession is better than rising-departing** → use the rising tone for low-pitched lines and the departing tone for high-pitched ones; ③ **the entering tone is "licorice among medicines"** — in Southern tunes the entering tone has its own proper sound and may serve as level, rising, or departing; **in Northern tunes the entering tone has no proper sound and has already been distributed among the three tones, so it may not be borrowed**; and **a Southern tune that uses entering-tone rhyme must use entering-tone rhyme throughout** (the only exceptions are the jia-ma and che-she rhyme categories); ④ self-check: sing it and listen whether it has turned into another word ("guang" heard as "kuang"); if it has changed, change the word, not the tune. **Within the six classes of rhyme endings, borrowing is often permissible; across classes it is never allowed** — mixing zhen-wen, geng-qing, and qin-xun is always wrong (counter-example: the 〔朝元歌〕 in 《玉簪记·琴挑》 "The Jade Hairpin: Flirting at the Qin," of which "**not one person can sing it to the mark**").

**4.6 Line pattern, beat pattern, and coda.** **The line pattern determines the beat pattern; the meaning of the text does not**: for a seven-character line, first decide whether it is **four-three** or **three-four**; self-check: read it through with the beats, and **the latter half after the break must stand as a phrase by itself** (in 《桃花扇·传歌》 "Teaching the Song," "配他公子千金体" ["to match his noble son's thousand-gold form"] breaks as "子千金体" ["son's thousand-gold form"], a counter-example, for which Wu Mei judged it "**fine words but no fine tune**"). **Distinguishing base characters from padding and marking phrase breaks is the filler's responsibility, not the composer's**. **The coda** always uses **twelve beats**, and **the eleven named patterns are used according to the mode of the suite; do not scramble the order** (reference-taoshu.md §7); the three cases in which the **coda may be omitted**, and "**随煞**" (suíshà — a trailing coda), are in reference-taoshu.md §7. **The last line must carry the most brilliant line of the whole tune.** **Keeping the law is dead; filling is alive**: it is permissible for a qianqiang or yao to differ from the preceding tune in the four tones, **but the places shifted must be supported by an example from an ancient work**. ⚠️ The "**table of level-and-oblique patterns for Southern codas**" in 《顾曲麈谈》 **may not be used as evidence** — two OCR versions differ greatly in the strings of level and oblique characters; **mark it "to be checked against the paper edition"**; the table of eleven named patterns (read from an image) may be used.

**4.7 The checking loop: sing by the beat.** **Each time you finish one tune, sing it once according to the score; "if there is the slightest awkward character, revise it at once"** (Kong Shangren, "Postscript to The Peach Blossom Fan"; he sought out veteran actors who thoroughly understood the suites). Li Yu gives the same rule from the other side: rising-tone characters **are lowest when sung but higher when spoken**; the author writes by reading, not singing, so "what reads high sings low" — this is the common disease of "**good for the study, bad for the stage**." → **When finished, someone must sing it through; reading aloud is not enough.** This occupies the same functional slot as the "examining the tune" step in both the qupai and banqiang systems.

## 5. Modes and Suites

**5.1 How far the playwright needs to understand this.** Wu Mei locks it down in one sentence: "**A mode is what fixes the pitch of the instruments' pipe.**" He also sets the depth: "**Tune and temperament are two different things**"; he himself excludes pitch-pipe theory, and says the correspondence between mode names and absolute pitch "**no one knows the reason for**." → **Boundary rule: the playwright's knowledge of modes stops at "pipe pitch + suite construction."** If asked about the generation of pitches by eighths, the twelve pitch pipes, or "why is Zhenggong not Huangzhonggong," answer: "That is temperament theory, not what tune-filling needs; Wu Mei also said it is unintelligible," and pull the question back to flute pitch and suite construction.

**5.2 Table of the seventeen modes and their affects.** The order is fixed: **first determine the play's separations, unions, sorrows, and joys → then determine the mode → then choose qupai within that mode to form a suite**; do not first pick pleasant-sounding tunes and then patch them together.

Examples of Yuan writers observing this law: **tragedy uses Nanlü and Shang; comedy uses Huangzhong and Xianlü; heroes and warriors sing Zhenggong; mockery and ridicule sing Yuediao.**)

**A name is not the same as having usable qupai**: three modes are lost in the North, and Shangjiao is lost even in the South; Wang Jilie further reduces the list to **the nine commonly used modes: Xianlü, Nanlü, Huangzhong, Zhonglü, Zhenggong, Dashi, Shang, Yue, and Shuangdiao**. Self-check: if you use a mode outside the nine, first confirm that it really has enough qupai to form a suite. **Failure condition**: short scenes, transitions, and patchwork arias may borrow modes with few tunes. The full table of qupai in the six gong and eleven diao, and the twenty-one rhyme categories of qu rhyme, are in reference.md §1.

**5.3 One suite, one mode; and three accepted ways of crossing the boundary.** If the tunes before and after in one suite are in different modes, that is **leaving the mode / mode encroachment (出宫／犯调)**, which prosody does not allow ("though the lyric text be fine, it cannot be set to pipe and string"). **Mode borrowing (借宫, Northern tunes) may only follow established Yuan practice and may not be pieced together at will** (self-check: can you name the Yuan work from which this borrowed-mode suite comes? If not, it is "painting a tiger"). **Five rules for patchwork arias (集曲, Southern tunes)**: ① borrow from the same mode or from another mode with the **same flute pitch**; ② **modes whose flute pitches differ sharply must never be joined**; ③ the first few lines must use the first few lines of the original complete tune (正曲); ④ the last few lines must use the last few lines of the original complete tune, and middle lines must use middle lines; ⑤ **reversing the order is never allowed**; **the mode of a patchwork aria = the mode of the tune whose opening lines it uses** (self-check: can you say which tunes are borrowed and which flute pitch they share? If not, do not use it). **General rule of mode encroachment: only modes with the same pipe pitch may encroach** (flute-pitch to mode table reference.md §2); **a parallel reservation**: Wu Mei reserves judgment on patchwork arias and does not encourage them; Wang Jide's claim that "Xianlü may interchange with Shuangdiao" was explicitly judged by Wu Mei as "not quite fitting" — **do not copy Wang's claim**. **Within one act you may change mode and rhyme, but only because the staging changes and the plot turns** (positive example: 《长生殿·定情》 "Plighting the Troth," where Dashi = the consort's investiture banquet and Yuediao = intimate words in the inner palace; negative example: 《琵琶记·吃糠》 "Eating Husks," which "changes mode without reason"); **self-check: what happened on stage at the moment of the mode change? If you cannot answer, do not change.**

**5.4 Laying out the suite.** **The one hard measure for Southern suites: tunes with added beats (赠板, slow tunes) come first, tunes without added beats (fast tunes) come after, and those where added beats are optional may go either before or after** (the three grades of added beats and the table of three tune classes are in reference-taoshu.md §5). **The entrance aria (引子) comes at the entrance, the coda at the conclusion**; at most three per act; **jing and chou do not use an entrance aria when they come on** (details reference-taoshu.md §5). **Choosing a suite by the six categories of tune feeling**: **joy / excursion / grief / quiet resentment / action / pouring out feeling**, plus four more: ordinary, martial, transition, and quiet (itemized lists reference-taoshu.md §4); **the long "pouring out feeling" suite is reserved for the play's main act and is best sung by sheng and dan** — self-check: **does the longest pouring-out suite fall on the act with the greatest value turn?** (isomorphic with the value turn in `sw-scene-craft`). **Failure condition**: the six categories are an entrance, not a cage. **"Zhuàn" (赚, also called 不是路 bùshìlù)** is used at the moment of a staging change as a joint; **in the whole play it is best confined to one act, and no more than two tunes in that act**. **A "cold" tune name = a tune no one has fixed beats for = unperformable**; self-check: before choosing a tune, check whether 《南词定律》 (Nanci Dinglü — The Fixed Rules of Southern Lyrics) or 《九宫大成》 (Jiugong Dacheng — The Great Compendium of the Nine Modes) has its beat pattern (**failure condition**: a veteran who sets new tunes himself is not bound).

## 6. Staging and Plot

- **Separate the two words first**: "**Sorrow, joy, parting, and reunion are called plot; the entrances and exits of the performers are called staging (排场).**" — **Staging is an account to be settled at the moment of filling lyrics, not the director's business** (the same layer as the "technical structure" of Fan Junhong [范钧宏]; cross-reference banqiang).
- **The law of alternating staging (common to both systems)**: "If the first act is sung by sheng and the second by dan, the third must use **a broad-voice role (阔口, kuòkǒu) or a lively scene with the whole company**; after two or three acts of slow tunes and long suites, there must be a **transitional short scene or a comic scene for chou and jing**." Self-check: arrange the act list by "lead singer / nature of tune feeling" and see whether three acts in a row share the same character and the same nature. **Failure condition**: for plays under ten acts, only keep "no two consecutive acts with the same lead singer." The sample is the full staging table of all fifty acts of 《长生殿》 (reference-tizhi.md §8).
- **Transitional short scenes may not be omitted**: "**without them the plot does not connect and the threads do not join**," and **short, fast tunes must be used**. Self-check: is there a transition between two large scenes? Does the transition mistakenly use a slow tune with added beats?
- **Hot and cold temper each other**: Li Yu's judgment is the reverse — "**chuanqi has no hot or cold; the only fear is that it does not fit human feeling**"; **a cold scene is not unperformable, it has simply not been polished**.
- **Jing and chou are structural members (two positions in parallel, not merged)**: **Wu Mei** — "**the more perilous the foil of jing and chou, the more extraordinary the realm of the words**"; even a little connecting tune must "fit thread to needle" (in 《帝女花》 [The Princess of the Flower] the jing and chou have only one act, which "seems rather cold"). **Li Yu** — "**comic business is the ginseng broth of the playgoer**," its position is rhythm management; two more rules: **comic business is not the exclusive property of the clown**, and **every piece of comic business must have a trigger in the scene** (self-check: cut any that cannot point to its trigger).

## 7. The Interface from Spoken Dialogue to Song

- **The first line of a song must have support in the preceding spoken line** (the most practicable item in the whole annotated corpus). Two examples from 《牡丹亭·惊梦》 "The Peony Pavilion: The Interrupted Dream": first the spoken "**What lovely weather**," then 〔步步娇〕; first the spoken "**If I had not come to the garden, how would I know spring is this beautiful**," then 〔皂罗袍〕 "So the whole garden is ablaze with purple and red." **Self-check**: circle the connective in the first line of the sung passage ("so," "how," "yet"), and look for its support in the preceding spoken line; if you cannot find it, the song starts out of thin air. **Mark this as common to both systems** — the same rule appears independently three times in 《朝阳沟》 (Chaoyang Village) and the seven Yue operas; in banqiang terms, "is the line before each sung passage an address term?" See banqiang.
- **The three things spoken dialogue "cannot fail to do well"**: ① when several people sing in one act, **even the joins must be vivid**; ② Kunqu tunes have few characters and long phrases, so **spoken dialogue must spare the singer's labour**; ③ Southern lyrics are "called one suite but are in fact **one tune, one idea**," and **the transitions all lie in the spoken dialogue**. The difference in hearing: when singing, strings and pipes chatter, and "**what is ten parts marvellous shows only seven; but in spoken dialogue every word and every phrase is clear to all**." Li Yu's self-check method is "**pen in hand, but mouth on the stage**"; self-check: if it is transparent on the printed page but muddled on stage, you have not "used your mouth for the actor and your ear for the listener."

## 8. Bangqiang (the Textual Device of High-Pitched Opera)

**Bangqiang (帮腔)**: short lines taken up by the stage band or a backstage chorus in high-pitched opera (Sichuan opera's high-pitched style, the Yiyang tune line). **The banqiang system has no such device**, which is why it gets its own section in a qupai-system text. The evidence is the nine bangqiang passages in Wei Minglun and Nan Guo's 《巴山秀才》 (The Bashan Scholar) (nine drafts, 1983; Sichuan opera high-pitched style).

- **It is treated as a character in the script**: the word "帮腔" is written at the head of the line, in the same position as a character's name.
- **Four functions; choose only one at each place**: **narration** (stating a fact the character cannot conveniently or will not say — the scholar suspects he is dead, and the chorus announces for him "the survivor who came through the great calamity") / **commentary** (exposing, settling accounts — Nichang sings "singing softly —" and the chorus cuts in "put on a smiling face!") / **ventriloquial participation** (speaking for the character, even deciding for him) / **emotional amplification** ("there is no inn on the road to the Yellow Springs," answered by "where will you stay, with three thousand wronged souls of Bashan?" — the parallelism is complete, while the subject shifts from "I" to "three thousand souls"). There are also two purely structural uses: **starting the song** and **taking up the next line**. Self-check: give each place a function label; cut any that cannot be labelled; if you switch functions within one passage, the audience no longer knows whose voice this is.
- **Three formal laws**: **extremely short** (six of the nine places are only 3–7 characters) — **bangqiang never carries a whole passage of lyric**; **its position is always where "the character cannot go on singing"**; **it can execute a change of tune** (starting 〔刮骨令〕 anew, "turning to the refrain of 〔孝顺歌〕," with the character following). Self-check: if a bangqiang passage exceeds one seven-character line, ask "why is this line not given to the character to sing?"
- **The lone exception: a character hears the bangqiang and adopts it.** The chorus sings "Take your wife to Chengdu!", and Meng Dengke answers in speech, "**Hey, that was a fine bit of help!**" and immediately does so → bangqiang turns from "the voice of the crowd" into "the voice of fate." **At most once per play**, and it must be placed at the point where the character has nowhere left to turn; use it a second time and it degrades into an ordinary supporting role.

## 9. Adaptation and Rewriting

- **Li Yu's "changing the tune" and the body/appearance distinction**: "**Keep the body, change the appearance**" (仍其体质，变其丰姿). **Body = the lyrics and the large plot structure, unchanged**; **appearance = comic business and fine dialogue, must be changed**. **Look for additions first among the play's existing characters; only add a person if you cannot find one** (Little Er in 《琵琶记》, Caiping in 《明珠记》, The Pearl Tale). **Failure condition**: Li Yu's motive for supplementing 《琵琶记》 was Ming–Qing chastity ideology; **the method is usable, the motive is not to be copied**.
- **Cutting length to shortness (the extensible-and-contractible script method)**: "**Rather than long and unfinished, better short with an ending**" (与其长而不终，无宁短而有尾): **mark the acts whose plot can be spared with a separate code**; the key is **the method of adding within cutting** — in the following act after the cut, add a few lines to point out that plot, or plant a hook for it in the preceding act.
- **Turning an old play into a performable version: Wang Jilie's abridged revision of 《紫钗记》 (The Purple Hairpin)**. First the diagnosis — "**the staging of all four dreams of Yuming lacks consideration**... The Purple Hairpin has the least fitting staging of all, and **truly cannot be performed**"; then **select fourteen acts and abridge them**, recording item by item which tunes were cut and why (too many slow tunes, one person cannot sing it all, the plot can be spared). **This is the only first-hand work record of "adapting an old play into a performable version"**, to be compared with the four questions of adaptation in `sw-format-adaptation`.
- **One action, three outcomes** (Wu Mei's four adapted scripts): the 《墨憨斋定本》 (Mohan Studio Definitive Edition) of Feng Menglong (冯梦龙) "**again carefully fixed the beat pattern for every tune**" → success; the adaptation by Sao Yinsheng (骚隐生) of 《不伏老》 (Refusing to Admit Old Age) from Northern to Southern → success; 《南西厢》 (The Southern Romance of the Western Chamber) from Northern to Southern → failure (when the suite in the original mode could not be joined, he **borrowed "tunes of similar line pattern" from other modes and filled at will**); the adaptation by Zang Jinshu (臧晋叔) of the Four Dreams to fit the metre → another kind of failure ("**attending only to prosody, paying no attention to the words at all**... **turning gold into iron**"). **Three conclusions**: ① **in adapting a Northern play to a Southern version, you cannot hunt line by line for "tunes of similar line pattern" and force-fill them**; if you adapt, rearrange mode—suite pattern—beat pattern according to the target system; ② **an adapted script must fix the beat patterns along with the text**; ③ **"fitting the metre" and "being alive" are two independent checks; neither may eat the other**. **When words and music conflict, change the qupai, not the words** — Niu Shaoya (钮少雅), in 《格正还魂记词调》 ("Corrected Tune Patterns for The Peony Pavilion"), "changed to patchwork arias... the original text still not a single character altered"; self-check: **if you change more characters than tunes, the direction is wrong**; **failure condition**: when the phrasing itself is wrong, you must go back and change the phrasing of the words.
- **Tang Xianzu's four moves in adapting the storybook** (against the source storybook 《杜丽娘慕色还魂》 Du Liniang Yearns for Love and Returns to Life): ① **delete relatives** to isolate the protagonist; ② **change the lawful into the unlawful** (opening the grave, originally presided over by Prefect Liu, becomes Liu Sheng's private digging, which violates the Great Ming Code's "opening a coffin and seeing the corpse: beheaded, principals and accessories alike") — the conflict of the whole second half follows from this; ③ **change "love at first sight" into "a tryst in a dream + a second search for the dream"**; ④ **keep the original's good lines unchanged in the spoken dialogue and change only the lyrics** (in the poem inscribed on the portrait only one character is changed: "not beside the plum, make it beside the willow" → "**not beside the plum, but beside the willow**"). Self-check: ask of each change, "did this change create a new opponent or a new legal risk?"; verbal polish with no structural change does not count as adaptation.

Continues into section 12 of banqiang (organizing traditional material) and `sw-format-adaptation`.

## 10. Modern New Qupai-System Writing

**This section stands parallel to "the method of tune-filling / modes and suites," not subordinate to it.** The practice of three writers **directly conflicts with Wu Mei and Wang Jilie's "filling must follow the score"; the ancient and modern lines are kept side by side, neither merged nor adjudicated.**

- **Weng Ouhong (new Kunqu writing from a Peking-opera background)**: 《大闹天宫》 (Havoc in Heaven) and 《高亮赶水》 (Gaoliang Drives the Waters) are Kunqu in style throughout, yet **use no suites; instead they select tunes in a mosaic of "broken gold and shattered jade,"** joined by modulation and transposition, on the grounds that "**suites are too constraining**."
- **Wei Minglun and Nan Guo's 《巴山秀才》 (nine drafts, 1983; Sichuan opera high-pitched style): four answers**: ① **no suites** — across the play's eight scenes **there is not a single mode label**, and the same tune is repeated across scenes and characters (〔红衲袄〕 is assigned to a blacksmith, a corrupt official, and a favourite concubine); the only trace of suite construction is "continuous singing." ② **select tunes in a mosaic, on the basis of emotion and the scale of the passage** (tune-to-emotion table reference-jinji.md §13) — **the criterion**: what can be explained is "how long this emotion lasts, what key it is in"; what cannot be explained is "what mode the previous tune was in." ③ **keep half the metre**: **one rhyme throughout each passage, strictly** (the 24 lines of 〔香罗带〕 stay in the you-qiu rhyme), while **character count and tonal pattern are entirely free**. ④ **do not invent tune names, but expand the tune-name column into a "singing instruction column"** — it may specify the vocal style ("Kunqu"), another genre's metrical pattern (Peking opera's "xipi erliu"), or the delivery ("rhyme speech," yùnbái).
- **Luo Huaizhen's 《班昭》 (Ban Zhao) takes the opposite road**: **written in modern vernacular, "yet I have not broken the rules in a single character"**; the tones of characters in odd positions, the rhymes at line ends, and the distribution of the entering tone into the three tones are all followed; the price is that "**sometimes it takes two days to fill a single qupai**."
- **Common ground and divergence**: all three treat modes not as a tonal system but as an index of emotion and scale; **the divergence is not adjudicated** — Weng and Wei loosen suites and line patterns, while Luo holds the line patterns and loosens the register. **Two opera genres, two authors, two eras (Weng and Wei) independently reach the same conclusion: modern new plays do not use suites**; yet 《班昭》 is equally valid. **How to choose**: does the script need to enter Kunqu's existing vocal system? If yes — take Luo's road (fill by the score, budget the hours); if no, and the genre has a living qupai tune repertoire (Sichuan opera's high-pitched style) — take Weng and Wei's road (broken gold and shattered jade, strict rhyme and loose character count).
- **Negative criterion: "fake youth Kunqu"** — "its essence is **that adolescent young lives convey the aged breath of late-Ming literati and loyalists of the old dynasty**" (luo-v6 p0109). Self-check: in a new script, are the words spoken by the young characters their own, or the words of literati three hundred years ago?

## 11. Famous Works Also Break the Rules

**There is only one way to handle them: "Learn their writing, but do not imitate their method"** (但当学其文，不可效其法 — Wu Mei on the Four Dreams of Yuming). Those named and the faults alleged: **《牡丹亭》 / the Four Dreams** (too many padding characters to phrase; 〈冥誓〉 "The Infernal Oath" leaves its mode and encroaches; all four dreams' staging lacks consideration; level, rising, and departing rhymes mixed with entering tone); **《桃花扇》** (many tonal faults, padding characters not well placed; 〈传歌〉 "Teaching the Song" has a faulty line pattern; 〈哭坛〉 "Weeping at the Altar" substitutes the Southern 〔朝天子〕 for the Northern, "**quite losing the point of the North–South interlocked suite**"; "fine words but no fine tune"); **《南西厢》** (its suite patterns are the least observance-worthy, see section 9; Li Yu judged it "first in merit and first in crime"); **《琵琶记》** (zhi-shi and yu-mu not distinguished, ge-luo and jia-ma used together; inverted characters and forced rhymes; **the last eight acts are not the work of Gao Ming (高明)**); **《拜月亭》** (many rare tunes with no fixed beats; with 《琵琶记》 it is the source of the Southern "**not seeking the mode or tuning the key**"); **《玉簪记·琴挑》** (mixes the geng-ting / zhen-wen / qin-xun rhyme categories); **《金线池》** (act four has no coda — the third exit for omitting a coda). The exact wording and page numbers for each item are in reference-jinji.md §14.

**Two ways to use this**: ① before citing a famous passage as a model, check whether it is on this list; if it is, avoid the fault named; ② **"former scholars labelled it this way" is not evidence** (1.5).

**Two disagreements that must be kept side by side**: Wang Jilie rebuts Jin Shengtan's rewriting of 《西厢》 — "**Jin's exclusive devotion to plain description is precisely the native colour of the Yuan writers**... **he is in truth a great calamity for The Western Chamber**"; Wu Mei and Wang Jilie both criticize 《桃花扇》, yet Kong Shangren's prefatory rules have their own system (long scenes of eight tunes, opposition to Tang-assembled lines, full spoken dialogue) — **the author's self-imposed rules and the prosodists' verdict are two coordinate systems; write both.**

## 12. Medium Boundaries: The Lines Specific to the Qupai System

| Item | Qupai system | Banqiang system |
|---|---|---|
| **The right to sing** | In zaju one person sings all the tunes of an act, exclusively; **the right to sing = the right to be the protagonist** | Role types sing in turn; anyone may sing |
| **Mode echoes** | May use the same mode and tune group for cross-scene echoes | Cannot — metrical patterns are not assigned to modes by emotion |
| **Tune names** | **Generally do not indicate content**, and some are unintelligible dialect names | Metrical-pattern names directly indicate speed and emotion |
| **One rhyme per passage** | One rhyme throughout each act or scene, no repetition across the four acts; repeated rhymes within a suite are allowed (stage song) | One rhyme throughout a passage; the whole play may change rhyme category scene by scene |
| **Bangqiang** | A presence unique to high-pitched opera; it can comment, speak for characters, and change tunes | None; the same functional slot is the refrain (合头) or offstage singing (幕内唱) |

The general-layer choices (the four-part pattern 起承转合 can be used; BS2 page numbers cannot be used; 程式 (chéngshì — performance conventions) is not setup; late entrance and early exit do not apply) are the same as in the banqiang system; see section 0.2 of banqiang. One more line: **"one person, one event" (一人一事) belongs to the Li Yu–qupai tradition**; the banqiang system speaks of the same thing as "vertical concentration" (纵的集中); each side uses its own term.

## 13. Diagnostic Checklist, Workflow, Training

**13.1 Diagnostic checklist**

1. Stage or study? If you cannot answer, the table in 1.4 cannot be used.
2. Have you read it twice — once only for staging and key scenes, once only for lyrics and modes (→ 1)?
3. Zaju: are the four acts' modes four different ones? Is act one Xianlü and act four Shuangdiao (→ 2)?
4. Zaju: copy the role markers into one column — has a second name appeared within the four acts? Is the subject of the "name" line the lead singer (→ 2)?
5. Chuanqi: in which scene does the protagonist appear? If the previous scene had sheng as protagonist, is the next scene sheng again (→ 3)?
6. Copy the rhyme words of each act into one column — has another rhyme category slipped in (→ 2, 4)?
7. Can you point to the wutou of each tune — which line, which one or two characters? If not, it is a dead tune (→ 4). In Southern tunes, are the padding characters all in dense-beat places, at the start or middle of lines, and never more than three per place (→ 4)?
8. Do all the qupai of a suite fall in the same mode? If they cross modes, can you say which flute pitch they share? What happened on stage at the moment of the mode change (→ 5)?
9. Arrange the act list by "lead singer / nature of tune feeling" — are there three consecutive acts with the same character and the same nature? Is there a transition between two large scenes (→ 6)?
10. Does the longest pouring-out suite fall on the act with the greatest value turn (→ 5, 6)?
11. Can the connective at the start of each tune find support in the preceding spoken line (→ 7)?
12. Can each bangqiang passage be given a function label? Does it exceed one seven-character line (→ 8)?
13. In an adaptation: do the characters changed outnumber the tunes changed? If so, the direction is wrong (→ 9).
14. Modern new writing: are you taking "fill by the score" or "broken gold and shattered jade"? Have you budgeted separately for the cost of each road (→ 10)?
15. Is the famous passage you cite as a model on the list in section 11? Is the last line of the coda the most firmly grounded line in the whole suite? Have you found someone to sing each tune by the beat after finishing it?

**13.2 Workflow**

1. **Ask the two switches first**: stage or study (1.4); zaju or chuanqi (2 vs. 3). Modern new writing goes through section 10 separately.
2. **Fix the main brain**: compress it into "one person + one event in four characters," then verify each subplot against it (6).
3. **Lay out the scene/act order**: fix the length (chuanqi no more than forty scenes; zaju four acts and one wedge), make an act list marking the category of tune feeling, the lead singer, and hot and cold; run the alternation law and the equalization of labour (3, 6).
4. **For each act: fix the emotion → fix the mode → take a suite of qupai in that mode** (5.2, 5.3); Southern suites are arranged slow / medium / fast (5.4), Northern suites follow the head and tail of the suite tables (2).
5. **Write the spoken dialogue and stage directions (科介)**, writing out the interface of each tune (the last line of speech) first (7); then **fill the lyrics** — consult the score for character count, line pattern, and the four tones, consult the rhyme book for the rhyme category, designate the wutou first and then set the brilliant phrase (4.4), and handle padding characters separately for North and South (4.3).
6. **Sing by the beat** (4.7) → **run the forty prohibitions and remove the worst six** (4.5) → run 13.1. If you need long and short versions before delivery, mark the code according to the "adding within cutting" method of section 9.

**13.3 Training**

- **Fill one tune against the original pattern**: mark 〔下山虎〕 (from 《幽闺记》) character by character for the four tones, then fill new content slot by slot; set old and new side by side, and refill if a single tone differs. "The harder it is, the easier it becomes good."
- **Write the same passage both ways**: the same scene, once filled by the score (Luo Huaizhen style), once in broken gold and shattered jade (Wei Minglun style), and compare the hours and performability (10).
