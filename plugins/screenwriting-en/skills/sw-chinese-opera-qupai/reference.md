## §1 Complete Table of Northern and Southern Qupai under the Six Gong and Eleven Diao · Table of the Twenty-One Qu Rhyme Categories

### 1a Tunes Belonging to the Six Gong and Eleven Diao (Seventeen Groups plus Xianlü Entering Shuang Diao)

**Source**: the table appended to Wu Mei's 《顾曲麈谈》 (*Random Talks on Opera*), Chapter 1, "On the Original Qu," Section 1, "On Gongdiao"; scan pages p0118–p0134 (notes `wu-mei-quxue-tonglun/03-guqu-gongdiao-qupai-biao.md`).

Wu Mei explains his purpose in making the table: "I set up a standard for recent writers of lyrics, and have therefore taken the gongdiao to which each tune belongs and listed them in detail below." "One need only link tunes into suites within their own gongdiao and arrange them as the ancients already did, and the fault of leaving the mode or modulating improperly will naturally not arise."

**How to read the table** (Wu Mei's original conventions): southern qu is divided into two classes, **yinzi (引子 — opening arias, used on entrance, sometimes accompanied by the flute and sometimes not, and very different from the tunes proper)** and **guoqu (过曲 — through-arias)**; northern qu makes no such division. The small notes in parentheses are Wu Mei's own (mostly distinctions of the "same title as another mode but a different tune" kind, and the key to avoiding misapplication). **Starting from Huangzhong gong, no southern-qu composite arias (集曲, jíqǔ — a new aria assembled from lines of several tunes) are recorded.**

**How to use the table (Wu Mei's own words plus a self-check)**
> "As listed above, the tunes belonging to the six gong and eleven diao are as clear as eyebrows on a face; **one need only link them into suites within their own gongdiao and arrange them as the ancients already did, and the fault of leaving the mode or modulating improperly will naturally not arise.**"
1. First fix the emotional character of the scene (parting or reunion, sorrow or joy) → choose the gongdiao → **take qupai only from that gongdiao's own box**.
2. To cross modes, first consult the flute-key table in §2: two gongdiao may modulate into each other only if they share a flute key; borrowing a mode (借宫, jiègōng) must copy a Yuan suite exactly.
3. When a tune title recurs, first read the small note in parentheses, then consult the mandatory checklist in §3.
4. The three diao marked "none in north or south" in the table — Xiezhi, Gong, and Jiao — together with Daogong and Gaoping, marked "no southern qu", **must not be used as the gongdiao of a suite**.

Those who write lyrics should use these rhymes: **fill in lines according to the register, and select rhymes by keeping to the category**, and they will then avoid transgressing the rules."

**Four differences showing why qu rhymes cannot use ci rhymes (p0135 area)**: (1) in ci, 支思 and 齐微 are merged; in qu they are separate; (2) in ci, the three rhymes 寒山, 桓欢, and 先天 are merged; in qu they are separate; (3) in ci, 家麻 and 车遮 are merged; in qu they are separate; (4) in ci, 监咸 and 廉纤 are merged; in qu they are separate. Add one point about the entering tone: "The entering-tone rhymes used in ci sometimes harmonize into the three tones and sometimes stand alone as entering tone, so **one must never follow the rule of assigning the entering tone to the three tones**... This is why writers of qu must never use ci rhymes."

**A fallback (a very practical one)**: "If a writer of qu cannot for the moment distinguish the qu rhymes, he may as well keep carefully to the *Jiyun*'s own distinctions between separate and shared use; this is far better than inventing. **Anyone who uses ci rhymes will inevitably transgress the rules.**" Worked example: qu rhyme category 1, the 东同 rhyme = *Jiyun* level tone 1 东, 2 冬, 3 钟 + rising tone 1 董, 2 肿 + departing tone 1 送, 2 宋, 3 用.

**Where it is loose and where it is strict** (p0135 area): loose — 江阳 is treated as one, 庚亭 is not divided, and level, rising, and departing tones are used together; within one aria, level and oblique rhymes may be interleaved. Strict — the three rhymes 支时 / 齐微 / 归回 "each keep their own boundaries and may not be borrowed for one another"; 居鱼 and 苏模 were interchangeable for ci writers but must not be mixed in qu (Wu Mei's original note: "In qu they were formerly also not distinguished; **the distinction began with 李笠翁 (Li Liweng)**"); 寒山 and 欢桓 versus 天田, and 监咸 versus 纤廉, "admit even less accommodation". **Zhenwen uses the -n (抵颚, "palate-touching") ending, Gengting the nasal (鼻音) ending, and Qinxun the closed-mouth (闭口) ending; these three sounds are kept strictly apart** (for the method of closing sounds and assigning them to categories, see reference-taoshu.md §6).

**Wang Jilie's other rhyme table (recorded, not transcribed)**: the character table of twenty-one qu rhyme categories that Wang Jilie compiled himself, in 《螾庐曲谈》 (*Talks on Opera from the Yǐnlú Studio*), volume 1, chapter 3 (p0042–p0098); under each category, characters are listed in columns by yin level / yang level / yin rising / yang rising / yin departing / yang departing / entering tone (as level, as rising, as departing), with fanqie (反切, fǎnqiè — traditional two-character spelling) and variant readings noted for each character; "over seven thousand characters are listed". The notes checked only category 1, 东同 (p0042), category 3, 支时 (p0048), category 7, 苏模 (p0060), and the last category, 廉纤 (p0098); the remaining categories **are not transcribed one by one and are marked "—"**; when needed, check the original book by page.

## §2 Conversion Table for the Seven Flute Keys · Flute-Key-to-Gongdiao Concordance (Wang Jilie's and Wu Mei's Versions Side by Side)

> ⚠️ **This section serves only as the basis for the modulation lookup table. Flute-key conversion belongs to the musical layer; this table is only for checking gongdiao** — that is, "whether two gongdiao share the same flute key, and whether one can borrow a mode or make a composite aria." Do not use it to discuss pitch height, pitch pipes, or key level itself; Wu Mei himself said that the correspondence between gongdiao names and pitch height "one does not know what it is based on" and "is quite inexplicable" (《曲学通论》 *General Treatise on Qu Studies*, Chapter 2, p0028).

### 2a Conversion of the Seven Flute Keys against Xiaogong diao (Transcribed Exactly from Wu Mei)

**Source**: Wu Mei's 《顾曲麈谈》 (*Random Talks on Opera*), Chapter 1, "On the Original Qu," Section 1; scan pages p0116–p0134 (notes `wu-mei-quxue-tonglun/02-guqu-lun-gongdiao.md`, section 2).

Baseline: the di (笛, dí — the Chinese transverse flute) has six holes and seven notes in all. **Treat the first hole as the gongche note gong (工), the second as che (尺), the third as shang (上), the fourth as yi (乙), the fifth as si (四), the sixth as he (合), and stop the second and third holes together as fan (凡) — this is Xiaogong diao (小工调).**

| Key name | Conversion rule (take a given syllable of Xiaogong diao as gong) | Full correspondence |
|---|---|---|
| Xiaogong diao (小工调) | Baseline | hole 1 gong, hole 2 che, hole 3 shang, hole 4 yi, hole 5 si, hole 6 he; holes 2 and 3 stopped together as fan |
| Fanzidiao (凡字调) | take **fan** of Xiaogong diao as gong | fan → gong, gong → che, che → shang, shang → yi, yi → si, si → he, he → fan |
| Liuzidiao (六字调) | take **liu** of Xiaogong diao as gong | liu → gong, fan → che, gong → shang, che → yi, shang → si, yi → he, si → fan |
| Zhenggong diao (正工调) | take **wu** of Xiaogong diao as gong | wu → gong, liu → che, fan → shang, gong → yi, che → si, shang → he, yi → fan |
| Yizi diao (乙字调) | take **yi** of Xiaogong diao as gong | yi → gong, wu → che, liu → shang, fan → yi, gong → si, che → he, shang → fan |
| Chizidiao (尺字调) | take **che** of Xiaogong diao as gong | che → gong, shang → che, yi → shang, si → yi, he → si, fan → he, gong → fan |
| Shangzidiao (上字调) | take **shang** of Xiaogong diao as gong | shang → gong, yi → che, si → shang, he → yi, fan → si, gong → he, che → fan |

> "The di has six holes, yet seven keys are used, so each syllable can serve as gong; this is the surviving sense of the ancients' **mutual rotation of modes (还相为宫)**."

**Differences between the two versions** (presented side by side, not adjudicated): (1) Wu Mei groups Yudiao under Gaoping ("southern lyrics are written as Yudiao"), while Wang Jilie lists Daogong and Yudiao together in one row; (2) Wu Mei gives Xianlü three keys — Xiaogong diao, Chizidiao, and Fanzidiao — while Wang Jilie gives only Xiaogong diao and Chizidiao, noting separately that northern qu may use Zhenggong diao; (3) for Shang diao, Wu Mei gives Liuzidiao and Shangzidiao, while Wang Jilie gives Liuzidiao, Fanzidiao, and Xiaogong diao and adds "determined by whether the qupai's melody lies high or low" — **when modulating, the safest choice is a pair that shares the same key in both tables**.

**Why a playwright should read this table (Wang Jilie, p0130, original text)**:
> "Every tune in a gongdiao has a fixed flute key, and every qupai has a fixed metrical pattern and a fixed melodic character, **which the composer cannot but know**. For in northern qu the borrowing of modes, and in southern qu the making of composite arias, **should always join tunes from gongdiao that share the same flute key**. Whether padding words (衬字) may be added at a given point and where they may not depends on whether the metrical pattern is sparse or dense; among melodies some are high and some low, and those whose high and low ranges match can be joined..."

**General rule for fandiao (犯调, fàndiào — modulation by borrowing a mode or making a composite aria; Wu Mei, p0116 area)**: "In sum, **borrowing a mode and making composite arias are both called fandiao; whenever another gong or diao is used, it must always share the same pipe key.** For example, Xianlü gong and Zhonglü gong both use Xiaogong diao, so one may modulate from a Xianlü tune into Zhonglü, or from a Zhonglü tune into Xianlü, without harm. Reason by analogy from this, and there will be little error (ancient pieces occasionally err too, and those errors must not be followed)."

**A received example of borrowing a mode**: after the 《西厢记》 (*Romance of the Western Chamber*) of 王实甫 (Wang Shifu) uses 〔正宫端正好〕, 〔滚绣球〕, 〔叨叨令〕, 〔倘秀才〕, and 〔滚绣球〕, it suddenly borrows 〔般涉调耍孩儿〕 to link the suite together. Wu Mei's verdict: "Only someone with a divine grasp of qu prosody can do this. There are many such cases among Yuan writers, **but one may only use their established method; never link suites on one's own, lest one incur the mockery of the painted tiger.**"

**Two classic examples of composite arias (Wu Mei, p0116 area)**: the 〔九回肠〕 of 张伯起 (Zhang Boqi) = 〔解三酲〕 + 〔三学士〕 + 〔急三枪〕 ("three times three makes nine, hence 'Nine Turns of the Intestine'"); the 〔巫山十二峰〕 of 梁伯龙 (Liang Bolong) = the twelve pieces 〔三仙桥〕, 〔白练序〕, 〔醉太平〕, 〔普天乐〕, 〔犯胡兵〕, 〔香遍满〕, 〔琐窗寒〕, 〔刘泼帽〕, 〔三换头〕, 〔贺新郎〕, 〔节节高〕, and 〔东瓯令〕. Wu Mei's attitude is to preserve it without encouraging it: "I say that **one should seek only fine writing, not novelty or age in the tune title**; but since this form exists, one cannot fail to mention it."

**Large composite arias and the rule of attribution (Wang Jilie, p0137)**: in the Southern *Romance of the Western Chamber*, the aria 〔十二红〕 in "Jiaqi" (佳期, The Tryst) selects lines from twelve tunes; in *The Shepherd's Tale* (《牧羊记》), 〔一秤金〕 in "Cooking Porridge" (煎粥) selects from sixteen tunes; in *The Palace of Eternal Life*, 〔羽衣第二叠〕 in "Dance on the Plate" (舞盘) and 〔羽衣第三叠〕 in "Reunion" (重圆) each select from a dozen or so tunes. "In composite arias of this kind, the lines are certainly not confined to one gongdiao, but **if the qupai of the first few lines belongs to a certain gongdiao, then the composite aria belongs to that gongdiao**. For example, the Xianlü 'Shi'er Hong' begins with 〔醉扶归〕 and therefore belongs to Xianlü gong; the Shang diao 'Shi'er Hong' begins with 〔山坡羊〕 and therefore belongs to Shang diao."
— Wu Mei uses this rule in *Random Talks on Opera* to judge the error in 〔十二红〕 in "Jiaqi" from *The Romance of the Western Chamber*: "It is a **Xianlü** composite aria, not a Shang diao one; its first tune title, 〔醉扶归〕, belongs to Xianlü gong. Since it is Xianlü, the flute key should be **Xiaogong**... yet the flute key is given as **Fanzidiao**, which makes it look like Nanlü gong." The *Nanci Luding* gives the correct solution by annotating the twelve titles sentence by sentence: 〔醉扶归〕, 〔惜黄花〕, 〔皂罗袍〕, 〔傍妆台〕, 〔耍鲍老〕, 〔罗帐里坐〕, 〔江儿水〕, 〔玉娇枝〕, 〔山坡羊〕, 〔东瓯令〕, 〔排歌〕, 〔太平歌〕, "matching them sentence by sentence, which is especially fitting" (notes `10-guqu-nanqu-zuofa.md`, section 1).

## §3 Qianqiang / Huantou / Yao · Three Grades of Amplitude · Mandatory Checklists for Same-Name-Different-Tune and Same-Tune-Different-Mode

### 3a Names and the Three Grades of Amplitude

**Source**: Wu Mei's 《曲学通论》 (*General Treatise on Qu Studies*), Chapter 3, "Tune Titles"; scan pages p0030–p0032 (notes `wu-mei-quxue-tonglun/11b-tonglun-gongdiao-diaoming.md`, section 4).

- **Yinzi belong to the slow-lyric (慢词, màncí) class; guoqu belong to the near-lyric (近词, jìncí) class.**
- The second statement of a tune: **in the north it is called yao (幺, yāo); in the south qianqiang (前腔, qiánqiāng), also called huantou (换头, huàntóu)** (small note: northern drama sometimes writes "wedge" [楔子] for this, which is a different thing).
- **Qianqiang** is used when two, four, or five pieces are strung together and **the line pattern is not changed by a single character**; **huantou** changes the opening of the preceding tune, adding or subtracting a phrase or a character or two.
- **The three grades of huantou amplitude (transcribed exactly)**:

| Grade | Amplitude | Example qupai |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Only the first line is changed** | 〔锦堂月〕, 〔念奴娇〕 |
| 2 | **The first, second, third, and fourth statements are each wholly changed** | 〔朝元令〕 |
| 3 | **Only at the third and fourth statements are the first two lines changed** | 〔梁州序〕 |

- **The closing tune is called weisheng (尾声, wěishēng — coda), also yuwen (馀文, yúwén — remainder), or yibujin (意不尽, yìbùjìn — meaning unexhausted), or shi'ershi (十二时, shí'èrshí — twelve double-hours)** (small note: because southern-qu codas all use twelve beats as their measure) — "they are in fact one and the same"; their format and line lengths differ slightly and should be weighed according to the gongdiao used. Small note: "The southern-qu registers discuss the coda in considerable detail and may be consulted; **today they are often mixed up, which is wrong**."
- **The abbreviation "benxu" (本序, běnxù)**: when the same tune serves both as a yinzi and as a guoqu, the guoqu does not repeat the tune title but is abbreviated as "benxu" (meaning "the original tune so-and-so"). In *The Lute* (《琵琶记》), the yinzi 〔念奴娇〕 (Niannujiao) begins "The rain has passed over the Chu sky..." and the following guoqu begins "Ten thousand miles of clear sky..."; the same pattern appears in *The Moon-Worship Pavilion* (《拜月亭》) in the 〔惜奴娇〕 (Xinujiao) yinzi and "Spring Thoughts, Listless" (春思恹恹). But Wu Mei points out that the convention is not applied consistently: *The Lute* also has 〔祝英台〕, 〔尾犯〕, 〔高阳台〕 and other tunes that serve first as yinzi and then as guoqu, "which ought to be marked benxu, yet are not." → **When reading Ming-Qing chuanqi scripts, "benxu" and a repeated tune title are two ways of writing the same thing; they must not be taken for two different tunes.**
- **A shortcut for telling whether a script is southern or northern** (demonstrated with Guan Hanqing's three plays; see reference-tizhi.md §9): in Yuan zaju, a repeat of the same tune is always written 〔幺／么篇〕; across the twelve acts of the three plays there is **not one instance** of "qianqiang" or "huantou". **Whenever you see "qianqiang", it is certainly southern qu.**

### 3c Four Pitfalls in Tune Titles (to Be Checked Also When Reading a Complete Corpus)

**Source**: Wu Mei's 《曲学通论》 (*General Treatise on Qu Studies*), Chapter 3, p0031 (notes 11b, section 4).

| # | Pitfall | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One tune divided between two modes, with entirely different melody and line structure** | 〔小桃红〕 occurs in both Zhenggong and Yue diao; 〔红芍药〕 in both Nanlü gong and Zhonglü gong |
| 2 | **The ancient form is lost to investigation, and popular transmission has added or removed words and lines so extensively that it "cannot be followed"** | 〔越恁好〕, 〔雌雄画眉〕 and the like |
| 3 | **Corruption of the written form has destroyed the original sense** | 〔生查子〕: 查 is 楂 (the story of 张骞 [Zhang Qian] riding the raft); 〔玉抱肚〕: Tang people called a belt baodu (抱肚, bàodù), now corrupted to 〔玉胞肚〕; 〔醉公子〕 now corrupted to 〔醉翁子〕; 〔朝天紫〕 was originally a peony name (see the 《牡丹谱》 of 陆游 [Lu You], *Peony Register*), now corrupted to 〔朝天子〕 |
| 4 | **One tune used twice (a yinzi and a guoqu sharing a name)** | See the "benxu" entry above |

**Two further points for reading tune titles**:
- **The non-Chinese layer in northern-qu tune titles** (p0030): "As for names such as 〔呆骨朵〕, 〔刺古阿纳忽〕, and 〔唐兀歹〕, **they were mostly taken from the vernacular of the time, and people today do not know their meaning**." When reading Yuan zaju and encountering such titles, **do not try to interpret them literally**.
- **A qupai that shares a name with a Song ci tune must never be filled according to a ci register** (p0030): northern qu "either slightly alters the words and lines, or **uses only the name and changes the tune**"; the southern-qu long tunes 〔喜迁莺〕, 〔称人心〕, and 〔意难忘〕 **are used only as yinzi and are not given a beat**, while the guoqu 〔八声甘州〕, 〔桂枝香〕, and 〔尾犯序〕 "**also use only the name and entirely change the line pattern**".
- **Five sources of tune titles** (p0031): (1) phrases from earlier poetry (〔鹧鸪天〕, 〔青玉案〕, 〔粉蝶儿〕); (2) place names (〔梁州序〕, 〔八声甘州〕, 〔伊州令〕); (3) named for their melodic character (〔步步娇〕, 〔急板令〕, 〔节节高〕, 〔滴溜子〕, 〔双声子〕); (4) seasons, people, flowers and birds, or whatever happened to catch the eye, "so varied and tangled that they cannot all be recorded"; (5) formed by mixing and modulating several tunes (two tunes: 〔锦堂月〕; three: 〔醉罗歌〕; four or five: 〔金络索〕, 〔雁鱼锦〕; those that explicitly state the number of borrowings include two-borrowing 〔江儿水〕, four-borrowing 〔黄莺儿〕, six-borrowing 〔清音〕, seven-borrowing 〔玉玲珑〕, eight-borrowing 〔八宝妆〕, nine-borrowing 〔九疑山〕, ten-borrowing 〔十样锦〕, twelve-borrowing 〔十二红〕, thirteen-borrowing 〔十三弦〕, and thirty-borrowing 〔三十腔〕). Wu Mei's verdict: "**These are all the cunning of literati; in fact there is no deep meaning in them.**"
