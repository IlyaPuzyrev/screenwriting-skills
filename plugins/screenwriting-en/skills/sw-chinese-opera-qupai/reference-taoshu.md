**Hard constraints on suite linking (Wu Mei, p0211–p0212, given item by item in the original)**:
1. “In Northern suites the joins between one piece and the next are the most rigorously controlled, stricter than the rules of Southern qu.”
2. **One singer throughout**: “A single role carries it. A Northern suite must be sung by one person from start to finish, unlike Southern lyrics, where no matter who the character is, the singing may be divided.”
3. **Four acts (折, zhé) make the unit, and the labor cannot be shared**: “Yuan plays generally stop at four acts, and the arias of those four acts may not be shared among other roles. In *Autumn in the Han Palace* (《汉宫秋》), the shēng (male lead) sings all four acts to the end; in *The Jade Flute Girl* (《玉箫女》), the dàn (female lead) sings all four acts to the end.”
4. **Length must be even**: too few pieces is called **shǎnsā** (闪撒 — flash-and-scatter: the singing is over before the audience can listen closely); too many is called **xùdao** (絮叨 — chattering: the singer cannot get through it). “Both draw the same ridicule, so the length of a suite deserves attention.”
5. **The maximum number of pieces differs between sanqu and dramatic qu**: “Yuan sanqu sometimes run to twenty pieces… **but within a play the most is twelve or thirteen.**”

**Two hard tonal patterns in the Shuangdiao 〔新水令〕 (Xīnshuǐlìng) suite** (p0232): “**The last line of 〔新水令〕 must use 『平仄去平』 (píng–zè–qù–píng: level, oblique, departing, level), and the first line of 〔步步娇〕 (Bùbùjiāo) must use 『去上平平平仄去』 (qù–shǎng–píng–píng–píng–zè–qù: departing, rising, level, level, level, oblique, departing)**, and the like; students probably do not all know this.”

**Two closing points** (p0232, p0234–p0235):
- “This suite imitates Ruoshi's *Handan* throughout, so there are many places where its lines and characters do not agree with the old tables. Yet **because custom has carried it down, this suite has come to stand among the orthodox patterns**. The student must fill words according to it if the result is to be harmonious with strings and bamboo.” (On *The Palace of Eternal Life*, “The Surrounding Hunt” — “custom has carried it down” can rewrite the orthodox pattern.)
- “Someone may ask: in the repeats (qiánqiāng) and yāo pieces (幺篇, yāopiān — further stanzas of the same tune) there are quite a few places where the four tones differ from the preceding tune — why? The answer: **keeping the rule is dead; filling words is alive.** … **Any place where a shift is made must be able to cite an established work by an older master as support**; that is why I have listed them all here.”

**The fundamental difference between Northern and Southern suites (original, p0141)**:
> “In Northern qu, although there are long suites and short suites, **the opening and closing several pieces of a suite in each mode are more or less fixed, while the pieces in the middle may be added, cut, altered, or even reversed in order**. In Southern qu, only the entrance aria must come at the entrance and the coda at the close; as for the middle pieces, it is very hard to fix which comes before which. Yet this does not mean there is no rule: Southern qu distinguishes **slow tunes from fast tunes**, and **slow tunes must come first, fast tunes last**.”

**Matching suites to role types (p0166, a direct rule of allocation)**:
- The **expression of feeling** category consists of tunes that are “all delicate and well-fitted, with a lingering emotional quality, and are mostly large suites and long arias; **the principal act of a chuanqi should use this kind of suite**, and it is suited to **shēng (specifically xiǎoshēng, young male) and dàn**.”
- The **joy** category suits **unison singing**; the **excursion** and **action** categories are also mostly suited to unison singing; the **grief** and **hidden resentment** categories are mostly suited to **dàn**, though a xiǎoshēng (young male) may also sing them.
- **When a shēng or jìng — jìng here meaning dàmiàn, a major painted-face role — or a lǎoshēng (older male) meets a tragedy, Northern qu is preferable**, as the various Northern Nanlü suites are “best suited to broad-voice (kuòkǒu) roles.” Original: “**In sum, broad-voice roles sing mostly Northern lyrics; Southern lyrics are only twenty or thirty percent. Southern qu are soft and effeminate, lacking heroic sound, and so are not suited to the voices and mettle of shēng and jìng.**”
- **Short transitional scenes** (popularly called “passage-link plays,” 过脉戏, guòmài xì): “Though the arias are not many, **without them the plot does not run through and the threads do not connect; they are absolutely indispensable in chuanqi**. Short tunes and fast tunes should be used, **and long suites of slow tunes must never be used**.”
- **Coarse tunes** (such as 普贤歌 and 光光乍) **are limited to chǒu, jìng, báimiàn (white face, a villainous painted-face role), and èrmiàn (second face, a secondary painted-face role)**.

**The seventeen mode–mood correspondences (the ancients on qu, transcribed as-is, p0161)**: Xianlü = fresh and far-reaching | Nanlü = sighing and sorrowful | Zhenggong = melancholy and martial | Huangzhong = wealth and lingering tenderness | Zhonglü = high and low in flashing turns | Daogong = ethereal and serene | Dashi = romantic and cultivated | Xiaoshi = graceful and charming | Gaoping = smooth and expansive | Banshe = gathering pits and trenches | Xiezhi = urgent, compressed, with empty rests | Shangjiao = sorrowful and winding | Shuangdiao = vigorous, quick, and soaring | Shangdiao = desolate, resentful, and longing | Jiaodiao = sobbing and lingering | Gongdiao = elegant and weighty | Yuediao = washing away with cold laughter.
> “This speaks of the modal sound of each Northern mode, and also of the tune mood suited to it.” **Four examples of the Yuan writers observing this rule**: “**For tragedy, use Nanlü and Shangdiao; for comedy, use Huangzhong and Xianlü; for heroes and great men, sing Zhenggong; for buffoonery and mockery, sing Yuediao.**”

**Why chuanqi cannot use Northern qu alone (p0161)**: “A zaju is measured at four acts, so it may use Northern lyrics alone, **and is moreover limited to one singer**; a chuanqi runs to forty or fifty acts, or at least no fewer than twenty or thirty, **and every role type must sing**. If it used Northern lyrics exclusively, not only would the tune names be insufficient, but the tune moods could not be finely expressed; **Southern qu must make up eighty or ninety percent and Northern qu ten or twenty percent**, and only then can singing and performance reach excellence.”

**The use of zhuàn (赚), also called “Not the Road” (不是路) (p0179)**: “**Zhuàn** exists in every mode and is also called ‘Not the Road’; **it is most suitable at the point where the staging (排场, páichǎng — the ordering of entrances, exits, and scene weight) changes and the mode and key are shifted.**” Examples: in *The Golden Sparrow* (《金雀记》), “Feigned Jealousy” (〈乔醋〉), 〔太师引〕→ zhuàn → 〔江头金桂〕; in *The Kite Misadventure* (《风筝误》), “Forced Marriage” (〈逼婚〉), 〔桂枝香〕→ zhuàn → 〔长拍〕. Limits: “**In a whole chuanqi it is best to use zhuàn in one act only; within one act no more than two pieces.**” Counterexample: *The Purple Hairpin* (《紫钗记》) uses zhuàn in four acts of the whole play, uses it in two consecutive acts, “Matchmaking” (〈托媒〉) and “Discussing the Marriage” (〈议婚〉), and uses as many as four pieces in “The Hairpin Reunited” (〈钗圆〉) — “all show a failure to study tune law and staging.”

**Good and bad examples of changing mode and rhyme within one act (p0174)**: Good — in *The Palace of Eternal Life*, “The Pledge” (〈定情〉), the first half, a Dashi suite, is the joyful banquet of the consort's investiture, while the second half, two Yuediao pieces, is intimate speech in the inner palace; in “The Secret Vow” (〈密誓〉), the Yuediao pieces are the Cowherd and Weaver Girl meeting in heaven, and the Shangdiao pieces are the palace prayer and oath; in “Burying the Jade” (〈埋玉〉), the whole Zhonglü suite is the six armies forcing the consort, and the final piece, 〔朝元令〕, is the escort setting out. “**In all such places, it is precisely by changing mode and rhyme within one act that the fit with the plot becomes clearer.**” Bad — in *The Story of the Lute* (《琵琶记》), “Eating Husks” (〈吃糠〉) “changes mode without cause.” Another example (p0174–p0176): in *The Tale of Washing Silk* (《浣纱记》), “Entrusting the Son” (〈寄子〉), the act opens with two pieces of Yudiao 〔胜如花〕, uses two Zhonglü 〔泣颜回〕 in the middle, and ends with two Dashi 〔催拍〕 and one Zhenggong 〔一撮棹〕: “**In one act four modes are used, because 〔胜如花〕 is a transitional tune suited to action, 〔泣颜回〕 is a tune for expressing feeling, and 〔催拍〕 with 〔一撮棹〕 is a grief tune suited to parting; this exactly matches the three successive stretches of plot in the act.**”

## §5 The Three-Way Division by Extra Beats · Detailed Rules for Entrance Arias and the List of Short Arias for Jìng and Chǒu · Examples of Southern Beat Patterns

### 5a Extra Beats and the Threefold Division into Slow, Medium, and Fast

**Source**: Wang Jilie (王季烈), *Talks on Opera from the Yǐnlú Studio* (《螾庐曲谈》), volume 2, chapter 3, 3.5; scanned pages p0150–p0154. Terms from volume 1, chapter 2, 2.2 (p0040).

- **Definition of the extra beat (volume 1, p0040)**: “One extra beat is added between each pair of regular beats, so that the time taken to sing the tune is doubled. **Only Southern qu has it; Northern qu has very little** — Southern qu has few words and long melodic phrases, so an extra beat can be added; Northern qu has many words and a pressed tempo, so it cannot.” There are two kinds: the **head extra beat** (头赠板, tóuzèngbǎn), marked `乂` and placed at the head of a character or melodic phrase; and the **waist extra beat** (腰赠板, yāozèngbǎn), placed in the middle of a phrase.
- **Rule of ordering (original, p0150 and p0154)**: “The order in which guòqǔ (过曲 — the main arias after the entrance aria) are linked **must always be slow tunes first, medium tunes next, fast tunes last**. Slow tunes, that is, fine tunes, all have extra beats; medium tunes have no extra beats but one beat to three eyes (一板三眼, yī bǎn sān yǎn — one beat with three subdivisions); fast tunes have one beat to one eye (一板一眼, yī bǎn yī yǎn — one beat with one subdivision).” “**Tunes with extra beats must be used first; tunes without extra beats must be used last; tunes where the extra beat is optional may come before or after.**”

> “In the first act, the first piece uses 〔桂枝香〕, the second uses 〔宜春令〕, the third uses 〔麻婆子〕: **thrown together out of order, with strange and uncanny tuning**… Did he not know that **〔桂枝香〕 is in the xiǎogōng key (小工调) and 〔宜春令〕 in the liùzì key (六字调), one high and one low, so that their natures do not admit each other**; and that **〔麻婆子〕 is a fast Zhonglü tune with beats but no melodic phrase (popularly called 干板, gānbǎn, “dry beat,” or 流水板, liúshuǐbǎn, “flowing beat”)**, so how could it be linked with slow tunes such as 〔桂枝香〕 and 〔宜春令〕?”
**Terms**: **干板／流水板** (gānbǎn / liúshuǐbǎn) — a fast tune with beats but no melodic phrase, which cannot be linked to slow tunes.

### 5b Detailed Rules for Entrance Arias and the List of Short Arias for Jìng and Chǒu

**Source**: Wang Jilie (王季烈), *Talks on Opera from the Yǐnlú Studio* (《螾庐曲谈》), volume 2, chapter 3, 3.4; scanned page p0148 (each item is a hard constraint); Wu Mei (吴梅), *A General Theory of Qu Studies* (《曲学通论》), chapter 11, “The Ten Knowings,” item 4, “Entrance Arias,” p0086–p0087.

**Wang Jilie's seven rules**:
1. The yǐnzi is **sung at the entrance**, in free tempo (散板, sǎnbǎn), with or without flute accompaniment; it is best if the yǐnzi and the following guòqǔ are in the same mode, **but the mode need not be the same**.
2. **In the first act of a chuanqi, when the principal shēng enters, a rather long yǐnzi is appropriate** (〔恋芳春〕, 〔满庭芳〕, 〔喜迁莺〕, 〔东风第一枝〕, 〔齐天乐〕); **in later acts a long yǐnzi is not appropriate.**
3. Some yǐnzi are made by joining two tunes: 〔破齐阵〕 inserts two lines of 〔齐天乐〕 into 〔破阵子〕; 〔女临江〕 takes the head of 〔女冠子〕 plus the tail of 〔临江仙〕.
4. **A yǐnzi may use only its first half or its opening lines; unlike a guòqǔ, it need not be filled in completely.**
5. **One person entering may use only one yǐnzi**; when several people enter at once, **it is best to assign one yǐnzi to be sung by several of them**; if one person has already sung a guòqǔ and only then does a second person enter, a separate yǐnzi should be used.
6. **In one act, no more than three yǐnzi may be used.**
7. **Jìng and chǒu never use a yǐnzi on entrance**; a short tune replaces it, and such tunes are mostly **sung or recited dry (乾唱／乾念, gānchàng / gānniàn — without instrumental accompaniment)**.

**List of short entrance tunes for jìng and chǒu (p0148)**: 光光乍、大斋郎、五方鬼、梨花儿、水底鱼儿、赵皮鞋、吴小四、雁儿舞、普贤歌、字字双、倒拖船、柳穿鱼、双劝酒、秃厮儿.

**Guòqǔ that have the nature of a yǐnzi and may serve as entrance pieces (p0148)**: 蜡梅花、望吾乡、金钱花、窣地锦裆、哭岐婆、一江风、六么令、上马踢、胜胡卢、秋夜月、忆多娇、出队子、缕缕金.

**Wu Mei on entrance arias (p0086–p0087)**:
- **Sanqu suites do not use a yǐnzi; only chuanqi does.** Its function: a person entering cannot immediately state the plot of the play, so first, using the scenery before his eyes or the feeling in his mind, he makes “a few covering words, which is why it is called yǐn, ‘to draw forth’ — it draws forth the many topics that follow.”
- The difference between North and South: Northern lyrics open with several pieces in free tempo and only after three or four pieces use fixed beats, **so Northern qu does not use a yǐnzi**; every Southern tune has a fixed beat pattern, and each role cannot sing a tune the moment he enters, so a yǐnzi is used.
- Common yǐnzi are taken from short and medium ci lyrics; long tunes such as 〔念奴娇〕, 〔薄倖〕, 〔东风第一枝〕, and 〔尾犯〕 may be used in excerpt, or **the original sequence itself may serve as the guòqǔ** — after the 〔祝英台〕 yǐnzi comes the 〔祝英台序〕 guòqǔ, as in *The Story of the Lute*, “Admonishing the Slave Girl” (〈规奴〉); after the 〔高阳台〕 yǐnzi comes 〔高阳台序〕, as in *The Story of the Lute*, “Refusing the Marriage” (〈拒姻〉).
- **How to write it**: “One must use one's own guts to stand in for another's voice, yet must adjust the sentence patterns and check the wording, so that a few phrases cover the business of the whole act in advance, **neither obscure nor vague**. This is the highest principle.”
- Models and counterexamples: **the yǐnzi of *The Story of the Lute* are all good; “opening the door and seeing the mountain, Gao Ming of Dongjia (东嘉, Dōngjiā) stands alone.”** In *The Tale of Washing Silk* (《浣纱记》), Fan Li's (范蠡) entrance 〔卜算子〕 — “Honoring the king and settling the hegemony, not below Huan and Wen” — “would be fine put in the mouth of the King of Yue, but is inappropriate coming from a minister” (**a misplaced voice**); only Xi Shi's (西施) yǐnzi 〔绕池游〕 is rather good, “the rest are either mean or diffuse.” *The Pearl* (《明珠记》) **often uses old lines by earlier poets, or alters one or two lines** — Wu Mei notes: “Many Ming writers did this; both Tang and Shen did; **it is still not a method to follow**.”

### 5c General Rules for Southern Sentence Pattern and Beat Pattern (板式, bǎnshì; One-Character to Ten-Character Lines)

**Source**: Wang Jilie (王季烈), *Talks on Opera from the Yǐnlú Studio* (《螾庐曲谈》), volume 3, chapter 2, 2.5; scanned pages p0213–p0218 (notes `wang-jilie-yinlu-qutan/03-juan3-puqu.md`). Original: “The beat patterns of each tune name differ, yet there is also one general rule that can sum them up. **What is that general rule? It is sentence pattern.** The lines in a tune — **after first identifying and excluding the padding words** — can be classified by beat pattern from one-character lines all the way to ten-character lines.”

**Names of beats (Wu Mei, *A General Theory of Qu Studies*, 11.9, p0094–p0095)**: a beat struck at the very start of the sound is the **solid beat** (实板, shíbǎn; also called 头板, tóubǎn, head beat); one struck halfway through a character is the **pulled beat** (掣板, chèbǎn; also called 腰板, yāobǎn, waist beat); one struck as the sound ends is the **closing beat** (截板, jiébǎn; also called 底板, dǐbǎn, bottom beat); when the last beat of one singer's tune and the first beat of the next singer's tune fall together, it is the **joined beat** (合板, hébǎn). A beat that comes before the tune is a fault called **hurried beat** (促板, cùbǎn); a beat that comes after the tune is a fault called **stalled beat** (滞板, zhìbǎn).

**Which tunes do not mark beats (Wang Jilie, volume 3, p0212–p0213)**: **In Southern qu, only the yǐnzi and zhuàn** (不是路, 破出破, 红衲袄, 青衲袄, and the like) **do not mark beats within the line; only a closing beat is placed at the end of each line**; all other guòqǔ mark several beats within a line. **In Northern qu, the first and second pieces of each act and the closing coda mostly do not mark beats**, only a closing beat at the end of the line; most of the middle pieces still mark beats.

**Which book to consult for beat patterns (volume 3, p0213)**: “**For Southern qu, *The Rules of Southern Lyrics* (《南词定律》) is the most detailed; for Northern qu, *Comprehensive Correct Tables of Northern Lyrics* (《北词广正谱》) is the most precise**… If these two books are hard to obtain, one may equally seek it in *The Great Compendium of the Nine Modes* (《九宫大成》), *The Imperially Authorized Register of Tunes* (《钦定曲谱》), and old tunes for which a modal register exists. But **the Northern qu in *The Imperially Authorized Register of Tunes* does not mark beats**; among old tunes with modal registers, only *Yinxiang Hall* (《吟香堂》), *Nashu Ying* (《纳书楹》), and this book have correct beat patterns. **Beyond these, the modal registers copied by vulgar performers do not distinguish principal from extra beats and often draw out beats to save trouble; they are absolutely unreliable.**”

**Three hard inferences (end of volume 3, 2.5)**: ① **a mistake in reading sentence division equals a mistake in beat pattern**; ② **padding words must first be identified and excluded** before the general sentence-pattern rules can be applied; therefore, if principal characters and padding words are not clearly distinguished, the beats cannot be marked; ③ **within the same line of the same tune name, the character count may not be increased or decreased at will** — add or remove one principal character and the whole beat-pattern rule changes.
**One latitude (p0238)**: “In marking beats one should indeed follow the old tables; but where the sentence pattern of a tune filled by a later writer does not agree with the old table, **one should consider the sense and sentence division of the whole text and adapt the beat pattern — one must not be bound to the table and read the sentence to pieces**.”

**The relation of padding words to beats (Wang Jilie, volume 2, 5.7, p0203–p0205)** — three hard rules for Southern qu:
1. **Position**: “They must be added **where the beat pattern is dense**, and must be added **at the beginning of a line or in the middle of a line**; **within the last three characters of a line, and where the beat pattern is sparse, padding words must never be recklessly added.**”
2. **Number**: “**Moreover, at each place there may be no more than three padding words.**”
3. **Part of speech**: “**And function words (虚字, xūzì) are preferable; content words (实字, shízì) should not be used.**”
Example (*The Palace of Eternal Life*, “The Secret Vow,” 〔山桃红〕): “俺这里” and “踹上这” are both used at the beginning of lines, no more than three characters at each place, and “deserve to be called a fitting work.”
**The difference from Northern qu (p0205)**: “This is said of Southern qu. **In Northern qu, padding words have no limit at all**, because **Northern beats are not fixed; when there are many padding words one may simply add beats on the padding words, unlike Southern qu, where beats may not be marked on padding words.**”

**How many padding words to use, graded by scene type (Wu Mei 11.8, p0088 and p0093)**: dialogue arias cannot avoid padding words; **in grand ensemble arias and in sanqu suites, “the fewer the better”**; in slow tunes with slack beats, two or three more are still permissible; **in tight tunes with urgent beats, one or two more and the singer cannot dodge in time**; **on tunes with drawn-out beats (抽板曲, chōubǎn qǔ), never use many padding words; the first line of a coda uses a waist beat — never add padding words there**. Criterion by character count: “Arias run from one-character to seven-character lines; only 〔虞美人〕 has a nine-character line… **anything beyond eight or ten characters is mostly padding words.**”
**The definition of padding words, with the most famous example** (*Random Talks on Opera*, on the craft of Southern qu, p0184): “**Padding words are characters that ought not to be in the tune.** For example, the first line of the first piece of *Eight Yangs* (《八阳》) should be a seven-character rhymed line; as now written, ‘**gathering up the great earth and rivers into one load**’ (收拾起大地山河一担装), the three characters ‘**收拾起**’ (shōushi qǐ, “gather up”) are padding words.”
**A beat-by-beat analysis of 〔桂枝香〕: eleven lines, twenty-three beats** (same page, original lyric from *The Story of the Lute*): “‘见’ has one beat and ‘忒’ has one beat, and the two happen to be adjacent; therefore a few padding words may be added to line 2 of 〔桂枝香〕 — when sung, the two beats are very close together and the singer can still catch the beat”; “‘将人轻贱’ and ‘非爹胡缠’ are the same”; “‘被’ in ‘被人传’ has one beat and is again linked to the beat on ‘缠’ in the preceding line, so padding words may also be added there.” **General rule**: “**In short, wherever the beat pattern is tight, padding words may be added; wherever it is slack, they are absolutely not allowed.**”
**Two specifications**: 〔皂罗袍〕, a Xianlü gong tune, **nine lines and twenty-five beats in all**; 〔河传序〕 **thirty-two beats**; 〔桂枝香〕 **eleven lines and twenty-three beats**.

- **The boundaries of rhyme interchange**: “Within each of these six rules, **rhymes may often be interchanged; outside it, they may not be borrowed**. If some do interchange, it is only because the character is in fact read differently.”
- **The threefold division into sound, voice, and rhyme (p0068)**: “The making of a character must have **a head, a belly, and a tail**. **Sound** (声) is the issuing of the sound, the head of the character… **Voice** (音) is the sustained sound, the belly of the character. **Rhyme** (韵) is the closing of the sound, the tail of the character, which is why it is called the remaining rhyme. Of the three, **rhyme occupies the last place and is the most important**.”
- **The entering tone rhymed separately in Southern qu (p0074)**: “Southern qu also interchanges the three tones while rhyming the entering tone separately, which exactly agrees with the method of the lyric writers… The entering tone may serve as level, rising, or departing, **but only according to the table**… It is not like Northern qu, where the character 『縠』 is fixed as 『古』. Thus if it were mixed into the three tones there would be no difference from Northern qu… Therefore **the method of rhyming it separately was distinguished, and the melody is fixed by changing the mode according to the table**.”

### 6b Rules for the Use of Level and Oblique Tones

**Source**: Wu Mei (吴梅), *A General Theory of Qu Studies* (《曲学通论》), chapter 4, “Level and Oblique,” p0033–p0034.

- **The character of the four tones in qu**: “**The level tone is still held in; the rising tone is pressed but not released; the departing tone goes and does not return; the entering tone is cramped and cannot turn its own melody.** Thus although all are oblique, rising is rising and departing is departing; **only the entering tone may be used interchangeably**.” Violating this rule is called **ào sǎng** (拗嗓 — a wrenched throat).
- The rules (given item by item in the original):
  1. “Where level is required, oblique may not be used; where oblique is required, level may not be used; where rising is required, departing may not be used; where departing is required, rising may not be used; where rising-departing is required, departing-rising may not be used.”
  2. “Single lines may not be repeated, **and four level, four rising, four departing, or four entering tones may not be used in succession**.”
  3. “In rhyming, there are places where level is required but oblique may also be used, places where oblique is required but level may also be used, and places where level is required but, unavoidably, **a rising tone may replace it**.”
  4. “**At the rhyme foot, entering-tone characters should not often replace level, rising, or departing characters.**”
  5. “Where several lines in one tune use oblique tones in succession, **it is best to alternate one rising and one departing**.”
  6. Quoting Shen Jing: “When meeting a departing tone, sing high; when meeting a rising tone, sing low; for level and entering tones, weigh their high or low pitch and do not let them be confused.”
  7. “**Level has a lifting sound, rising has a pausing sound, departing has a sending-off sound.**”
  8. “Broadly, level, departing, and entering tones are there as soon as the mouth opens; **only the rising tone must start from a level-tone pitch, gradually lift, and then turn back down heavily** — this is a natural principle.”
- **The two ways North and South handle the entering tone**:

| | Northern qu | Southern qu |
|---|---|---|
| Whether the entering tone has a proper sound | “In the North the entering tone has no proper sound, so it is distributed among the three tones level, rising, and departing, **each with its own assignment, and may not be borrowed**.” | “In the South **the entering tone has its own proper sound**, and may also be applied to the three tones level, rising, and departing, **with no restriction**.” |
| Shen Jing's (词隐, Cíyǐn) view | “The entering tone may replace the level tone; this alone divulges the secret of creation” — he held that Southern qu should also follow *Rhymes of the Central Plains* (《中原音韵》) and permit the entering tone only to replace level. | **Wu Mei objects**: “He did not know that Southern qu and Northern qu are precisely different.” |

> Wu Mei's metaphor: “In general, the presence of the entering tone in ci and qu **is just like licorice in medicine**: whenever there is a deficiency, or the wording of a level, rising, or departing character is not right, and there is no way out, one entering-tone character lets you stretch the rules and joke your way past. Therefore **it may serve as level, as rising, or as departing; and when it serves as level, it may be yin or yang, and need not be bound by Northern pronunciation**.”
> Wang Jilie's accompanying rule (volume 2, 5.5, p0200–p0201): when Southern qu uses entering-tone rhyme, it usually **uses entering-tone rhyme throughout** — *Seeking My Kin* (《寻亲记》), “Sending the Conscript” (〈遣役〉); *The Two Pearls* (《双珠记》), “Giving the Pearl” (〈与珠〉); *The Palace of Eternal Life*, “Recovering the Capital” (〈收京〉) — “without mixing in one rhyme of level, rising, or departing; this is the most fitting.” **The only exception is the two rhymes 家麻 (jiāmá) and 车蛇 (chēshé)** (“the characters of the three tones, read quickly, all become entering tone without needing a change of sound”). Counterexample: Yuming's Four Dreams (玉茗四梦, Yùmíng sìmèng) “often mix one or two entering-tone characters among level, rising, and departing rhymes” — “**this is not at all a method to follow**.”

### 6c Detailed Rules for Yin and Yang

**Source**: Wu Mei (吴梅), *A General Theory of Qu Studies* (《曲学通论》), chapter 5, “Yin and Yang,” p0035–p0037.

- Definition, with North and South reversed: Zhou Deqing (周德清) “took the clear as yin and the muddy as yang; therefore in Northern qu **every lifted character is called yang** (rising from low to high is called lifting) **and every pressed-down character is called yin** (descending from high to low is called pressing). **Southern qu is exactly the opposite**: every clear-sounding character is lifted up, and every muddy-sounding character is pressed down.” (The original's parenthetical: “The above yin and yang are spoken of Northern qu, where lifting is yang and pressing is yin; Southern qu reverses this.”)
- **What happens when it is wrong**: “**If a character ought to be lifted but a yin character is used, the sound will surely be at odds; if a character ought to be pressed but a yang character is used, the character will surely be at odds.** Once sound and character are at odds in yin and yang, the melody must be out of tune. **If you bend the melody to fit the character, the sound is no longer its own sound; if you change the character to fit the melody, the character is no longer its own character.** Never mind that the listener finds it harsh; the singer's throat will also be pricked.”
- **Two examples of changing the character to fit the melody**:

| # | Source | Original | Fault | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Rhymes of the Central Plains* (《中原音韵》) records the Northern tune 〔四块玉〕 | “彩扇歌、青楼饮” (With painted fans they sing, in the courtesan houses they drink) | The singer sang “青” (qīng) as “晴” (qíng) — this one character **needs a rising sound, but “青” is a yin (lower) tone** | Change it to “买笑金、缠头锦” (gold to buy laughter, brocade for the head-wrap) and only then does it fit. “This is what is meant by **the sound not being its own sound**.” (p0035) |
| 2 | *The Story of the Lute* (《琵琶记》), 〔尾犯序〕 | The last line of the first tune, “公婆没主” (The parents have no one to rely on), and then the line “冷清清” (Cold and clear) | “冷” (lěng) is a **pulled beat** and must be sung straight down, so a rising tone is appropriate; “清清” must be lifted, so a yin tone is appropriate. But the next line, “相思两处一样泪盈盈” (Two places longing alike, tears brimming), has “泪” (lèi), a departing tone whose breath is spent as soon as the mouth opens, and “盈盈” (yíngyíng), a yang character not easy to lift | It must be sung with the sound of “英” (yīng) to fit (p0036) |

- **The matching formula**: “Broadly, **yin characters should match rising tones, and yang characters should match departing tones**.” Test case: the changed head of 〔长空万里〕, in the three lines “孤影” (lone shadow), “光莹” (bright and clear), and “愁听” (sorrowfully listening) — “‘孤’ (gū) is yin matched with rising, ‘愁’ (chóu) is yang matched with departing, and both sing beautifully; only ‘光’ (guāng) sounds like ‘狂’ (kuáng), and that is because **yin is matched with departing**.”
- **Yang characters are appropriate at lifts**: “When a yang character is lifted, its sound **is inhaled and goes in**; its lifting is inward, which is why yang characters are especially fine. When a yin character is lifted, its sound **is exhaled and goes out**, like the departing tone's one-way journey with no return.” But “**one cannot say categorically that yang may never be used at a lift**.”
- **Even *Romance of the Western Chamber* does not fit**: “Even though *Romance of the Western Chamber* (《西厢》) is the ancestor of tonal law, **in the opening line ‘游艺中原’ the character ‘原’ should by rule be a yin tone, but ‘原’ is actually yang, and must be sung as ‘渊’ to fit**.”
- **The order of rising and departing** (*A General Theory of Qu Studies*, chapter 7, p0063; notes 09, item 39): **departing-rising in combination is better than rising-departing** — in a tune the departing tone is the easiest to set off, while the rising tone turns at an extremely low point; going from departing to rising is top-down (easy), and going from rising to departing is bottom-up (difficult); **use rising-tone characters in low-pitched lines and departing-tone characters in high-pitched lines, and use as few rising-tone characters as possible**.
- **The melodic pattern is determined solely by the four tones** (Wang Jilie, volume 3, chapter 3, p0240–p0241; notes `03-juan3-puqu.md` 3.2):

| Tone | Melodic pattern | Gongche (工尺, gōngchě) example |
|---|---|---|
| Level tone | Mainly level | Yin level uses “四” (sì) or “尺” (chě) or “工” (gōng); longer phrases use “四上四,” “尺工尺,” “工六” |
| Yang level | First phrase slightly low, second phrase slightly high | “合四,” “上尺,” “尺工” |
| Rising tone | **From low to high** | “工合四,” “四上尺,” “尺工六” |
| The “paused sound” of the rising tone | The second phrase falls, then the third and fourth phrases rise again | “Not every rising-tone character need use the paused sound, but **any character using the paused sound is undoubtedly a rising-tone character**” |
| Departing tone | **Mainly a far send-off, from high to low, exactly opposite to the rising tone** | “伬仕五六,” “五六工尺,” “尺上四合” |
| Yang departing (exclusive to it) | The first phrase starts low, and at the second phrase suddenly lifts high | “六仕五六,” “工五六工尺” — **unusable for yin departing** |
| Entering tone | **Entirely the same as the level tone**: yin entering is like yin level, yang entering like yang level | “Read, it has an entering tone; sung, it has none” |

> From this follow three hard constraints: ① at places in the tune register marked “rising and departing may not be shifted,” **replacing the character with one of another tone is equivalent to scrapping the existing modal register**; ② **where rising and departing join and yin and yang alternate is the wùtóu**, and the wùtóu must use brilliant wording; ③ **the entering tone, when sung, equals the level tone**, so entering-tone rhyme cannot be mixed with level, rising, and departing rhymes.
> One more self-check (Wang Jilie, volume 3, chapter 5, p0300–p0312): **three consecutive characters of the same tone within one line (especially three departing tones or three rising tones) will inevitably produce a wrenched melody** — after finishing a tune, mark its four tones once and scan them.

**How to find the wùtóu (the two authorities read together)**: Wu Mei defines it as “the wùtóu is the place in a tune where the three sounds level, rising, and departing join in a chain.” In a seven-character line, **the third, fourth, and fifth characters may not have the same sound**; generally **yang departing joins yin rising, yin rising joins yang level, or yin departing joins yang rising, and yang rising joins yin level** (*A General Theory of Qu Studies*, chapter 10, p0078–p0079). Two known anchor points in Southern qu: “Tradition holds that **line 1 and line 7 of 〔黄莺儿〕**, and **line 7 of 〔皂罗袍〕**, are wùtóu.” The practical method (Wang Jilie, volume 2, 5.6, p0203): “**In *Comprehensive Correct Tables of Northern Lyrics*, the places marked ‘rising and departing may not be shifted,’ and in the Southern tune registers the places marked ‘the two characters so-and-so have a wonderful rising-departing’ or ‘the two characters so-and-so have a wonderful departing-rising’ — all these are places where wùtóu should be used.**” Another of Wu Mei's rules: “In Southern qu, the places marked **‘departing-rising’ and ‘rising-departing’** in Shen Jing's register may be taken as wùtóu and must all be strictly observed.”

- **Which modes each tradition lost (p0028)**: “Since the Yuan, **the North has lost three** (Xiezhi diao, Jiao diao, Gong diao), **and the South has lost four** (the same three Northern losses plus Shangjiao diao). **Thus the North has only fourteen and the South only thirteen.**”
- **“The thirteen modes” is a different matter (p0028)**: beyond the seventeen modes there was a further change to thirteen modes — the six surviving gong were no longer called “gong” but “diao” (Xianlü, Huangzhong, Zhenggong, Zhonglü, Nanlü, and Daogong were all simply called diao), and Jiang Weizhong (蒋惟忠) of the Ming uses this name in his *Register of the Thirteen Modes* (《十三调谱》). **When reading Ming tune registers and meeting “the thirteen modes,” do not take it for another modal system: it is the count remaining from the seventeen after the six gong were renamed.**
- **How tunes were assigned to modes (p0028–p0029)**: “**Each tune differs in sadness and joy, hardness and softness; each mode also differs in high and low, base and exalted; and within the pipes there is further difference in pitch, rise, and fall. Therefore the high and low, the mournful and joyful qualities of each tune's sound were matched by similarity and assigned under each gong and each diao**, and the flute key was also fixed to its measure.” “The deployment of each gong and each diao is extremely strict, **like soldiers each having their own commander, not permitted to overstep.**”
- **The reversal on Northern strictness and Southern laxity (p0029)**: “The laws of North and South **were originally not two different regimes**… **at the beginning of the craft it was not that the North was strict and the South lax** — from the appearance of *The Story of the Lute* (《琵琶记》) and *The Pavilion for Worshipping the Moon* (《拜月亭》), which created the fashion of **not seeking the mode or counting the tunes**, later writers often wrote recklessly and confounded things. **This is the fault of learning from the ancients.**” → The premise that “Ming and Qing chuanqi all observed the modes” is false; see reference-jinji.md §14.

**Three rules for writing the coda** (same chapter):
- “The coda brings a whole piece of music to a close; it must **exert even more spirit, and the last line in particular must close on the most brilliant phrase** to be fine. Northern codas are always good; writers of Southern qu often **end sloppily, merely seeking to finish**, and good codas are very rare in drama.”
- **Tang Xianzu's (汤显祖) reversal**: “Only in **Tang Ruoshi's Four Dreams is every coda good**, though they also have many padding words.” — elsewhere in this chapter (p0094) he criticizes the Four Dreams for too many padding words; **the two statements must be held together, not one alone**.
- **A coda always uses twelve beats** (section 11b, item 4: “the closing tune is called wěishēng, also yúwén, or yìbùjìn, or shí'èrshí”; small note: “because Southern codas all use twelve beats as their measure”); **the first line of a coda uses a waist beat, and padding words must never be added** (p0088).

### 7b The Three Situations in Which the Coda May Be Omitted, and the Suísà

| Case | Condition | Source |
|---|---|---|
| One | **One tune made into two, four, six, or eight pieces** — no coda is used in any case | Wu Mei 11.6, p0091 |
| Two | **Two tunes each stopping at one or two pieces** — no coda is used in any case | Wu Mei 11.6, p0091 |
| Three | **In the final act, the close has already been taken over by the official judgment and the verdict** — evidence from Yuan zaju: act 4 of *The Gold Thread Pool* (《金线池》) closes on 〔收江南〕, followed by Shi Haowen's (石好问) instructions plus eight lines of 〔词云〕, with no coda in the whole act — | `guan-jiufengchen/03-jinxianchi.md`, section 3 |

| Appendix | **In plays that repeat one tune, and in the tunes of short acts, “the coda may often be dispensed with”** | Wang Jilie, volume 2, p0160 |

**Suísà (随煞, “following coda”), also called běnyīn shà (本音煞, “coda on the original note”)**: no coda is used; **the last line of the suite is sung somewhat slowly and that serves as the closing sound**. Example: in *The Story of the Lute*, “Expressing Feeling” (〈陈情〉), the last line of the second piece of 〔归朝欢〕, “For the state forgetting home, how dare I fear the toil” (为国忘家敢惮劳), is sung a little slowly with a lingering, swaying tone and thus brought to a close. (Wu Mei 11.6, p0092)

**Self-check**: if the coda has reached “the end of a spent crossbow” (强弩之末, qiángnǔ zhī mò), it has not closed — go back and check whether the matter was already finished earlier; pull out the coda's last line and read it alone: it must be the most firmly grounded line in the whole suite.
