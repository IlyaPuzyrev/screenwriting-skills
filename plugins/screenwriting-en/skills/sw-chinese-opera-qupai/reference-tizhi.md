**Three techniques derived from this table (p0174)**:
1. **Moving to another mode and changing rhyme within a single act is legitimate, but it must be caused by a change of staging or a turn of plot** (the three examples 〈定情〉, 〈密誓〉, and 〈埋玉〉; see reference-taoshu.md §4e, final paragraph).
2. **The Northern-tune rule that one act is sung by one person may be adapted**: in 〈觅魂〉, the complete Northern Xianlü suite has the jing sing the first half and the mo the second. “Although this does not agree with the general Northern rule that a whole act is limited to one singer, **a long suite of this kind simply cannot be sung through by one person's strength; the spirit leaves the body and another role takes over, but it is still, after all, the same Tongyou**, so although it adapts the established methods of the ancients it still does not go against them. **Only someone who deeply understands the principles of qu could have made this innovation.**”
3. Overall assessment: “**In choosing modes, assigning roles, and arranging the plot, it takes care that parting and reunion, grief and joy are interwoven and cross-matched, so that the performers are never unevenly worked and the audience feels an inexhaustible succession of wonders. From antiquity to the present, no chuanqi staging has surpassed this.**”

**The law of alternating staging (p0169, common to both systems)**:
> “If the sheng sings the first act and the dan the second, **the third act must use a broad-voice role or a lively ensemble play**; if two or three acts of slow arias and long suites have passed, **a short transitional scene or a comic play performed by chou or jing must be interspersed**.”

**Where sheng and dan make their entrances (p0167)**: Chuanqi has always “mostly taken **one sheng and one dan** as the masters of the whole play” (Fan Li and Xi Shi in 《浣纱》 (Washing the Silks), Li Yi and Huo Xiaoyu in 《紫钗》 (The Purple Hairpin), Liu Mengmei and Du Liniang in 《牡丹亭》 (The Peony Pavilion), Zhao Ruzhou and Xie Suqiu in 《红梨》 (The Red Pear), Hou Fangyu and Li Xiangjun in 《桃花扇》 (The Peach Blossom Fan), Emperor Minghuang and Yang Guifei in 《长生殿》), “this sheng and dan are the main brain of the whole chuanqi, **and must appear in the second and third acts**. The first act of a chuanqi always explains the general idea, so **its second act is really the first, and its third act is really the second**.” **Exceptions coexist**: 《邯郸梦》 (The Handan Dream) is led by a laosheng (Lu Sheng), and 《钧天乐》 (The Music of Heaven) by a laosheng (Shen Bai) and a xiaosheng (Yang); “if an author can invent from his own heart and capture a singular realm, **he need not cling to the established methods of the ancients**.”

**Why literati chuanqi did not get staged (p0166–p0167)**: “The plot may be extraordinary and the diction beautiful, but if it does not fit the modes it cannot be given to the throat; if the modes fit and the syllables are harmonious and beautiful, **without attention to staging it cannot be performed on the carpet stage**… Thus the chuanqi they wrote, though beautiful in words, did not circulate in the singing halls, **and were instead inferior to plays compiled by actors, which could still win applause. That is why this collection discusses staging in particular detail.**”

### 9b Structural Conclusions from Reading the Three Plays Together

| Item | The Injustice to Dou E | Saving a Courtesan | The Golden Thread Pool | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wedge | Present, at the start of the play | **Absent** | Present, at the start of the play | The wedge is optional |
| Wedge qupai | Xianlü, one 〔赏花时〕 | — | Xianlü, 〔端正好〕 + 〔幺篇〕, two qupai | **Only these two tunes; they may carry a yāo piece; at most two qupai** |
| Wedge singer | **chongmo** Dou Tianzhang | — | **zhengdan** Du Ruiniang | **Variable** |
| Singer of the four acts | zhengdan (40 qupai) | zhengdan (44 qupai) | zhengdan (39 qupai) | **Fixed, and exclusive** (all 123 qupai in the four acts of the three plays belong to the zhengdan) |
| Mode of the four acts | Xianlü · Nanlü · Zhenggong · Shuangdiao | Xianlü · Shangdiao · Zhenggong · Shuangdiao | Xianlü · Nanlü · Zhonglü · Shuangdiao | **The first act is always Xianlü, the fourth always Shuangdiao**; the two middle acts may change |
| Rhyme category of the four acts | Youhou · Qiwei · Xiantian · Jielai | Qiwei · Youhou · Zhenwen · Yumo | Zhenwen · Youhou · Gengqing · Xiantian | **One rhyme per act, four acts without repetition**; the rhyme category is a property of the act and may repeat across plays |
| Yāo pieces (幺篇, yāopiān — a repeat of the same tune) | 0 | 3 | 2 | Optional; **never written as “qiánqiāng” (前腔 — a repeat of the preceding tune) or “huàntóu” (换头 — a repeat with a changed opening)** |
| Coda (尾声, wěishēng) | Present in all four acts | Present in all four acts | **Absent in Act Four** | The final act's closure may be handed over to the official verdict and the rhymed judgment |
| Song-to-speech ratio | 1:3.1 | 1:1.8 | 1:1.7 | Tragedy narrates through speech; comedy balances song and speech; a single act may fall as low as 1:0.7 |
| Title-and-name couplet | Two lines | Two lines | **Four lines** | As many lines as there are plot threads |

- **The wedge has its own rhyme category**: in The Injustice to Dou E the wedge is in zhenwen and Act One in youhou; in The Golden Thread Pool the wedge is in yumo and Act One in zhenwen. **The wedge's mode is always Xianlü (the same mode as Act One), but its rhyme category starts afresh and is not read together with Act One; “four acts, four rhymes, no repetition” does not count the wedge.**
- **Repetition of rhyme within a suite is allowed**: the character 头 (tóu) rhymes five times in Act One of The Injustice to Dou E; 休 (xiū) four times in Act Two of Saving a Courtesan; 人 (rén) and 门 (mén) three or four times each in Act Three of Saving a Courtesan. → This empirically demonstrates that “**dramatic song may repeat rhymes; pure song may not**.”
- **The right to sing = the right to be protagonist** (Saving a Courtesan): Song Yinzhang (宋引章), the woman at the center of the case, played by the wai-dan, does not sing a single qupai in the whole play; all the singing goes to Zhao Pan'er (赵盼儿), “the person who comes to solve the problem,” and the play's title was accordingly fixed as 《赵盼儿风月救风尘》 (Zhao Pan'er Saves a Courtesan with Wind and Moon) — **structure, singing rights, and title are one and the same decision**.
- **The singer and the person who drives the action may be separated** (The Golden Thread Pool): the zhengdan sings 41 of 41 qupai, but the one who drives the plot is Prefect Shi, who does not sing (three times he remains onstage to speak an exit poem, twice he lays a plan, and in the final act he stages a fake trial).

**Rule of allocation (original text)**:
> “In a chuanqi, **it is best to have every role category represented, and yet not to repeat them**. To have all the categories is easy; not to repeat them is very hard. So **for the principal characters of the play avoid repetition; supporting roles may repeat; but within a single act repetition must still be avoided**.”

**Matching with suite types** (p0166, i.e., reference-taoshu.md §4e, final paragraph): long suites of expressed feeling suit sheng and dan; joy suits unison singing; excursions and action also mostly suit unison singing; grief and hidden resentment mostly suit the dan; **when sheng-jing (the big painted face) or laosheng meets a tragedy, Northern qupai are appropriate**; **coarse arias are limited to chou, jing, white-face, and second-face roles**; jing and chou do not use a yǐnzi on entrance but short arias (for the list see reference-taoshu.md §5b).

### 10b The History of Role-Type Differentiation and the “Equalization of Work and Rest”

**Source**: Wu Mei (吴梅), 《曲学通论》 (Qǔxué tōnglùn, A General Treatise on Qu Studies), ch. 6, “On Method, Part One” (己), “Equalizing Work and Rest”; scanned pages p0046–p0047 (notes `wu-mei-quxue-tonglun/07-tonglun-zuofa-shang.md`). **The most distinctive item in this chapter.**

- **History of differentiation (original text)**: “In chuanqi the role types are summed up as sheng, dan, jing, and chou. **From the mid-Ming, when the Haiyan style flourished and Kunqu followed it, role types multiplied** — among sheng there were the names **laosheng, guansheng, jinsheng, and ersheng**; among dan there were the names **laodan, zhengdan, chadan, xiaodan, and tiedan**; among jing there were **great, small, and middle** distinctions; only chou remained one. Counting them, there are thirteen categories; people today speak of **ten role categories**, giving the round number.”
  > ⚠️ **Wu Mei gives thirteen categories, Wang Jilie fifteen; the two sets of categories differ (Wu Mei has jinsheng, ersheng, chadan, and xiaodan, and lacks cishadan, zuodan, guimendan, wai, and mo). Set them side by side; do not merge them.**
- **Before Kunqu, one role type carried the whole play**: “Before Kunqu existed, a chuanqi generally used one person from beginning to end; from the opening to the close, no matter how many dozens of acts, one role type bore it all; **there was never a case of several people sharing the labor**.”
- **The Kunqu method of matching act to role type**: “Once Kunqu flourished, the analysis of role categories became finer, and each troupe kept no fewer than seven or eight performers of each category. So whenever a play is performed, the role categories set down in the play are first checked act by act: **for the same sheng role, which act should use guansheng and which jinsheng; for the same dan role, which acts should use zhengdan and which xiaodan**; each is determined by what the words and lines in the songs are like, and only then is it fixed as this or that role.”
- **A hard rule for the lyric writer, with two reasons**: “The lyric writer must understand the actors' work and rest: **if the preceding act has the sheng as protagonist, the next act must not use the sheng again**, and the same for other roles. There are two reasons: first, the actors take turns and so are not overworked; second, costumes and hair ornaments take a good deal of time to change — **from the preceding act's exit to the next act's entrance is only three to five minutes**, and in that extremely short, rushed interval to change the most difficult costumes and ornaments, even ten pairs of hands could not do it.”
- **Named failure cases**: “Literati who could write lyrics that could be sung were already few; those who understood this principle, unless they had themselves performed, were fewer still. In the famous chuanqi I have read in the past, this fault is especially common: **Tang Ruoshi's (汤若士, i.e., Tang Xianzu) 《紫钗》 (The Purple Hairpin) and Xu Yucun's 《镜光缘》 (The Mirror's Edge) suffer from it even more; that is why they cannot be performed regularly**.”

## §11 The Original Text of the Sixteen General Principles of 《桃花扇》 · The Thirty-Character Table of the Four Colors and Four Qi in the Program

**Source**: Wikisource, 《桃花扇凡例》 (General Principles) and 《桃花扇纲领》 (The Program), based on the Kangxi printed edition; notes `kong-taohuashan/00-fanli-gangling.md`. The original text has not been abridged or rewritten; the notes have converted traditional to simplified characters, and 齣 is uniformly written 出 (chū — scene).

### 11a The Sixteen General Principles (Transcribed as Is)

1. The play is named The Peach Blossom Fan, so the peach-blossom fan is like a pearl, and the brush that wrote The Peach Blossom Fan is like a dragon. It threads through clouds and enters fog, sometimes straight, sometimes oblique, but the dragon's eyes and claws never leave the pearl; the viewer should use a discerning eye.
2. The gains and losses of court politics and the gathering and scattering of literati are all verified as to time and place, with no fabrication. As for young men and women in love, or guests exchanging banter, though there is some embellishment, it is not the stuff of pure fiction.
3. Staging has rises, falls, and turns, all opening unique realms; it comes suddenly and leaves abruptly, so that the audience cannot predict the situation. **Whatever situation can be predicted is a stale formula.**
4. Each scene's threads run continuously, **may not be moved, and may not be reduced**. It is not like old plays, which drag in this and that from east and west to make up a scene.
5. In the various editions of lyric writing, every long act conventionally uses ten qupai and every short act eight. Actors cut the complex and take the simple, singing only five or six, and often their cuts and keeps are inappropriate, betraying the author's painstaking care. **Now I fill only eight qupai in a long act, and six or four in a short act, so that they cannot cut again.**
6. The names of the tunes are not chosen for novelty; the suite patterns are all those familiar to the people of the time, so there is no need to puzzle them out — they enter the mouth and become song. **But the words must be new and striking, not one character stolen from another's leftovers.**
7. Lyrics are never filled in at random: **when a feeling in the heart cannot be spoken and a scene before the eyes cannot be seen, one borrows lyrics to express it**. Again, when one matter is told a second time and has already been said in spoken dialogue, here one replaces it with song. If what should be spoken is put into song, the listener will not understand, and the connection before and after will break. And if it has already been said in spoken dialogue, why must it be put into song again?
8. Making a tune must have purpose and interest: **each piece should make a piece of writing, each line a line of writing**. Set on the desk or sung on the stage, it should move and inspire, making people beat time and sigh in admiration — this is what is meant by “singing well.” If one forces it out in perfunctory fashion with no meaning at all, then for singer and listener alike it is drudgery.
9. Lyrics entering the modes and conforming to level and oblique tones: **the whole is governed by clarity of meaning**. I often see Southern arias twisted and strained so that people cannot understand them; though forced to fit strings and bamboo, they can only serve as gongche notation — how can that be called lyric writing?
10. The allusions used in the lyrics are taken up at random, without showing the seams of piled-up erudition. Rotten things are made new, stiff things made alive. **Pointing at ghosts and heaping up corpses** — these must not be used.
11. Spoken dialogue is modulated and ringing, its sentences neat and practiced; the set business and the banter all have a distinctive interest. **Better not to be popular than to injure elegance** — this quite captures the intent of the poets.
12. In old editions, spoken dialogue made up only three parts, and the actors on entering added seven of their own; vulgar mannerisms and vicious jokes often turned gold into iron and became a burden on the writing. **Now the spoken dialogue is full and complete, and not one character may be added.** If the piece is somewhat long, this is the reason.
13. The laughter, anger, and scolding of set business are like plain-line portraiture: brows and beard must appear completely, and whatever draws the audience in depends entirely on this. Now all is marked out in detail; face and spirit leap off the page, bursting with life — and what will it be when an actor imitates it?
14. Role types exist to distinguish the gentleman from the petty man, **and sometimes, when the proper role is insufficient, one borrows chou or jing**. A clean face or a painted face is like a person's beauty or ugliness; one should appreciate it beyond such outward marks as sex and coat color, as when judging a horse.
15. **The entrance and exit poems are the beginning and end, the order and coherence of a rhyme**; if one uses old lines or vulgar lines and hastily fills the space, the whole scene loses color. **Most current editions favor Tang-assembled lines, which is also a worn-out convention. Now I have made new poems for all of them**, so that the beginning has a head and the ending has a thread, and the meaning of “setting out and adorning the return” can be roughly recovered.
16. The full text has forty scenes; **in the upper volume, a test scene at the head and an intercalary scene at the end; in the lower volume, an added scene at the head and a continuation scene at the end** — this too is the order and coherence of the forty scenes as a whole. With a beginning and an end, the breath is full and the spirit complete, and it escapes the well-worn path of parting and reunion, grief and joy; may it not be called drama?
   (The Wikisource text of this item reads “下本首下本首加一出” — “in the lower volume, at the head, an added scene at the head of the lower volume”; the three characters “下本首” are duplicated, a scribal repetition on that page; corrected to a single occurrence according to the sense and the play's actual scheme.)

**The sixteen items grouped by function** (an arrangement made in the notes for transfer into the skill, not a grouping in the original text):
- Theme and through-line: 1 (the pearl and the dragon)
- Historical discipline: 2
- Structure: 3 (the rises and falls of staging, against “stale formulas”), 4 (each scene's threads may not be added to or cut), 16 (the overall order of forty scenes plus the four unnumbered scenes — test / intercalary / added / continuation)
- Number and prosody of arias: 5 (eight qupai in a long act, six or four in a short act), 6 (familiar tunes in the suite, new words), 9 (mode and tonal pattern subordinate to clarity of meaning)
- Division of labor between song and speech: 7 (what should be sung and what should be spoken are mutually exclusive), 8 (each piece a piece of writing, each line a line of writing)
- Rhetoric: 10 (allusions not piled up)
- Spoken dialogue and comic business: 11, 12 (spoken dialogue complete and full, not one character to be added), 13 (stage business marked out in detail)
- Role types: 14

**Kong Shangren's own explanation (original text)**:
> Color is the image of parting and reunion. Men have their companions, women their ranks; they are distinguished as left and right, and the two sections balance to the last grain. Qi is the number of rise and fall. Gentlemen form a party, petty men form a faction; counted as odd and even, the two sections differ not by a hair. Daoist Zhang is a man outside the world, who sums up the case of rise and fall. The Old Master of Ceremonies is a nameless man, who closely examines the scene of parting and reunion. Bright as a mirror, level as a balance: it is called chuanqi, but it is truly the way of one yin and one yang. — Casually settled by the Man of Yunting.

**Three key points**: ① The axis of classification is not good and evil but **function** — “color” governs the line of parting and reunion, “qi” governs the line of rise and fall, and each line is symmetrically balanced, left/right or odd/even. ② There are only two principal-color characters (Hou and Li); **the intermediate color = the intermediary who drives parting and reunion** (Yang Longyou, Li Zhenli); **the composite color = performers, booksellers, and the like, who thread the needle**; **the embellishing color = those who appear only to support the atmosphere**. ③ **The two figures of the warp section are frame characters, listed in a section of their own, which shows that they are part of the establishment and not decoration** — Daoist Zhang “sums up the case of rise and fall” (in continuation scene 40, 〈入道〉 “Entering the Way”), and the Old Master of Ceremonies “closely examines the scene of parting and reunion” (test scene 1 / intercalary scene 20 / added scene 21 / continuation scene 40), and the Old Master of Ceremonies takes part in events in Scenes 3, 32, and 38 in the capacity of a master of ceremonies of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices.

### 11c Appendix: The Four-Level Chain in 《小识》 and the Revision Loop for Lyric Writing in 《本末》

- **《小识》 (“Minor Notes,” third month of the wùzǐ year of Kangxi, 1708)**: “Chuanqi means transmitting the extraordinary in an event; **if the event is not extraordinary, it is not transmitted**.” The four-level chain (顶针, dǐngzhēn — a chain in which the end of one clause becomes the beginning of the next):
  > “**The peach blossom on the fan** is the extraordinary within the ordinary; the peach blossom is **the bloodstain of a beauty**; the bloodstain is she who kept her chastity and waited for marriage, whose head was broken and bloodied **rather than submit to a powerful traitor**; the powerful traitor is a remnant of the Wei eunuch faction; the remnant is one who brought in music and women, gathered goods and profit, formed factions and took revenge, and **destroyed the imperial foundation of three hundred years**.”
  It comes to rest on a material witness: “The imperial foundation is gone — where is the powerful traitor? Only the bloodstain of a beauty, the peach blossom on the fan, chattered about by every mouth and clear before every eye: this is the extraordinary within the ordinary, what need not be transmitted and yet can be transmitted.”
- **The source of “borrow the feeling of parting and reunion to write the sense of rise and fall”**: this line is often attributed to the 《小引》 (“Short Preface”) or the 《小识》, **but its actual source is the fumo's spoken lines in the test scene 《先声》 (“First Sound”)**; the closing passage of the 《纲领》 — “Color is the image of parting and reunion… qi is the number of rise and fall” — is its taxonomic version. **When quoting, cite the right source.**
- **The lyric-writing method in 《本末》 (“The Whole Story”)**: Kong admits, “Though I know a little about modes, I fear they will not suit the mouths of singers.” He had the Suzhou man Wang Shouxi (王寿熙), then an actor who thoroughly understood suite patterns, come to him morning and evening: “He showed me the suite patterns of the song texts… and I filled them according to the manual. **Whenever a piece was finished, I had it sung to the beat; if a single character was crooked, I revised it at once, and so the whole work has no jaw-breaking fault.**” — **this is the standard revision loop for qupai-system lyric writing**, and it forms the inside and outside of item 9 of the General Principles. Also: in the bǐngxū year (1706) he watched performances at Mount Heng for two days, and “when something did not satisfy my mind, I called the troupe leader and corrected it on the spot” (a second revision by way of rehearsal direction).
- **One piece, 《砌末》 (“Stage Properties”), is missing**: the table of contents prefixed to the Peach Blossom Fan entry on Wikisource has only seven items — Short Preface, Minor Notes, The Whole Story, General Principles, Evidential Research, Program, and Preface — **there is no 《砌末》**; the common electronic editions at gushiwen, Guoxuemeng, and elsewhere also include only the forty-four scenes of the main text. One must consult a collated paper edition or a facsimile of the Kangxi printed edition. **This is a known gap; do not assume that Kong Shangren did not write it.**
- **The five kinds of material in 《考据》 (“Evidential Research”)**: ① unofficial dynastic history in annals form — the anonymous 《樵史》 (The Woodcutter's History) in twenty-four sections, the only category **arranged by day**, from which come the dates such as “third month of guǐwèi” and “fourth month of jiǎshēn” placed under each scene title; ② collected works of the participants (thirteen poems from Hou Chaozong's 《壮悔堂集》 (Collected Works of the Hall of Great Remorse), etc.); ③ pieces in contemporary poetry and prose collections that touch on characters in the play (Qian Muzhai, Wu Jungong, Yang Longyou, Mao Bijiang, Shen Meisheng, Chen Qinian, Gong Xiaosheng); ④ **plays written by the villain himself** — two works in the 《石巢传奇》 (Shichao chuanqi): 《十错认春灯谜》 (Ten Misidentifications, or The Spring Lantern Riddle) and 《燕子笺》 (The Swallow's Letter), that is, Ruan Dacheng's own plays, used for direct performance in Scene 4, 《侦戏》 (“Spying on the Play”), and Scene 25, 《选优》 (“Choosing the Best Singer”); ⑤ each item notes the number of pieces of material; **the count itself proves that “verified as to time and place, with no fabrication” is not an empty phrase**.

## §15 Table of Structural Evolution · Zaju vs. Chuanqi Comparison

### 15a Structural Evolution (Wu Mei, 《中国戏曲概论》)

**Source**: Wu Mei, 《中国戏曲概论》, base text `_text/wu-mei-xiqu-gailun.ocr.txt` (Jilin People's edition collected works), **all quotations marked `[to be checked]`** (the full text of this book was not found online; all the notes' to-be-checked marks are retained); notes `wu-mei-xiqu-gailun/01`, `02`, `03`.

**(1) What zhugongdiao (诸宫调, zhūgōngdiào — all-mode narrative singing) supplied (vol. 1, p0281–p0283)**

|---|---|
| The name is the structural method | “The Song dynasty's dàqǔ (大曲 — grand suite) and zhuǎntà (转踏 — a round dance form) merely took one tune and repeated it in a round… but in this collection each mode has sometimes more than ten qupai, sometimes only one or two, and then changes to another mode. **It joins several modes to sing one matter, and so it is called zhugongdiao.**” → zhuǎntà = one tune repeated; zhugongdiao = several modes strung together; **“changing the mode” means changing the section and changing the emotion** |

**(2) The physique of zaju: four contrasts with zhugongdiao (vol. 1, p0288)**

| # | Zhugongdiao | Zaju |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | No scene titles | **Conventionally four acts**; exceptions: Ji Junxiang's 《赵氏孤儿》 (The Orphan of Zhao) in five acts, Zhang Shiqi's 《花月秋千记》 (The Flower-Moon Swing) in six — “but these are not often seen” |
| 2 | No role categories; one person plays and sings throughout (“roughly the same as later storytelling”) | Divided into mo, dan, wai, chou, and other categories, **with mo and dan as the principal ones**; what the Yuan called “both dan and mo complete” |
| 3 | No action states | “**What records action is called kē (科 — action cue), what records speech is called bái (白 — spoken passage), what records singing is called qǔ (曲 — song)**”; “it joins song, dance, speech, and action into one — **this is the advance of dramatic song**” |
| 4 | (Outside physique) language | “As for language, **there is only one school, that of běnsè (本色 — ‘true color,’ the plain native style); there is no such thing as profuse diction or dense embroidery**” |
| 5 (separate paragraph) | — | “Jiangxi píchàng (嘌唱 — a popular song form) in particular could alter the old and the constant, **and separately created the form of North–South mixed suites (南北合套, nánběi hétào)**. Once the many sounds began, the rules of lyric writing were fully prepared.” — **the North–South mixed suite was created in the Yuan by popular pure-singing clubs (not literati)** |

> **A note on scope**: Wu Mei's “four acts” is **a general rule, not an iron law**, and he himself gives two counterexamples, of five and six acts. **The loosening of the zaju “four acts and one wedge” did not begin with Ming writers; the Yuan already had it.**

**(3) Four ways Ming zaju broke Yuan zaju (vol. 2, p0301–p0302) — a table of exchanges**

| # | Yuan drama | Ming drama | Examples given by Wu Mei |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mostly four acts | **Not fixed** | Xu Wei's 《四声猿》 (Four Cries of a Gibbon), Shen Zijin's 《秋风三叠》 (Three Folds of Autumn Wind) = one act each; Wang Heng's 《郁轮袍》 (The Yulun Robe), Meng Chengshun's 《桃花人面》 (Peach Blossom, Human Face) = as many as seven or five acts. → “**So the number of acts is not fixed.**” |
| 2 | Mostly one person singing alone | **This rule not kept** | Act Three of 《花舫缘》 (The Flower-Boat Bond) is sung by the dan; in 《春波影》 (Spring Wave Shadow) Act Two is sung by Lady Yang and Act Four by the old nun. → “**So the singing role is also not fixed.**” |
| 3 | Mostly Northern lyrics | **Largely Southern qu** | Wang Daokun's 《高唐梦》 (The Gaotang Dream), Lai Jizhi's 《挑灯剧》 (Trimming the Lamp). → “**So Northern and Southern lyrics may also be used interchangeably.**” |
| 4 | Language crude and plain; **staging extremely poor** | Language polished and beautiful; **staging has order** | “Yuan drama's staging was extremely poor; the Ming brought order to it.” |

> **The price (original text)**: “Yet **for the breath of vastness and rugged majesty, the Ming writers fall far short of the Yuan** — this too is a natural tendency in literature.” → **The four loosening moves are a single package deal; one cannot pick just one to loosen.**
> Another breach of form (p0304): **Xu Wei's 《女状元》 (The Female Top Graduate) “alone used Southern lyrics to write a play, breaking the fixed form of zaju; from this time Southern drama proliferated”** — the starting point of Southern zaju is attached to this one work.

**(4) Three changes of vocal style (vol. 2, p0297–p0298, transcribed as is)**

| Period | Content |
|---|---|
| First period | At the founding of the dynasty, half followed the lingering habits of the Yuan: “After that Southern drama grew daily more flourishing; household troupes marked the beats, and each strove for greater splendor; writers appeared in crowds and washed away the manners of the ancient, rough Yuan. Thus the whole realm followed the wind, and it was able to contend with the ancient Music Bureau.” |
| Second period | **The Haiyan tune**. Yang Zi of Ganchuan in the Yuan received the tradition of Guan Yunshi; his eldest son Guocai “obtained the secret depth of the Southern sound, **created a new melody, and called it the Haiyan tune**; between the Western Yangtze and the two capitals all harmonized with it.” |
| Third period | **The Kunqu “water-milled” tune**. “In the Jiajing–Longqing period, Wei Liangfu (魏良辅) of Taicang and Liang Chenyu (梁辰鱼) of Kunshan were famous throughout the realm for their singing. **Liangfu investigated sound and rhyme, sitting and lying in a small tower for nearly twenty years; he examined and fixed the metrical patterns of The Lute and created the water-milled tune. Chenyu wrote Washing the Silks and gave it to him**; it was flowing and securely harmonious, far above the old tunes of Yiyang and Haiyan.” |

> Two facts useful to the playwright: ① the water-milled tune was **fixed on the metrical patterns of The Lute**, which therefore became the “mother text” of Kunqu metrical patterns; ② **a new vocal style can only establish itself with a new play to match** (Liangfu created the style; Chenyu wrote Washing the Silks and gave it to him). **The style and the play came out together.**

**(5) Four branches of Ming chuanqi writers (vol. 2, p0308, transcribed as is)**
> “As for the branches of writers, they may be roughly divided into four. After 《琵琶》 (The Lute) and 《拜月》 (The Moon-Worshipping Pavilion) appeared, **most writers favored plainness and simplicity**. After 《香囊》 (The Scented Sachet) and 《连环》 (The Chain) appeared, **writers admired ornate diction**. After Yuming's 《玉茗四梦》 (‘Four Dreams’) **used the method of Northern lyrics to write Southern lyrics**, many **overstepped the rules**. After the various chuanqi of Ciyin, **which sought to fit the rules with vulgar language**, the **doggerel-makers** became numerous. Thus those who corrected the excess of plainness used **silken language**, and those who reformed the profusion of ornament favored **běnsè**. **Those who corrected Yuming's prosody and also excelled at polished diction were Wu Shiqu and Meng Zisai; those who kept the method of Wujiang and yet brought it out with urbanity and elegance were Wang Boliang and Fan Xiangling.**”

**(6) Five respects in which the Qing surpassed the Ming, and the reform of scene count (vol. 3, p0323)**
1. **Reform of the old theater format**: “**Ming chuanqi generally took forty scenes as the measure, and the shorter ones still had thirty; they were drawn out and overflowing, with many defects**… Again, the facts were bizarre, and at the point of exhaustion they would resort to gods, immortals, ghosts, and monsters to provide a place for the reunion of sheng and dan. **The Qing took their material from fiction and did not indulge in invention; detailed or abbreviated, elaborate or simple, they always hit the mark; long plays had no wasted words, short plays no cramped faults.** Thus 《拈花笑》 (The Flower-Plucking Smile) and 《浮西施》 (Floating Xi Shi) **complete one matter in one act, making it convenient to watch without weariness**. Yang Lihu's 《吟风阁》 (Pavilion for Singing to the Wind) and Jingshi Shanmin's 《红楼梦》 (Red Chamber Dream) **are good when performed separately and also good when sung together**.”
2. **Prosody manuals**: the Ming had no good manuals (in 《太和正音》 (Great Harmony and Correct Sounds) the distinction between principal and padding characters was unclear; Shen Jing's 《南谱》 (Southern Manual) had not gathered everything) → the Qing had Rules for Southern Lyrics (“the metrical patterns could be followed”) and the Nine Modes Compendium (“there was a basis for fixing the notation”).
3. **Rhyme**: Rhymes of the Central Plains distinguished only the yin and yang of the level tone → in the Qing, Shen Chenglin's 《韵学骊珠（辑要）》 (Pearls of Rhyme Studies, Abridged) already reached the departing tone, and Zhou Ang's 《中州》 (Central Plains) further distinguished the two rising tones, with the marvel of “**choosing characters according to the tune**.”
4. **Books on temperament**: the Ming had only Wang Jide and Wei Liangfu → the Qing had Mao Qiling's 《竟山乐录》 (Jingshan's Record of Music), Chen Li's 《声律通考》 (A Comprehensive Study of Tonal Rules), Ling Tingkan's 《燕乐考原》 (Origins of Court Banquet Music), and Dai Changgeng's 《律话》 (Talks on Temperament).
5. **Catalogues and song scores**: Huang Wenyan's 《曲海》 (Sea of Qu) and 《曲海总目提要》 (Annotated Catalogue of the Sea of Qu); Ye Tang's Nashuying and Feng Qifeng's Yinxiangtang.
> **Summary sentence**: “**Weighing names against realities, they may surpass the former worthies; only there are not many writers, and here they fall short.**” That is, **the Qing had better tools than the Ming and fewer works**.
> **Periodization summary (p0343)**: “I have often said that **above the Qianlong era there were plays and there was song; in the Jiaqing–Daoguang period there was song but no plays; after the Xianfeng–Tongzhi period there was truly neither play nor song**.” — **“Play” (staging / scene design) and “song” (diction / musical law) are two different things, and each can live or die on its own.**

**(7) The progenitor of chuanqi and the distinction of names (Wang Jilie, 《螾庐曲谈》, vol. 4, p0314–p0320)**

| Item | Original text |

## §16 The Three-Way Division of Labor (Making Lyrics / Setting Notation / Singing) and the Distinction between “Qupu” and “Gongpu” · Which Editions to Read and Which Manuals to Consult

### 16a The Three-Way Division of Labor

**Source**: Wang Jilie, 《螾庐曲谈》, vol. 1, ch. 1, introduction; scanned pages p0033–p0034 (notes `wang-jilie-yinlu-qutan/01-juan1-duqu.md`).

> “Yet with these four aspects, one person's energy cannot necessarily master them all, so it is well to **divide the work along separate paths**: **those strong in literary grace take on the making of lyrics; those skilled in musical temperament take on the setting of notation; those with a quick ear, an agile mouth, and a bright voice take on the singing**. Only by joining these three kinds of talent in careful study can one exhaust what Kunqu can do.”

| Division | What it does | Which volume of Talks on Qu |
|---|---|---|
| **Making the lyrics (zhìqǔ)** | Filling words — writing song text according to a qupai's metrical rules; **this is the playwright's job** | Vol. 2, “On Making Lyrics” |
| **Setting the notation (pǔqǔ)** | Fixing the gongpu, marking the beats and noting the gongche | Vol. 3, “On Setting Notation” |
| **Singing (dùqǔ)** | Performance | Vol. 1, “On Singing” |

**Two sequences: the order of making ≠ the order of learning (p0034, the program of the whole book)**
> “In terms of the **constitution** of Kunqu, the order is first to fill words, next to set notation, and then to sing; but in terms of **learning** Kunqu, one must first learn to sing, and only then learn to fill words and set notation. For **without learning to sing, the choice of qupai, the placement of padding characters (衬字, chènzì — syllables added beyond the prescribed count), and the arrangement of the four tones can never be right — even if the words are excellent, they cannot be given to strings and pipes.** Therefore my talks on qu begin with singing, then take up making lyrics, then setting notation, and finally discuss origins and evolution.”
→ **The three tasks of choosing a tune, placing padding characters, and arranging the four tones are decided by singing technique before the pen touches paper.**

**A sharp judgment (vol. 1, p0042)**: “Those who study Kunqu today mostly take actors and flute players as their only teachers, **and such people know few characters and have limited understanding of meaning**… **As for the four tones, yin and yang, and rhyme, these are not within their knowledge — how can one expect them to teach? This is something the student should seek in books** — such as Zhou Deqing's 《中原音韵》 (Rhymes of the Central Plains), Fan Shanzhen's 《中州全韵》 (Complete Rhymes of the Central Plains), and Wang Jun's 《音韵辑要》 (Essentials of Phonology).”

**The four steps of setting notation (vol. 3, p0212)**: “First, **correct the metrical patterns**; second, **distinguish the four tones and yin and yang**; third, **identify the principal melodic figures**; fourth, **connect the gongche**.” And “**where the beats are sparse the gongche should be simple; where the beats are dense the gongche should be elaborate; without first fixing the metrical pattern, there is no way to fix the melodic pattern**.”
**The theory of principal melodic figures** (zhǔqiāng, vol. 3, ch. 4, p0245): “**Where a certain line and a certain character of a certain qupai has a certain fixed melodic figure, this is the principal figure of that qupai.**” What follows, p0246–p0300, is an extremely long index of principal figures (mode by mode, qupai by qupai, listing “certain qupai, certain line → see certain act”), **a lookup table for the notation-setter that does not enter the skill**. But it proves one thing: **the “vocal personality” of a qupai is fixed in a few principal figures**, so changing the qupai = changing the principal figures = changing the emotional color; **the reason a jíqǔ (集曲 — a patchwork aria) must “take its first lines from the first lines of the main tune and its last lines from the last lines of the main tune” is precisely that the principal figures are mostly at the beginning and end of lines — disorder the order, and the principal figures scatter.**

**The same qupai with different words must have a different gongpu (vol. 3, p0239)**: “**When the gongpu for the same qupai differ from one another and cannot be made consistent, it is because the four tones and yin and yang of each character in the lyrics differ from one another.**” → **“Filling words” in the qupai system is not stuffing characters into a fixed melody; it is placing the four tones into a fixed framework of metrical patterns and melodic figures.** (At the end of the Tongzhi period, Yu Quyuan, Zhang Wenxiang in the Guangxu period, and others “imitated tanci (弹词 — plucked-string narrative song) and made actors force the gongpu of tanci onto their singing”; Wang Jilie judged this as “**literati who do not understand musical temperament presuming to be arbitrary**.”)

**There is another firsthand record of the same workflow in Ming–Qing chuanqi** (Wang Jilie vol. 4, p0345, recording Yuan Lingzhao): “**Lingzhao was a disciple of the Lan Garden; on flowery mornings and moonlit evenings he called for song and marked the beats; as soon as one lyric was off the draft he had the actors learn it, and within a fixed number of days it was presented**” — parallel to Kong Shangren's “whenever a piece was finished, I had it sung to the beat; if a single character was crooked, I revised it at once,” and Weng Ouhong's banqiang (板腔体, bǎnqiāngtǐ — the paired-line vocal system) workflow of “revise the words — compose the tune — ask Wang Yaoqing to vet the tune.”

### 16b The Distinction between “Qupu” and “Gongpu”

**Source**: Wang Jilie, 《螾庐曲谈》, vol. 3, ch. 1, the first sentence of the opening; scanned page p0209.

> “**What corrects the punctuation, distinguishes principal and padding characters (正衬, zhèngchèn), and adds metrical marks, giving the composer a standard, is called a ‘qupu’; what distinguishes the four tones and yin and yang, the high and low of melodic figures, and notes the gongche and beats at the side, so that the singer takes it as the norm, is called a ‘gongpu.’**”

| | Qupu | Gongpu |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | **A metrical handbook for filling words** | **A musical score for singing** |
| For whom | The playwright (the lyric-maker) | The actor / flute player (the singer) |
| Examples | The Imperially Commissioned Qupu; Rules for Southern Lyrics; Expanded and Corrected Northern Lyrics | The Nine Modes Compendium; Nashuying; Yinxiangtang; 《集成曲谱》 (Jicheng Qupu) |

Wang Jilie admits that usage confuses the two (p0210): “**The name ‘qupu’ has not been correct for a long time; this book too uses the name ‘qupu’ for what is really a gongpu — following convenience and custom, I have not had time to correct it.**”
→ **When answering “where do I look up Kunqu prosody?”, you must first ask whether the person wants the prosody for filling words (a qupu) or the score for singing (a gongpu) — both are called “qupu,” and confusing them will give the wrong book.**

### 16c Which Manuals to Consult

**Source**: Wang Jilie, vol. 2, ch. 1, 1.4 (p0119), 2.6–2.8 (p0137–p0140); vol. 3, 2.4 (p0213).

| Book | Wang Jilie's judgment |
|---|---|
| **《钦定曲谱》 (The Imperially Commissioned Qupu)** | **The practical first choice**: a lithographic edition is easy to buy; the Northern qu are taken from the Ming 《啸馀谱》 (The Xiaoyu Manual) and the Southern qu from Shen Jing's (沈璟) 《南曲谱》 (Southern Tune Manual); “the four tones are noted beside each character, and the metrical pattern, principal and padding characters, punctuation, and rhyme are all marked one by one. **It is indeed a book the lyric-writer cannot do without.**” **But the Northern qu do not mark beats**, and it has five further faults (see below) |
| **《北词广正谱》 (Expanded and Corrected Northern Lyrics; Li Yuanyu)** | The most careful and accurate for Northern qu; it notes where “rising and departing tones may not be shifted,” **and is the basis for checking wutou (务头 — the pivotal phrase of a qupai)**; “it circulates very little and is hard to buy” |
| **《南词定律》 (Rules for Southern Lyrics; Lü Shixiong et al.)** | The most careful and accurate for Southern qu, **with the most detailed metrical patterns**; equally hard to obtain |
| **《九宫大成南北词宫谱》 (The Great Compendium of Northern and Southern Tunes in Nine Modes)** | The most complete, “**but it is too elaborate and complex; the beginner reading it finds nowhere to begin, and its arrangement of modes by the twelve months is a self-made antiquity that only adds to later confusion**” |
| **《吟香堂》; 《纳书楹》 (Yinxiangtang; Nashuying)** | Among gongpu for old plays, **those with correct metrical patterns** (listed alongside 《集成曲谱》 [Jicheng Qupu]) |
| Gongpu copied by vulgar actors | “**Principal and added beats are not distinguished, and many beats are pulled out to save trouble; they are absolutely not to be relied on.**” |

**Wu Mei's choices (the two authorities side by side)**: for Northern qu he **recognizes only the Nine Modes Compendium** — “the Northern qupu in The Xiaoyu Manual **is absolutely not to be followed**” (Random Talks on Opera, “Methods of Northern Qu,” p0208); Random Talks lists as old Southern manuals “Shen Ning'an's 《南九宫谱》 (Southern Nine Modes) and **Niu Shaoya's 《南曲范》 (Models of Southern Qu)**,” while the General Treatise lists Shen Zijin's 《南词新谱》 (New Manual of Southern Lyrics); **the two books name different manuals**; his reason for choosing Rules for Southern Lyrics is that it is “the most carefully examined and corrected, **and is a palace edition, still easy to buy**” (notes `10-guqu-nanqu-zuofa.md` §0).

**The five faults of the Imperially Commissioned Qupu (Wang Jilie p0137–p0139, numbering as in the original)**
1. **Padding characters in Northern qu are not marked, and many are mixed in as principal characters** (for example, in the fourth line to the last line of 〈古水仙子〉 of the Huangzhong mode, the repeated rhyme characters are all padding characters left unmarked; so too the characters “吉丁丁当” (jīdīngdīngdāng, a clanging sound) at the head of each line of 〈笑和尚〉 in the Zhenggong mode);
2. **Many characters and lines are missing** (the first two lines of 〈赏花时〉 in Northern Xianlü, “A hundred-foot turtle mountain clusters the emerald smoke, ten thousand fathoms of rainbow light scatter brocade,” are missing; two lines including “Biyun Pavilion” are missing from 〈梁州第七〉 in Northern Nanlü, etc.);
3. **It includes variant forms and omits the current standard pattern** (for example, under 〈山麻秸〉 of Southern Yuediao it includes 〈豆叶黄〉 of Xianlü into Shuangdiao, while the standard pattern is lost);
4. **At the end of one qu it wrongly joins another** (for example, the eight lines from “chìjǐn” onward in 〈玉交枝〉 of Northern Nanlü, the twenty lines from “wǒ zěnshēng” onward in 〈神缠曲〉 of Northern Shuangdiao, and the urgent qu including 〈满江红〉 of Southern Zhenggong);
5. **Qu that are utterly different are mistaken for one tune because their names are the same** (for example, 〈山麻秸〉 of Southern Yuediao and 〈豆叶黄〉 of Xianlü into Shuangdiao; 〈白鹤子〉 of Zhenggong and 〈好观音〉 of Dashi, etc.).
> “These are only the main points. If the student compares and collates the Expanded and Corrected Northern Lyrics, Rules for Southern Lyrics, and the Nine Modes Compendium, he will know in detail what is right and wrong, what is gained and lost.”

**Editions: whether principal and padding characters are clearly distinguished determines whether one can fill words from the book (p0140)**: old woodblock editions mostly do not distinguish principal from padding, or distinguish them incorrectly; qupai are fond of marking alternate names, and errors are especially many (〈梁州序〉 and 〈泣颜回〉 are mistakenly given as 〈好事近〉; 〈雁儿落带得胜令〉 is marked only as 〈雁儿落〉; 〈沽美酒带太平令〉 only as 〈沽美酒〉). **The Dong (Wujin) edition is reputed to be fine, but it has not corrected the ancients' errors**; the Liu family edition of Guixi mostly keeps strictly to the ancients' line and ink; **the Moling Bookshop edition is carefully collated, with principal and padding clearly distinguished, and truly stands above the Dong editions**; the Hanfenlou typeset edition “is carefully collated throughout, corrects the qupai names, and distinguishes principal and padding, **so that the lyric-writer may take it as a model**.”

### 16d Which Editions to Read

**Source**: Wang Jilie, vol. 2, ch. 1, 1.3; scanned page p0118. **This is the most concentrated series of judgments on chuanqi through the ages in the whole book, transcribed as is.**

| Work | Wang Jilie's judgment |
|---|---|
| 《元人百种》 (The Hundred Plays of the Yuan) | The best of Northern qu; their modes all fit the rules and their diction is invariably superb; **but the spoken dialogue is too blunt and the staging is especially under-considered** |
| 《琵琶记》 (The Lute) | The song text is quite good and fits the rules, **but acts such as “Eating Chaff” and “Cutting Her Hair” repeatedly change mode and rhyme and are not fit to be a model** |
| 《荆钗记》 (The Thorn Hairpin) | Fits the rules, but the song text is mediocre |
| 《幽闺记》 (The Secluded Boudoir) | Has some fine lines, **but many of its qupai are rare and unfamiliar** |
| 《白兔记》; 《杀狗记》 (The White Rabbit; Killing a Dog) | Though highly reputed, their language is uniformly poor and not fit to look at |
| Yuming's Four Dreams (玉茗四梦; Tang Xianzu 汤显祖) | Their diction is the crown of Ming chuanqi, **but offenses against mode and tune are not few; the spoken dialogue is omitted or sketchy, and the staging is especially under-considered** |
| 《浣纱记》; 《明珠记》; 《玉玦记》; 《红梨记》 (Washing the Silks; The Pearl; The Jade Ring; The Red Pear) | **Song and speech are both beautiful; among Ming chuanqi they are the best** |
| 《燕子笺》; 《春灯谜》 (The Swallow's Letter; The Spring Lantern Riddle) | Staging is especially ingenious and the lyrics are novel, only a little too sharp and cutting (Ye Huaiting mocked them as “opening the way for Liweng's bad letters”) |
| 《笠翁十种》 (Liweng's Ten Plays; Li Yu) | **Ingenious in conception; for actors to dress up and perform, they are quite moving — this is their strength** |
| 《桃花扇》 (The Peach Blossom Fan) | **The spoken dialogue is the most polished, and the lyrics are good too; but the level and oblique tones are often off, and the padding characters are not well placed — these are its shortcomings** |
| The works of Wu Shiqu (吴石渠; Wu Bing 吴炳) | Rich and lush in literary talent |
| Jiang Xinyu's nine plays (蒋心馀九种) | They learn from Yuming but are more careful than Yuming in prosody; all are good works |
| **《长生殿》 (The Palace of Eternal Life)** | “**Diction, structure, and staging all excel, and moreover the modes fit the rules and the spoken dialogue is polished; every beauty is present, and there is nothing to criticize**” |

**The order for learning qu (original text)**: “So one who would learn to write qu should **first read The Palace of Eternal Life**, then the Hundred Plays of the Yuan, Yuming's Four Dreams, The Lute, The Secluded Boudoir, Washing the Silks, The Pearl, The Jade Ring, The Red Pear, The Swallow's Letter, The Spring Lantern Riddle, The Peach Blossom Fan, Wu Shiqu's five plays, Liweng's ten plays, and Zangyuan's nine plays, **learning what they do well and discarding what they do badly**; then more than half the way of writing qu will have been thought through.”

**Two opinions of Wu Mei, set side by side**: ① “Those who learn from Yuming **must read many Yuan plays and must not read only the ‘Four Dreams’** — this is what is meant by ‘aim above the mark and attain only the middle.’” (An Outline of Chinese Opera p0265 `[to be checked]`); ② reading the Four Dreams, “**one should learn only their writing, and must not imitate their method**” (Random Talks on Opera, “Methods of Southern Qu,” p0188).
**The genealogy of schools Wu Mei gives (p0265–p0266 `[to be checked]`)**: Yuming (Tang Xianzu) → Canhua (Wu Bing, implicit and reserved) / Baizi (the line of Meng Chengshun, sharp and pointed) → Zangyuan (Jiang Shiquan) → Yiqing (Huang Xieqing) → Tanyuan (Yang Ensou). **The farther from the source, the weaker** — this is the implicit rule of his ranking.

**The boundary between refined and vulgar (Wang Jilie p0117)**:
> “Poetry and ci are both suited to refinement, but qu has places where it should be vulgar and places where it should be refined. **The refined is not unrelieved elegance; it must come out from a transcendent brush. The vulgar is not unrelieved coarseness; it must contain a meaning that lingers.** Therefore piling up allusions with no spark of wit, and using vulgar words that injure elegance, are both unsuitable for writing qu.”
**The theory of běnsè (Wang Jilie p0187)**: “**《汉宫秋》 (The Autumn Palace of Han) is in the mouth of Emperor Yuan and Zhaojun, so it uses beautiful and delicate words; 《陈抟高卧》 (Chen Tuan Asleep on High) is in the mouth of a hermit, so it uses transcendent and free language; the mouth of a butcher or householder therefore never makes clever speech. Yet where it does not make clever speech is certainly běnsè, and even where it does make clever speech it is still běnsè.**” — běnsè = fitting the identity of the speaker, **not the same as unrelieved coarseness**.
**How to judge whether a qupai-system script is a “play” (Wang Jilie vol. 4, p0314)**: are the song text and spoken dialogue in the voices of the characters? **Narrative writing in the author's voice is not a play; it is storytelling.**
