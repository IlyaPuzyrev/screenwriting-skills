## 1. Seven Methods for Breaking Story: Master Table (Source, Method, and Anchors for Headcount / Weeks / Beat Counts)

| Method | Source | One-Sentence Method | Quantitative Anchors |
|---|---|---|---|
| **35-beat whiteboard + scissors and tape** | Chase, Sopranos foreword p0010–11 | List beats one line at a time on the whiteboard → an assistant types them up in separate A/B/C columns → **cut them apart, then "marry" them back together in episode order with clear tape** → retype as the outline | About **35 beats per episode**: roughly 13 each for A and B, 5–6 for C, a few for D; about three months from start of writing to start of shooting |
| **Ten chairs + six weeks to lay out a season** | Wells (ER), Douglas ch. 5 | Starting the first week of June, the whole staff spends six weeks "pounding out" the season's turning points and act-out landing points → one writer is assigned to write the treatment → full-room notes → revise → **another round of notes** → only then write the script | **10 chairs** around the table = 4 senior writers + 4 staff + 1 researcher + Wells; **about eight weeks per episode**; notes meetings Monday, Wednesday, and Friday, four to five hours each |
| **Four-act grid + colored index cards** | Douglas ch. 4 | Logline → two tests (credibility / rooting interest) → three tent-poles → work backward → **one color of card per story line**, each line laid out separately, then merged | About 28 scenes/hour: **A story 12–16 beats, B story 8–12, C story 3–8**; 40 cards on the A story = too many fragments, or the story won't fit in one episode |
| **Mini boot camp + five-episode pipeline** | Lindelof (Lost), Douglas Chinese ed. p0234–235 | **About 9 people spend a full 3 weeks talking only about "blue sky"** to produce the blueprint; during production, a 4-hour meeting every day before going to set | **There are always five episodes in the pipeline**: one being broken / one being outlined / one being written / one being shot; a **full-time bible keeper** (Gregg Nations) answers "that was established in season three, so it's hard to overturn" |

## 2. Prebble's Four Room Rules (Verbatim from the Succession Writers' Room)

**Prebble's four room rules (the most directly copyable)**: ① **How to open a pitch**—don't say "This is shit but –" (which amounts to telling people not to listen while asking them to comfort you first); instead say **"This is the trashy version" / "I know this is vivid, but…" / "Not this, but maybe – Something like this."** Because Succession is a show that withholds, **starting from the pulpiest version and then pulling back is how you keep the dramatic flavor**. ② **Two ordering rules** (settled by the argument over "Would Logan sell the company?"): **"An appeal to reality over the mythic was always the most powerful pitch in the room, only occasionally losing out to whatever was the most funny."** ③ **"Maybe" culture**: "stay open until you absolutely have to close down the narrative," so as not to miss "that last possible moment of magic"; the "Maybe Connor does this" left in the script is an invitation to the director and actors. ④ **The room's real product is "what this show is not"**—the generation chain for E7 runs "public suicide" → "performance art that fakes a suicide" → finally "he decides not to go onstage, not to get on the cross, not to sing"; **"Pitching is the way in which you work out what the show isn't, as well as what it is."**

## 3. The Leave-Behind: Physical Specifications and Two Versions

**3.1 leave-behind (story area / treatment / one-pager).** Spec (Goldberg ch. 8 p0067, original): **"Not the whole story, one page tops, three paragraphs with the beginning, middle, and end, like jacket copy on a book. Punchy, well written."** Function: to let the other side relive the excitement of the pitch afterward; the producer also needs it to pitch again to the platform. **The same story must be written in two versions** (Appendix D introduction p0169, the most overlooked point): **"The network takes it on faith that the character conflicts will be spelled out in the beat sheet, but a producer won't grant a freelancer the same benefit of the doubt."** → **For the platform/financier, write "this episode's conflict + hook"; for the production company/showrunner, write "this episode's conflict + the one thread in the regular character relationships that gets touched."** A conspiracy show should be written as **a secret structure rather than the audience's order of discovery** (Appendix F, seaQuest): use **"What we will discover is that…"** and **"What X doesn't know is that…"** to make clear "who is manipulating whom and who doesn't know what," and state that **at series level only character relationships may change; the power configuration must be restored**.

## 4. Beat Sheet / Outline: Terminology, Length, Format, and Four Hard Annotations

**3.2 beat sheet / outline (several names for the same object).** Terminology (Douglas ch. 4): **treatment** is theoretically a dialogue-free prose version of the whole story, but "nobody writes treatments like that"; in television, being asked for a treatment means being asked for a few pages of summary; **beat sheet** describes major events act by act without listing specific scenes; **outline** lists the scenes actually to be written. The heading may be `Revised Outline` / `Rough Beat Sheet by` / `Treatment` / `Story Area by`—**follow the production's name for it; don't argue about the label**.
- **Length and deadline** (Goldberg p0088–89, original): **"usually six or seven scenes per act, four acts per episode"**; scene headings included, one paragraph of description per scene; **6–20 pages**; **the WGA allows 7 working days**. Douglas's format target: **one page per act, about 4 pages for a one-hour outline**; scene numbers restart at #1 in each act; each "step" must be **a scene with a protagonist, a goal, and opposition**; divide beats by content, not by time or place (new conflict in the same location = new beat).
- **Four uses**: to test whether an idea really contains a script ("good ideas often fall apart when they take shape") / so you don't have to worry about plot moves while writing the script / to reassure the producer / **a production tool—the line producer uses it to scout locations, build sets, and budget**. The fourth means: **every scene must specify a location and filmable action.**
- **Douglas's four hard annotations**: ① Enter each scene as close to the conflict as possible; don't let characters wander and eat up screen time; ② **animal action is expensive**; spend it only when dramatically necessary; ③ from the squad room you can't see exteriors—if you want to write driving, change the slug line, otherwise cut it; ④ **"the protagonist's fear of heights can't be seen on screen"—if it can't go in the script, don't put it in the outline; if you do write it, say how it will be played**. Also: **What day of the story is this? Settle it at the outline stage; don't wait until you've written 50 pages** (typing exposes "this scene reads as daytime before and after, but it's actually night," "the character shouldn't be at work at night").
- **The temp dialogue dilemma** (p0090): including dialogue shows conflict, but bad temp dialogue makes the producer worry about your dialogue ability; **the worst is when it appears verbatim in the script** (which means you didn't rethink the scene when writing it). The reverse: one EP was furious because temp dialogue tossed off casually in the room didn't make it into the script—"these words tossed out in the room were precious as gold." → Instruction (Sheldon Bull, Smith p0270): **"If producers pitch a joke, they will expect to find that joke in your outline. Write it down verbatim and put it in."**
- **The outline is a contractual paid step**: it generates "story by" credit; a contract with cutoffs may end here; it may be **"locked"**; on low-budget shows, prep may even begin from the outline.

## 5. First Draft: The 14-Day Method, the Half-Hour of Prep, and Draft-Name Discipline

**3.3 First draft.** **Don't make sweeping changes to an assignment script without notice** (Douglas ch. 4): the author once changed the ending of Act Four on her own initiative, and the producer called, upset—the original ending was his idea; "**This was about respect**." Rule: if while writing you find a problem the outline didn't expose, **solve it, staying as close as possible to the spirit of the outline; if you depart too far from the approved beats or need a major restructure, call first**; but don't call at every bump—"If they wanted to wrestle with the everyday problems of writing a script, they would have written it themselves instead of hiring you." (Goldberg p0094)
- **The 14-day method**: deliver the first draft two weeks after the outline is approved: **28 beats ÷ 2 scenes/day = 14 days**. Each day: in the morning, read and revise yesterday's work → treat today's scene as "the most important piece of writing of your life" → write two or three pages **and then stop** → after lunch, get away from it → in the afternoon, reread and revise, then write the second scene, and stop. **Keep the disagreement**: another type gets up at 4 a.m. and writes in a frenzy without looking back, treating it as raw material to cut later—**painter vs. sculptor, no superiority either way**.
- **The half-hour of prep before starting** (Goldberg p0094–95): Is the car scene's slug `INT. CAR` / `INT. MARK'S CAR` / `INT. THE MUSTANG`? Is the dialogue heading `DR. SLOAN` / `MARK` / `MARK SLOAN`? "These may seem like small details to you, but they aren't." → **Before starting, make a list of standing sets and locations and how each is written, and check the spelling of every character name and the usage of every dialogue heading**.
- **Draft name = draft stage** (Miller ch. 9): **rough draft → writer's draft (several versions) → (some write a reader's draft) → first draft → endless rewrite**. Three hard rules: the primary task is simply to put words on the page; **you don't have to write in order**; **don't edit while you write**. When you're stuck, go back to character wants—"Every scene will have at least two characters with different wants."

## 6. Second Draft, Polish, Colored Pages, and Keeping Drafts

**3.4 Second draft, polish, colored pages.**
- **Revising ≠ rewriting** (Douglas ch. 4): fixing spelling, tightening dialogue, cutting a paragraph are part of writing; **rewriting means rethinking structure or even character**—"put the script down first and put everything on the table," otherwise you "turn into a pretzel trying to fit a structure around scenes that don't belong." **Polish** is a small fix (often dialogue polish), not restructuring, new characters, or a new arc; **"cultural wash"** is also called dialogue polish (bringing in a second writer to make characters from a given background sound natural; **usually uncredited**).
- **Colored pages** (Goldberg p0107): each revision is printed on dated colored pages, in the fixed order **pink → blue → green…**, with every change marked with an asterisk. The implication is that **someone is always still giving notes**—producers → studio → network → production after approval → actors. The Sopranos pilot went from Production Draft 7/16/97 through **6 revisions** to the Final Shooting Script of 8/25/97.
- **Keep all drafts** (someone always says "I liked that line better in the previous draft"); Miller's method is a master catch-all document called **cut-outs**; anything cut gets pasted into it.
- **Writing long vs. writing short (the disagreement appears in Section 9)**: Armstrong writes **70+ pages** per episode (the norm is 60; some shows squeeze to 50), "I like to write long and I encourage my fellow writers to write long. **I rewrite long.**"; Kelley is the opposite: "If I can say something in four words, I will never use five" (Blum p0253).

## 7. Spec Series-Selection Rules and a Tone-Matching Case

**4.1 The rules for choosing a show to spec.** Definition (Goldberg ch. 5 p0043): "No one asked for it. No one's paying for it. No one wants to read it."—but "**it's the only way in**." TV specs rarely exceed 65 pages: **"The fat ones are always the last to be picked."**
- **Five rules for choosing a show** (ch. 6 p0051–52): choose a show **that will last** (a spec for a canceled show is "about as welcome as month-old lox"); choose a show **that won't change drastically** (don't spec season one; "the staff themselves haven't found the way to write it yet"); **avoid heavily serialized shows** (if there's a long arc, write a stand-alone, "one that could fit into any season"); choose a show **the industry is watching**; **dare to break all of the above** (in the early 1990s a pair of comedy writers surfaced from a pile of Cosby / Night Court specs with an *I Love Lucy* spec and landed on the #1 comedy).
- **Two "almosts"**: **some shows won't let you shine** (Walker, Texas Ranger has black-and-white characters and dialogue used only for exposition)—**"It's not your job to write the show you think it should be; it's your job to write the best possible version of the show that is."**; **some shows nobody wants to read** (readers favor shows they've seen).
- **Tone match matters more than "the show everyone agrees is good"** (the Martial Law hiring case, p0050): agents sent Homicide and The Practice (too dark), Ally McBeal and Sex and the City (also wrong); in the end they had to say outright, "send us Xenas. Send us Hercules. Send us Buffys." The writers hired came from Star Trek: Voyager, Hercules, and La Femme Nikita.
- **Douglas's opposite rule (the disagreement is preserved)**: **"DON'T SPEC THE SERIES YOU PLAN TO PITCH."** (English ed. ch. 6, rule 2)—producers know their own minefields (they've heard enough dog stories, enough floaters in the pool, and two actors had a fight yesterday so you can't write them falling in love), and **an outsider can't possibly know**. Evidence: in 2010, producers from procedurals to sci-fi to family drama were all reading **Breaking Bad** specs, "they were reading for ability with characters"; Souders used a **Mad Men spec** to hire for Smallville ("action writing doesn't matter on our show… **what we care about is character writing**"). But the "fairy tale" in the same chapter is about a woman writer who broke in precisely by **submitting specs to the same show over and over**, and Douglas himself notes, "Okay, that's opposite the lesson in the fairy tale." → **Specing other shows is the standard route; repeatedly specing the same show works only if you have the stamina to "trade three scripts for a note."**

## 8. Researching a Show: Shot-by-Shot Title Breakdown, the Stasis Question, and the Angle Only You Can Bring

**4.2 Researching a show.**
- **Method one: break down the main title shot by shot** (Goldberg ch. 9 p0069–76). A main title costs $40,000–100,000 and uses only 10–12 shots chosen from thousands of feet of film—**each one is the EP's understanding of the show**; three types: **format sequence** (voice-over/text states directly what the show is, Law & Order), **mood sequence** (tone, X-Files), **character sequence** (Hunter). The test: **"Does your story fulfill what the main title promises? If it doesn't, you're pitching the wrong stories."** Full readings of the five examples are in Goldberg ch. 9 p0069–76.
- **Method two: ask what would surface in stasis** (Douglas, rule 3). Don't use story lines already laid out in the show—those arcs will be over before you finish writing, and **the big changes that turn the series are executive decisions, not something a freelancer does**. The right question: **"What impulse or issue would surface from these characters in stasis (the state in which they behave normally)?"** Then dig into "**why they do it**." Companions: **catch the voices**—"ask what separates Vampire Bill's word choice and syntax from Vampire Eric's in True Blood; what separates Peggy from Pete in Mad Men. **The differences are on the page.**"; and **"Be unpredictable within the world of the show"** (example: writing about breast cancer, **give the disease to a man**).
- **Method three: bring an angle only you can bring**. You're a marine biologist specing Dexter → he often goes out on the water; what could the creatures in those waters add to a conspiracy? While Friday Night Lights was still on the air, **the best specs all came from people who had lived in Texas and knew the rattlesnake and dust-storm lore**—"You could smell the authenticity in the pages."

## 9. Pitching an Episode: Quantity and Length, Delivery, Six Producers' Room Discipline, and Three Outcomes

**4.3 Pitching an episode.** **"A pitch is an audition."** (Goldberg p0061) Everyone in the room would rather be elsewhere, and each has three other things to do—**"So you've got about two minutes to win them over."** (p0062)
- **How many to bring, how long each (three books merged)**: Goldberg—**three solid ideas plus half a dozen half-formed notions**; Smith (ch. 17 p0260) disagrees—**prepare 10–15 springboards** (the producer will cut one or two because "it's been done elsewhere" and one or two more because "we've done that or are about to"); Blum (p0255) gives the length—"a springboard is **three to five sentences**… never more than six sentences at most." → **For quantity, use Smith; for length, use Blum: pitch 3–5 sentences first, move on if it's cut, and come back to the long "several paragraphs" version if someone bites. A pitch meeting usually lasts only 30–45 minutes.**
- **What to pitch and how**: only broad strokes—**"You might start with the teaser, then launch into the major character arcs and the act breaks."**; the **Full Monty rule** ("these unemployed steelworkers strip to make money, but they all have bodies like mine"), don't start with "there's a town in England with a bad economy and the factories have all closed…"; **"What happens next?" is the sound of success.** Two bad pitches: the **rewind type** ("the patient's name is Fred… did I mention he has a dog? Let me go back…") and the **beat-by-beat type** ("Joe walks into the hospital and says 'Hello, Dr. Sloan,' there's coffee on Sloan's lapel…", reaching the point 45 minutes later). Matt Williams's version of the same rule: **"pitch the sizzle, not the steak."**
- **Room discipline** (six producers' advice from the room, point by point): **don't be married to what you pitch** (p0066—the producer will change the premise, the killer, the type of disaster on the spot; catch it first; if the suggestion really wrecks everything, **explain why**, otherwise "you have just talked yourself out of $27,000"); **never lose your temper** (the author once had a supervising producer get up mid-pitch, "You keep pitching—**I'm going to the bathroom to throw up**"; he laughed and said "Guess you don't like that one," went on to the next, and **got the assignment**); **follow their lead**, especially the person talking loudest; **"Never appear desperate or needy"**; **never open by saying an idea is great or flawed** ("many a writer has sold the weakest, lamest idea tossed out in a pitch"); **when someone bites on an idea, stop pitching the others at once**; **the worst opening is "Have you done the one about…?"** (once you ask, they stop listening to anything else; on the other hand, hitting a story they've done **doesn't count against you**—it proves "they know what our characters would do"); **do a pre-pitch first** (call a lower-level writer beforehand and say "I'm thinking roughly about…"); two from Landau 2e—make eye contact with **everyone** in the room (fail to look at one junior executive and he may sink your pitch after you leave), and **stop talking when you're done** ("**Finish the pitch and don't buy it back**… **Do not give in to this temptation**").
- **What to pitch (at the story level)** (ch. 10 p0077–83): **"If it's a detective show, don't think about the case. If it's an occult show, don't think about the monster. If it's a medical show, don't think about the illness." What then? Conflict.** Three starting questions: **How do my characters get involved in this story? What is at stake for them? How is this different from the story we told last week?** The story must be character-based and conflict-driven, and give the protagonist a **character turn** before the end. Example: the Matlock pitch that sold had only two and a half acts of character turns (he's appointed a temporary judge and has to sentence a man he believes innocent to death; at the end of act two he goes to death row and says "I'd like to help you," and the other man says "Haven't you helped me enough? You are sending me to the electric chair." He smiles: "But you are innocent and now I am going to defend you on appeal.")—**at the pitch the writer had no idea what the case was or who the real killer was**: "We had the major story moves and the important conflicts for the lead—that's all that was important to the pitch."
- **Three outcomes** (Smith p0268–70): ① They want time to think → leave the leave-behind, and call your agent on the way out to have him follow up ("suggest it yourself. **Strongly.**"); ② A flat no → the only rescue is to **ask to pitch again in a day or two** and ask which directions they want to explore ("two strikes will leave a bad taste"); ③ **"Go write an outline" = a deal; stop pitching immediately**.
- **After the deal, you're a stenographer** (Sheldon Bull, Smith p0270): **"When you are a freelance writer in a story meeting with producers and staff writers, you are not an equal. You are a stenographer."** Companions: do your homework first; come in with story ideas and jokes; before you leave, pin down the delivery date; **ask the producer's assistant for several past outlines from the show as format samples**.
- **Why you aren't sent off alone to crack story** (p0087, the list of production realities you don't know): the per-episode budget; how many days each for interior stage work and location work; which actors' contracts limit how many episodes they appear in per season; one actor works only three days a week; some episodes must be shot in six days instead of the usual seven. And staff writers don't break story alone either—**the whole group together, sharing an understanding, so that anyone can answer for the freelancer when the EP walks into the editing room, and the answer will match the EP's** ("because their jobs are to second-guess and mimic him").

## 10. Notes in Practice: The Phone Code Table, Metabolize, Warm/Cold Reads, and the Note-Giver

**Phone code table** (p0101): "Nice work" / "Dynamite" = good; **"Really good first draft" = may love it or hate it, but there will be a lot of notes**; **"Thanks for getting it done so fast" = take cover; they think it's garbage**.

**5.3 Metabolize: don't execute the notes; digest them first** (Landau 2e ch. 14, Berger on Le Bureau). Canal+'s note went to the producer Berger, not the showrunner Éric Rochant; he spent about 90 minutes on the phone and wrote **six pages of notes**, "it was so brutal that I hung up and went for a walk in the Tuileries to sort things out." Éric's process: ① **"Let's take the time to go through every word and every meaning of what Canal+ are saying."** (done over a weekend) ② enter the process he calls **"metabolization"**—"He had to **metabolize** everything, to integrate the notes and remarks on the script." ③ then **raise his own questions**, going back and forth several times. ④ the second version of the pilot "became stronger, tighter, and clearer." → **First go through every word and every layer of meaning (without arguing), then digest (without copying), then come back with your own questions, several times.**

**5.4 Find your own readers first: warm read → cold read** (Miller ch. 10). A **warm read** reader **knows your project**; the first choice is **other writers** ("The notes writers give you will be different from notes other people, even people in the business, will give you"); the discipline is **don't defend the work, "Just listen and take notes."** A **cold read** is **having someone with no knowledge read it** ("The reader knows nothing")—**from the start, set aside two or three of your smartest, most ruthless friends for cold reads and tell them nothing**; the **question you must ask** is **"Ask your cold readers where they think your story is going."** (if they can answer it = pass; if they can't or get it wrong = go back and find where it went off track; then ask them **why** they think so and **which elements helped them most understand your world**).
- **Three rules for filtering notes**: ① **look for repetition** (if several readers say similar things, there really is a problem); ② **look for what you already agree with** (if a reader points to a weakness you already knew about, you must address it); ③ **look for the note that makes you deeply uncomfortable**—"The note itself may not be precise, but **your mind is rebelling against the note for some reason**."
- Two companion methods: **read aloud** ("If you stumble over dialogue or description, change it."; print a paper copy every time and mark it on paper, to resist editing while reading); **pull one thread and fix it alone** (pull the scenes of an entire B story into a separate document, fix them, and put them back). **To fix a logic problem, don't fix the scene where the problem appears**: go back one or two scenes—"That's like saying if we put a Band-Aid here, it'll fix this broken leg." The root cause is "**Whenever something serves your plot, it will feel forced.**"

**5.5 The one giving notes.** **Anonymous reads** (Souders, Smallville, 2011): when a submission comes in, **tear off the first page and number it** → send it to several readers → **at least two people read every script** → only those that get a "+" reach the showrunner's desk (the motive: the pressure of agents calling to demand "put this person's script on top" was too great). **The source of a note may know more than you think**: "the note you get may come from someone who **knows what happens four episodes from now**, regardless of whether they like what you wrote"; "if you think your script doesn't need another draft, you're being childish." **Embrace the rewrite** (Eric Trueheart): "New writers want to get it perfect the first time… and you never, ever do. **The rewrite is your biggest friend.**" Miller: professional TV writers "**rip the script apart, write entirely new sections, rearrange sequences—we do heavy lifting**… **If you don't like to rewrite, this isn't the job for you.**" The seven rules for the note-taking side are in SKILL.md, Section 5, 5.1.

## 11. The Rank Ladder, Shelf-Life Notes, the Step Deal, and Three Definitions of Freelance

> **Currency flag**: the figures come from **2003 (Goldberg) / the 2007 Chinese translation and the 2011 English 3rd edition (Douglas) / 2009–2011 (Smith) / 1999–2001 (Blum)**. **All amounts and week counts are out of date** (WGA minimums are renegotiated every three years); **the structure (the seven-rung title ladder, the two cutoffs in a step deal, the 50% credit rule, staff writers writing the show's episodes, and the freelance slot as an audition) is still valid.** The full title table follows.

| Level | English | Chinese Title | Key Points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | freelance writer | 自由编剧 | writes only one episode; **does not attend story meetings, does not watch screenings, has no office on the show** |
| 2 | staff writer ("baby writer") | 试用编剧 | **someone who has never been in a writers' room**; a season usually gives them 1 episode (paired with a senior writer to "supervise," but that person is ready to write it for you at any moment); **up or out at the end of the season**; their script fee **offsets their salary** |
| 3 | story editor / executive story editor | 故事编辑 | goes through every stage of one episode; a season usually means writing 2 original episodes, plus polish and rewrites |
| 4 | producer / co-producer | 制作人 | three kinds of people mixed together (the on-set producer type / the writer who came up / the hybrid); if shooting starts tomorrow and the script has a problem, **he may be the one who stays up all night fixing it while the credit still goes to the original writer** |
| 5 | supervising producer | 监督制作人 | **at the break-in stage, this is the title you're most likely pitching to**—usually has the power to give you an assignment and guide the script (the deal needs EP approval) |
| 6 | creative consultant | 创意顾问 | "a mysterious title"; given to high-prestige writers, **no need to be in the office**, may not write or attend meetings at all—or he may be the real showrunner |
| 7 | executive producer / showrunner | 执行制作人／剧目管理人 | see Section 1 |

**step deal and its two cutoffs** (Douglas Chinese ed. p0200–201): **the first comes after the story**—if the story doesn't work, or they don't think you can deliver a high-quality script, the deal **ends**, and someone else writes the script from your story or even writes a new story; **the second comes after the first draft**—triggered by **your failure to capture the characters' "voices," or to express what the series is about, or simply by bad writing** (flat or purely expository dialogue, scenes with no focus, a lack of dramatic tension). **The 50% credit rule**: if the story is basically yours → you keep story credit; **if 50% of what you wrote remains in the final draft → shared credit**; if the new writer changes more than 50% → **you lose it**. **The total of a step deal exceeds a package price by design, as a reward** (the option has to be picked up for you to reach the next step). Goldberg calls it **"cut you off at story"**—"We aren't going to invest $27,000 in you unless we are convinced you can do the job." (p0089) **Sitcoms have only one cutoff** (Smith p0270): after the story outline is delivered, and "few sitcom producers actually cut writers off"; read the contract to see whether you're hired for "story and teleplay" or **"story with option for teleplay"** (the latter pays a little more because it's the option that allows them to cut you mid-way).

**Is freelance a career or an audition (all three together make the full industry picture)**: Douglas ch. 5—freelancing was once a lifestyle; ch. 6—**"the freelance work now is really an audition (audition scripts) for getting into the writers' room"**, and **much of it is rewriting rather than original work** (reworking from an outline / the story works but the script needs a rewrite / just dialogue polish), "**Say yes. Think of it as dues.**"; Souders (2004)—**"we have two freelance episodes this year, and we give them to the assistants."**

## 12. The Real Reasons People Get Replaced or Can't Get In, the Seven Sins in Full, and Room Politics

**The real reasons people get replaced or can't get in.** The basis for hiring/firing (Goldberg Introduction p0018) is almost always **one script, sometimes one page of one script**, at most two scripts plus a half-hour interview. **Casting a writing room** (p0124): "Casting a writing room is a delicate process and is **just as important as casting your actors**." What Goldberg wants: besides talent, **tough, confident, willing to fight for an idea in the room, willing to challenge the showrunner; able to produce an eleventh idea after ten have been shot down**. Two counterexamples: a story editor on a hit show whose scripts were excellent and were shot almost as written was not renewed because she was "**too enthusiastic**" (that EP wanted a quiet room where he could think aloud and the staff would take notes); a young woman writer with a good script who spoke softly and looked down in the interview—"**She just wasn't tough enough**." → **The "right personality" for a room varies by showrunner; in an interview, first judge what kind of room this is.**

**Douglas's seven sins ("The Staff from Hell," ch. 5; she says she committed all of them)**: ① **Don't distance yourself from the writers' group**—the veterans all pick rooms close to the showrunner's office; when he steps out he sees them, finds them for last-minute rewrites, and gossip reaches their ears first. ② **Don't mix your personal life with work**—the signal that you've crossed the line is "a discomfort in the room, or the head writer saying 'let's move on'." ③ **Don't keep other plans**—"driving off the lot for lunch = you'll be late in the afternoon." ④ **Don't hide at home to write**—she wrote a beautiful first draft at home two weeks early and came back to find that the previous episode had killed off the character she needed for a turn, a key location had been vetoed by the network, two actors were dating, and an intern had taken her office; "**Stay connected, even if you get virtually nothing written all day and have to work all night at home.**" ⑤ **Don't treat your script as precious**—"If you're a good enough writer to be on the staff you're skilled enough to rewrite… **If you don't, someone else will.**" ⑥ **Don't disparage the room's culture**—the smarter move is not to join on their terms but to **find another shared interest with at least one person and build an ally**. ⑦ **Don't stay on a show that doesn't suit you**—"misery is not an essential rung on the ladder"; you need not just credits but **advocates** (the next producer will certainly call the previous one, and will even ask other writers in the room "what was it like working with you"); the test: the show is good enough and you still write well in a bad atmosphere → stay and build credits; **your writing is declining → talk it through and leave** (ideally negotiating a gracious recommendation).

**Smith's additions on room politics** (ch. 18 p0276–77): before the roundtable, read the script you're going to revise and take notes; don't improvise on the spot; **humiliate someone in front of their boss and he will come for you**; observe first, then open up; **don't talk behind people's backs** (listening to someone else do it is also tacit agreement); ally yourself upward if possible; **the most important rule is exactly Douglas's #4: don't work at home.**

## 13. Everything Costs Money: The Cat-to-Dog Line-Item Account, Page-Count Economics, and a Case That Works

**7.1 Everything costs money, and locations are the first thing changed.** The account for `EXT. STREET – DAY  A CAT ambles off the sidewalk and is run over by a car.` (p0127–129): a trained show-biz cat plus handler; a car driven by a non-regular character can't use the show's picture car, so you need a rental plus a stunt driver; street permits, location fees, off-duty police for traffic. After the line producer negotiated item by item, the EP's conclusion was one line: **"Make it a dog."** (cat $5,000, dog $800, and no trained cat could be found anywhere in L.A. County; Isis became the dog-form Anubis, and the character's gender was changed throughout the script.)
- **Page-count economics**: an episode shoots an average of **7–12 pages a day**; a company move (rigging, striking, loading, driving, unloading, rigging again) easily eats a quarter of the day → combine scenes / change to a third location / put everything on standing sets. **The test question: "are the locations central to what the scenes are about, or are they just decoration?"**
- **A case that works** (West Wing "The Drop-In," p0130): Sam yells in Toby's office and slams the door, then the next scene continues the argument in an upscale bar—illogical, but that bar is the bar of the Regal Biltmore, **"five seconds by elevator" from C.J.'s location in the same episode**. Sorkin added a few bar-specific lines and moved the scene there; **it works because the scene is about the two men's emotions and politics' demands on idealism, not about location**.

## 14. The Budget-Forced Bottle Episode: The Economic Model and Transformation of West Wing "17 People"

**7.3 The bottle episode forced by budget: treat the limitation as a signal that "it's time to advance the main story."** West Wing "17 People" (S2E18). Sorkin lays out the economic model in the episode's preface: **Warner used deficit financing to cover the gap between what NBC paid and the actual cost**, expecting to reach about **100 episodes**, at which point the per-episode value in syndication would exceed cost—"**until then every episode loses money**"; the real source of overruns was "**it takes us a little longer to shoot and that means overtime and that's the real money**." So Warner ordered: the next episode would use **no guest cast, no locations, no new sets, no extras and no film**. Sorkin's transformation (the line most worth copying): **"In other words, I got to write a play. It was time for Bartlet to start coming clean about his MS."**

## 15. Actors: Vetoes, Availability, Bodies, and Writing for Actors

**7.4 Actors: vetoes, availability, bodies, and writing for actors.**
- **An actor can veto an entire story 24 hours before shooting.** Martial Law "Sammo Blammo" (p0097–99): the original setup had Grace going undercover as an arms dealer and Sammo playing her butler; **Sammo Hung refused to play the butler** (he had raised no objection at the outline or one-page proposal stage—"An actor generally focuses only on the show he's shooting"). The location was set, the guest cast signed, the script couldn't be dropped → changed to Arsenio Hall going undercover → he refused too → **24 hours before shooting** changed to Gretchen Egolf going undercover and Sammo as her butler. "**Same story, same structure, entirely different scenes.**" Lesson: **the same basic story can be redone in three completely different ways.**
- **Actor availability is a script parameter**: Diagnosis Murder's writers' guidelines state directly—scripts are usually **68–70 pages**; **Mark Sloan appears in no more than 32–35 pages** (Van Dyke's work schedule); **7 shooting days**; at least **2–3 days on standing sets** (hospital, police station, beach house).
- **If the actor is good enough, cut the dialogue** (Fellowes, Downton footnote): **"A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue."**
- **An actor's veto can be a good thing** (Fleabag): Sian Clifford stopped the author on set from changing a line—"**It's good. Stop it.**"; Andrew Scott **insisted** that the Priest say "I love you too" at the end—"thank God he did" (**the last line of love in the whole series was added at the actor's request**).
- **Create a role for a specific actor** (Fleabag): Olivia Colman saw the stage play and said she could do it anytime—"**I ASKED IMMEDIATELY. I then ran to my producers, cracked open the pilot script, rewrote the end and created the part of Godmother specifically for her.**" The original pilot ending had no Godmother, and that storyline later became S1's prop main line. Same principle (Succession, Frank Rich's S2 introduction): Gerri was originally a male Jerry; in season four Sarah Snook's pregnancy gave rise to a mid-season story line—**the premise being that "the series' architecture could support it without buckling the overall design."**
- **On-set rewriting is the norm, not an accident** (Fleabag): Brett Gelman's S2E6 kitchen monologue was **rewritten in eleven minutes in the car on the way to set**, and he had thirty seconds to memorize his lines. **But the premise is that the structure was already locked; only the lines changed**—the content of Martin's monologue changed, its function (making Claire kneel and beg him to leave) did not.

## 16. Alts, Freebie Takes, the Economics of "Writing Long," and Recycling Cut Material

**7.5 alts, freebie takes, and the economics of "writing long"** (Armstrong's Note on the Text lists seven reasons the script differs from the finished show, each one a working method):
1. **The edit—the core method is to write long.** **70+ pages** per episode (the norm is 60; some shows squeeze to 50). Two layers of reasoning: ① the economies of scale in television shooting mean that shooting a few more pages **of dialogue** (not car crashes) "returns exponential rewards," because the edit can refocus the story; ② writing long **allows you not to choose**—you don't have to cut those "small or weird or nothingy" moments when you're "snow-blind" after 40 drafts; sometimes they become "**the heart of a scene or just a jewel of a moment**."
2. **Alt**: a day or two before shooting, the showrunner picks a few lines (**0 to 10–15**) that might be "funnier or better or truer," **sends them to 3–5 writers asking for alts**; from 10–20 options, narrow to **5–10 kept in your pocket**, and after the scripted version is shot, feed them to the actors as appropriate.
3. **Late change** (changes too close to shooting to be typed into the script, mostly because of practical location logistics, **made in ballpoint pen**) and **fog of war** (small changes in word order and individual words; he doesn't watch the monitor with the script, only the performance; **an actor's slight paraphrase counts as an "implied note,"** meaning the line doesn't have a natural feel—the trade-off is that if he keeps changing until the day of shooting, he has to tolerate how much the actor can learn in a short time).
4. **Improvisation—the freebie take**: after the scripted version is shot, do one "freebie." **The value is not the occasional improvised line but the feeling it gives every take: "a spirit of freedom and collaboration."** Also **ADR** (looping, for plot clarification, detail changes, added laughs) and **the remembrance of things past** (there are always changes after the read-through, "sometimes just reorderings and sharpenings, sometimes a wholesale restructuring"; actors remember the old draft and sew the deleted fragments back in on the day—"**a reassuring safety net**").

**Cut scenes move to a place with a lower "resting heart rate"**: the Succession footnotes repeatedly record **material recycling**—the S1E1 scene of Shiv bringing Logan the old-house photo album was cut and later sewn into S2E8 "Dundee"; Tom's "You're fired. Follow the dream" scene was shot for both S1E3 and S2E2 and never aired. Fellowes's method is more systematic: **for every cut, note "what was lost"** (an explanation, a new color for a character, an explicit statement of theme), and decide whether to restore it in a later episode (the scene of Carson remembering Mary's childhood was restored by reshooting).

## 17. When the Episode Can't Be Saved

**7.6 When the episode can't be saved.** Cobra "A Few Dead Men" (p0133–134): shot in Vancouver, budget under $650,000 per episode; the whole fourth act was a big military-exercise set piece, and with weeks of rain in January, the forest turned to mud, and every alternative location was booked by other productions—in the end **the climax was shot in a high-school gymnasium**, written at midnight in a hotel room, "**it was terrible. And it was necessary.**" (a shutdown costs tens of thousands of dollars a day.) "There are times in television when you have to say, '**This episode isn't going to work**' and move on to the next one."—even the best shows have a stinker or two a year; **saving the unsavable will wreck the next three episodes**. Other force majeure events: the week of the beach episode it poured rain → it became a warehouse episode; the lead lost his voice two days into shooting → the whole thing was rewritten to focus on the secondary lead; one Diagnosis Murder episode was due to shoot on the Saturday when Princess Diana died in a car crash, **new pages went out first thing Sunday, and the European princess became an heiress**.

## 18. A Comparison Table of Five Workflows and the Counter-Evidence from Reality / Unscripted

| Workflow | Representative | Method | Preconditions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard American writers' room** | ER (Wells), Dexter (West), Succession (Armstrong/Prebble), Smallville (Souders) | 8–12 people, 5–6 hours a day, for months; group break → individual writing → full-room notes → revise → more notes; the showrunner has the deciding vote | Multi-strand ensemble; the capacity for continuous supply |
| **Single author + substitute writers' room** | Fleabag (Waller-Bridge) | No writers' room; **five roles** replace it: dramaturg / script editor (sees what you haven't written yet) + story producer (talks with you, sticks up Post-its) + director (only objects when it's "not true") + actors (guard and veto the lines) + editor (**"Editing is essentially the final draft of the script."**), plus a subject-matter consultant | Half-hour, six-episode seasons, a strong personal voice, highly autobiographical material; **and you must actively write these five roles into the work plan instead of hoping for inspiration** |
| **Zero writers' room, zero outline** | Euphoria (Levinson) | Writes every episode himself, directs most; **no outlines** (reason: outlines kill unpredictability) | Strongly autobiographical material + creator also directing + a permissive platform. **This is the exception, not a transferable method** |

**The reality/unscripted counter-evidence** (Douglas 3rd ed. feature, Scott A. Stone interview): reality shows' deliverables still include a rundown with "a breakdown by act, six act breaks, a beginning-middle-end, and a cliffhanger"; a show's pre-production is written in a bible "about four inches thick" and approved by the platform; Stone says "**60% of my time is writing or rewriting treatments**." But his characterization explains exactly why you should study the writers' room: **"This is a producer's medium… whereas scripted series are a writer-driven medium."**

## 19. Inside John Wells's ER Writers' Room, Smallville's Anonymous Reads, and Dexter's February Camp

> The full version of three rows from the master table in Section 1. Sources: Douglas English 3rd ed. ch. 5 "A Slice of Life" (ER) and ch. 7 (Souders, West, interview year **2011**); the Chinese 2nd ed. ch. 5 p0196–p0199 provides the terminology mapping.

**19.1 The room's physical setup (transcribed as-is; usable directly as a "writers' room" set)**
- **One long, wide dark wood-grain conference table fills the whole room**; around it the fixed configuration: **4 senior writers + 4 staff writers + 1 full-time researcher + Wells himself**.
- **The two editions disagree on the number of chairs; both are recorded side by side**: the English 3rd ed. says **10 chairs** (exactly these 10 people), the Chinese 2nd ed. p0196 says **12 chairs**. → **Take the headcount of 10 as authoritative.**
- **At the back of the room** there are more chairs and a few sofas (the Chinese translation says "**more than 10 chairs and a few sofas**"), for the **full-time physicians on the staff** and production people who need to be there.
- **All chairs face a monitor**—dailies are watched there; the room doubles as a screening room.
- **A large whiteboard on the wall**: filled with **the plot points and story breakdowns of the 12 episodes to be completed this season** (one board for the whole season, not one per episode).
- **Another board / a side cabinet on one side**: possible scene ideas listed in **colored markers** under four headings—**Big / Serious / Humorous / Other** (the Chinese translation says "grand, serious, humorous, etc."). → The most copyable thing: **don't ask "which episode does this belong in?" first; ask "what kind is it?"**; the existence of the Humorous column is an institutional guarantee of a comic buffer in every episode.

**19.2 Season and episode schedule (table from the Chinese ed. p0197)**

| Stage | Duration | Deliverable |
|---|---|---|
| The whole group designs the season (from **the first week of June**) | **6 weeks** | "the ideas for each episode of the season and the specific story lines; the team has to **pound them out**"—setting **all the major turning points, where the act breaks fall, and how many episodes an arc covers** |
| Full episode process | about **8 weeks** | see the chain below |

- **The episode chain (as-is)**: assign one writer to write a **story treatment** → come back and **hear the full room's notes** → revise once → **another round of notes** → only then is he sent to write the **script** → the script comes in and gets **a round of notes** → **second draft** → if workable, prep for shooting. **Notes happen three times, and the treatment stage accounts for two of them.**
- **Notes meetings**: **every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday afternoon**; the English edition says **four to five hours** each, the Chinese says "**more than 4 hours**"; the method is **to go page by page, scene by scene, to the end of the script**.

**19.3 A real notes meeting (a Written By reporter visiting ER in its earliest season)**
- Opening image: the writers file out of their offices and head straight for the conference room, **each carrying a photocopy of that script**; **Wells sits at the head**; coffee is brought in but work starts immediately.
- The episode under discussion: a teenager with **cystic fibrosis**, a mother afraid her son will die, **the turn being that the son doesn't want to be saved**, putting Dr. Ross between the mother, the son, and the Hippocratic oath. The room's questions fall into three categories—**character relationships** (what about the boy's girlfriend), **the protagonist's choice** (does Ross save him), **repetition** ("has the audience already seen this Ross trait").
- **Wells's two interventions = the showrunner's standard moves in a notes meeting**: ① the story was "almost there," but **one key action was missing, an action that would make the emotional rhythm of the whole passage more subtle and precise**—which led to a discussion of "a character who wasn't developed enough"; ② he **proactively raised a new topic: several scenes to lighten things with comedy**.
→ **A notes meeting is not item-by-item fault-finding; it's pointing out "the missing action" and then naming the next topic to discuss. The note-giver owns the agenda, not the solution.**
- (The three reasons for "a tape recorder rather than a laptop" are in SKILL.md 5.2 and are not repeated here.)

**19.4 Kelly Souders's Smallville (2011, ch. 7)**
- **The room model (verbatim)**: "a group of writers **breaking story together and producing a rough outline**, and then someone goes off to write it. On some shows the writers break story individually; **we keep it in the group. Multiple minds are better than one mind. A very traditional writers room.**"
- The **anonymous reads** process was covered in Section 10, 5.5; here only the motive and the boundary: the motive is **the pressure of agents calling to demand "put this person's script on top" is too great**—"**that system doesn't work well for us**"; the boundary is "**we really do read without knowing who wrote it**." → Inference for submitters: **a recommendation can't move this kind of room; only what's on the page after the first page is torn off can.**

**19.5 Wendy West's Dexter (co-executive producer, 2011, same chapter)**
- **The February "camp"**: the production's work period is **February to October**, so February is the off-season; camp's biggest task is **to define the season's arc and "who this character really is"**; **the whole of February is eight writers talking, just talking** (not writing).
- The "break the emotional line first" quote is in Section 1; two companions: ① **everything is filtered through Dexter's point of view** (plus voice-over), "this helps locate your position in the story"; ② **the direction is locked while the path is open**—"usually we know the beginning and the end. **We know what's in episode twelve and then everything has to work to that.**"

**20.2 Smith's ladder: ten titles and an 8–12 person group (2009–2011)**
- **Bottom to top (as-is)**: **Term Writer → Story Editor → Executive Story Editor → Co-producer → Producer → Supervising Producer → Story Consultant → Consulting Producer → Co-executive Producer → Executive Producer**. Compared with Douglas's seven-level table: Smith splits level 4 into Co-producer/Producer and level 6 into Story Consultant/Consulting Producer, **and has no "staff writer"; the lowest level is Term Writer**.
- **Scale**: a sitcom uses **5–20** writers who are also writer-producers, **8–12 on average**; not every show uses all the titles, and **a rising star can skip levels** (Story Editor straight to Co-producer).
- **Four things don't change with title (as-is)**: "all staff writers write scripts, join in group writing sessions, and attend **read-throughs, run-throughs, and final shoots**."
- **Negotiate five things in the contract**: title, weekly salary, **guaranteed weeks of employment**, guaranteed number of episodes, and a timetable for future promotion. The counterintuitive rule: **the more guaranteed weeks, the lower the weekly salary.**

**20.3 Blum's institutional items (1998 MBA; amounts void, items still knowledge)**
① **A no-cut contract trades job security for lower pay**: 10% lower weekly salary for 13+ weeks, 20% lower for 26+ weeks. ② **Syndication/repeat ladder**: series produced after 1988-08-08—**the first 52 weeks at 2/3 of the base, the next 52 at 5/6, then the normal base**. ③ **The head-writer system for daytime serials is explicitly recognized**: "**the head writer's fee may be reduced by as much as five times the base script fee**"; **the base for rewriting or polishing a non-prime-time series script is 30% of the script fee**. ④ **Payment timing**: pay within **48 hours of delivery where possible, at most 7 days**, and "**payment of compensation shall not be conditioned on the company's receipt or approval of the submitted written material…**". ⑤ **"format" and "series bible" are separately priced deliverables**, and **each story line beyond the sixth is billed separately** (→ `sw-series-engine-bible`).

## 21. The Multi-Camera Sitcom Production Week and the Freelance Stage Timetable

> Sources: Smith ch. 14 p0202–0204 (production week, **2009**) and ch. 11–13 p0145–0146, p0192–0193 (freelance timetable, **2009**). The day counts are for sitcoms; for one-hour drama see Section 22.

**21.1 The five days before shooting (multi-camera, transcribed as-is)**

| Day | What Happens | The Writer's Situation That Day |
|---|---|---|
| **DAY ONE** | **table reading**: producers, writers, actors, department heads, network and studio executives attend; **everyone but the executives sits around one big table / a ring of tables**, the actors read through the draft to be shot that week → notes → the writers go to revise; the director and departments prepare physical elements at the same time | **"If the script looks bad, the writers face a very long night."** |
| **DAY TWO** | usually **another read, another round of notes**; if the script is okay, the director starts designing camera positions and blocking, **possibly a full run-through** | **some shows fix this day as rewrite night regardless of the script's state** |
| **DAYS THREE AND FOUR** | actors learn lines seriously, **the script is locked**; the director blocks and walks the camera | notes continue, revisions continue |
| **DAY FIVE (shooting day)** | possibly **two full run-throughs**—first the formal **dress rehearsal**, then notes, final changes, maybe a meal, then the **final shoot** | in the edit, **the best parts of both are cut together into the finished show** |

- **Three ways to break stories as a group at the start of a season (choose one, don't mix)**: ① pitch stories one by one → each writer does the beats for his own episode → come back and revise collectively → then each writes his script; ② **the group sets the beats for each episode together** → individuals write scripts separately; ③ **the group sets the beats and also writes the script scene by scene together**. Serialized shows must first decide how the **season story arc** advances, then distribute pieces of the arc across episodes.

| Stage | Duration |

| Writing the **outline** | **2 days–1 week** (ch. 11 also notes "pitch selected → **2–7 days** to deliver the outline") |
| **Revising the outline** | **2–5 days** |
| **First draft** | **1–2 weeks** (about 30 pages) |
| **Second draft** | **2 days–1 week** (notes **within a day or two** of the first draft) |

- **Staff go through the same steps faster; specs set their own pace.** One freelance assignment overall = **3–5 weeks** of intensive writing.
- **The room meeting eats part of the time first** (same producers, disagreements recorded side by side): **Matt Williams**—"almost always **half a day to three days** in the room beating it out," then **two days** to write the outline (at season's end, "**Bring it back tomorrow**"); **Lapiduss**—the first freelance story gets "pitched out to within an inch of its life," must fit **a slot between episodes 5 and 8**, then **1–3 days** to nail it down beat by beat; **Gurvitz**—one day breaking in the room, and the returned outline should "**better reflect exactly what was said in the room**."
- **One disagreement on delivery discipline** (preserved): **Irma Kalish**—don't say "Do you want it good or do you want it tomorrow?"; "**Make it good and hand it in when you promised.**"; **Douglas** (Section 22)—if you finish a day or two early, **don't hand it in early**; let it cool for a day. The two don't conflict: **finish early, deliver on time.**

## 22. Douglas's Six-Week Professional Schedule for One Episode and the Day-by-Day 14-Day Method

> Sources: Douglas, "Rule Two: Take the series deadline seriously" (Chinese 2nd ed. p0033–p0034; English 3rd ed. ch. 1–2, same passage) and ch. 4 on the first draft (Chinese p0167–p0168). These are one-hour drama numbers.

**22.1 The standard cycle for one episode = 6 weeks (transcribed as-is)**

| Step | Duration | Wording in the Source |
|---|---|---|
| Writing the **story outline** | **1 week** | "you may spend 1 week writing the story outline" |
| **Revising the outline** | **a few days** | "a few days to revise it" |
| Delivering the **1st draft** | **2 weeks** | "deliver the 1st draft of the episode two weeks later" |
| **Hearing notes** (notes meeting) | **1–2 days** | "then a day or two to hear the notes" |
| Writing the **2nd draft** | **1 week** | "then write the 2nd draft within 1 week" |
| **Summary** | **about 6 weeks** | "only about 6 weeks in total from pitch to 2nd draft" |
| (**Polish / revisions for production**) | another **1–2 weeks** | "if polishing or revising for production, add another week or two" |

- **There is only one consequence of missing the deadline (as-is)**: "if you can't finish on time, the showrunner has to hand your work to another writer."
- **This table does not contradict Wells's "about eight weeks per episode" in Section 19**: Wells's eight weeks is **the whole process from assignment to ready-to-shoot** (including three rounds of notes meetings and waiting for approvals); Douglas's six weeks is **the writer's own net writing cycle** (up to the second draft).

**22.2 The 14-day method day by day (as-is, seven steps)**
- **Starting point**: the English 3rd ed. says **deliver the first draft two weeks after the outline is approved**; the Chinese 2nd ed. renders this sentence as "for a multi-episode series, after its outline is approved, it usually takes two weeks to determine how many episodes to write"—**follow the English edition**; the Chinese goes off the rails semantically here.
- **The arithmetic (as-is)**: "say you have an outline of **28 scenes** (four acts, nearly 7 scenes per act), and I like to follow my outline strictly, so I can **write 2 scenes a day**. Look! **14×2=28.**"
- **The seven steps of a day**: ① **in the morning, read carefully through what you wrote the previous day** and "get yourself in the right state"; ② "I treat it as if these next few pages were **the single most important scene I have ever written in my life**… **full of subtext and spirit**, while letting the story unfold as tightly as I can manage"; ③ before writing, choose one of three: **imagine the whole scene / jot down ideas while walking / close your eyes and wait for the characters' voices**; ④ **write 2 to 3 pages, then stop**; ⑤ the reason (the key to the whole method): "**forcing yourself to write actually lowers the quality**"—rest, lunch, errands, exercise, phone calls, **physically leave it**; ⑥ **later in the day (afternoon or evening), reread and polish the scene you wrote during the day**; ⑦ repeat ①–⑤ for **the second scene**, and stop when it's done.
- **The result (the whole payoff)**: "by the time I reached the end of the whole script, **my first draft had already been edited and polished, because I was crafting my work every day.**"
- **The opposite personality and the "painter vs. sculptor" conclusion are in Section 5**; only the handling of fear of the blank page is added here: "**if you're scared by the blank page, just put everything down—anything.**"

## 23. Beat Sheets and Leave-Behinds as Physical Objects: What Six Documents Look Like

> Source: Goldberg & Rabkin appendices D–I (**2003**). This section takes only "the document's shape and length," **not the plot**; the corresponding spec clauses are in Section 3 (leave-behind) and Section 4 (beat sheet / outline).

**23.1 The headings of six documents: the same kind of document has four names**

| Document | Heading (as-is) |
|---|---|
| Martial Law leave-behind | `MARTIAL LAW` / `"Sammo Blammo"` / **`Story Area by`** / `Lee Goldberg and William Rabkin` |
| Martial Law beat sheet | `MARTIAL LAW` / `"Sammo Blammo"` / **`Revised Outline`** / `Written by` |
| seaQuest leave-behind | `SEAQUEST 2032` / `"Depths of Deceit"` / **`Treatment`** |
| seaQuest beat sheet | `SEAQUEST 2032` / `"Depths of Deceit"` / **`Rough Beat Sheet by`** |
| Diagnosis Murder one-hour episode | `DIAGNOSIS MURDER` / `"A Passion for Murder"` / **`First Draft Beat Sheet by`** |
| Diagnosis Murder two-hour episode | `DIAGNOSIS MURDER` / `"The Last Laugh"` / **`Rough Beat Sheet – First Draft`** |

**23.2 The five-part functional template of a one-page leave-behind (story area)**
The Martial Law one is **about a page and a half, five paragraphs**, each doing one job: ① **open with the teaser action and immediately present the visual problem unique to this show** (opening move: `The story opens with our heroes narrowly thwarting…`); ② **the villain enters + his situation** (one sentence of backstory); ③ **the villain's problem is the story engine** (`Only problem is, …`); ④ **why the villain must use the protagonist**—turn the protagonist's expertise into the villain's tool (the sophisticated procedural move of "forcing the case onto the protagonist"); ⑤ **the device + countdown + a closing line** (the closing line can pun on the show's title). → The one-pager holds **three constraints** (can't stop, can't dismantle, time limit), and those three constraints are themselves a plot machine of three acts or more.

**23.3 The difference between the two leave-behinds (the one thing most worth remembering in this section)**

| | Martial Law Version | seaQuest Version |
|---|---|---|
| Length | about a page and a half, five paragraphs | **about two pages of prose, no scene numbers** |
| Running character conflict | **not written**—"we didn't deal with the character conflicts that would be running through the story, **just the conflict that drives the episode**" | written |
| Secrets | only the hook from the audience's point of view | **all secrets written out**—using `What we will discover is that…` and `What X doesn't know is that…` (spec in Section 3) |
| Intended audience | enough for a producer to sell the story to a network | for decision-makers to see hierarchy and manipulation |
| Risk noted by the authors | **not enough for a freelancer seeking an assignment**: "The network takes it on faith that the character conflicts will be spelled out in the beat sheet, but **a producer won't grant a freelancer the same benefit of the doubt.**" (p0169) | — |

**23.4 Format comparison of the two beat sheets (same kind of document, two showrunner styles)**

| | Martial Law (Appendix E) | seaQuest (Appendix G) |
|---|---|---|
| Scene labels | **full slug + scene number within the act** (`1. EXT. OIL REFINERY – NIGHT`) | **only locations in caps, no scene numbers** (`OCEAN –`, `NEW ORLEANS –`, even `SOMEPLACE –`, `SOMEWHERE –`) |
| How each scene is written | one to three paragraphs per scene, with dialogue points and camera directions (FADE OUT, POV, SHIFT PERSPECTIVES) | one paragraph per location, mostly action and information |
| Length | about **15 pages** | about **6 pages** |
| Act markers | TEASE + four acts, **END OF ACT after every act** | TEASE + four acts, **ACT headings only** |

→ **At the "rough beat sheet" stage, even locations can be written as SOMEPLACE**; lock the order of power moves and information reveals first, and refine in the next draft. The authors' own characterization (Appendix G introduction): **"Showrunners have their own styles, which can be reflected in their beat sheets, too."**

**23.5 Three sets of measured data on scene distribution (calibrating Section 4's "6–7 scenes per act, four acts")**

| Document | Scenes per Act | Total | Observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Martial Law "Sammo Blammo" (action-buddy show) | TEASE 2 / ACT ONE 5 / ACT TWO 8 / ACT THREE 5 / ACT FOUR **10** | about **30 scenes** | **the later acts of action shows clearly fragment** (10 scenes in act four, mostly short cross-cut scenes) |
| Diagnosis Murder "A Passion for Murder" (one-hour whodunit, **shot**) | TEASE 1 / ACT ONE 6 / ACT TWO 7 / ACT THREE 6 / ACT FOUR 6 | about **26 scenes** | the closest to "6–7 scenes per act"; **no tag; the last scene doubles as the tag**. (The notes' scene-by-scene transcription has one or two more entries than the labeled scene count; the document's scene labels govern.) |
| Diagnosis Murder "The Last Laugh" (two-hour episode) | **continuous scene numbers 1–44 across acts**: ACT ONE 1–5 / TWO 6–15 / THREE 16–23 / FOUR 24–27 / FIVE 28–32 / SIX 33–36 / SEVEN 37–40 / EIGHT 41–44 | **44 scenes / eight acts** | **eight acts, no TEASE marker**; locations in caps, one paragraph per scene |

- **The one-hour format**: it has slugs (`INT. COMMUNITY GENERAL – ER – DAY`) but **no scene numbers**, one or two paragraphs per scene, **all in the present tense**, stating action and dialogue points, four acts + TEASE.

**23.6 How much white space a first-draft beat sheet can leave (the most stress-relieving practical finding)**
Both Diagnosis Murder documents expose the true state of a first draft—**white space is allowed**: you can write **"Brilliant farce to be figured out."** (followed only by the known outcome: the setup happens, the money box appears, everything goes wrong, and the specific farce waits for the script stage); you can write **"(or something like that)"**; you can write **"(or whatever the game show is called)"**; you can write a location as **SOMEPLACE**. → **The beat sheet's job is to lock the "order of information" and the "act-out positions," not to finish the script**; but **act-outs, clue planting and payoff, and character turns must be nailed down.**

## 24. Julian Fellowes's List of Writing Principles Distilled from 419 Footnotes (Each Tagged with Its Decision Type)

> Source: the Fellowes footnotes in the `Downton Abbey: Series Two` script collection, **419** in total. The table gives each principle and **what kind of decision it is**. Five categories: **[Narrative craft]** (a pure writing choice), **[Editing choice]** (the finished show differs from the script, decided by the editing consensus), **[Historical accuracy]** (verification of historical fact, law, medicine), **[Actor-driven rewrite]** (performance quality or availability, physical conditions rewrote the script), **[Production cost]** (money for locations, sets, props, animals). Items 18b and 19 already appear in Sections 15 and 16; here only the category is marked.

| # | Principle (Fellowes's Own Words / Distilled) | Category |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"Downton dilemma = both sides are right"** (his most frequent self-description): "a typical Downton argument, where hopefully you're on both sides." | [Narrative craft] |
| 2 | **No wholly unsympathetic character**: "There are no entirely unsympathetic characters in Downton. But there is always a sub-text, or at least I try to suggest one." | [Narrative craft] |
| 3 | **Open each episode by restating the running lines in two or three lines of dialogue**: "never assume that they will remember every detail of an earlier episode, because they won't." | [Narrative craft] |
| 4 | **An ensemble scene advances six or seven lines, one sentence each**: "the audience has to store it all and then put the plots together at the end." | [Narrative craft] |
| 5 | **Let the audience know more than the characters**: "you engineer uncomfortable meetings, and then you let the audience fill in the detail." | [Narrative craft] |
| 6 | **Once the audience knows, you don't have to show them**: no line at the moment of hearing the truth; sex and violence left to the audience's imagination—"owes more to old Hollywood than modern television" | [Narrative craft] |
| 7 | **The upstairs and downstairs emotional stories must carry equal weight** ("giving them both the same attention"), and use **mirror pairing** so the same event is told once in each register | [Narrative craft] |
| 8 | **Deliberately create scenes between two people who would never otherwise meet** ("there is no greater distance at Downton than that between the Dowager Countess and Daisy")—for this he allows a little **institutional irregularity**, and notes the reason for it in the footnote | [Narrative craft] + [Historical accuracy] |
| 9 | **Three blocking devices**: the moment everyone rises at the end of dinner can regroup character pairings; the segmented flow of a hunting day can likewise "can bring characters together or push them apart"; **passage acting** in corridors is used to run several lines in parallel | [Narrative craft] |
| 10 | **The retention premise of workplace drama**: "One of the basic questions of any drama is: **why do these men and women stay?**"—the answer is "it's their livelihood," so every so often remind the audience that this is a workplace with a budget, wages, and a hierarchy | [Narrative craft] |
| 11 | **"truth reminders"**: every so often plant a truth that pricks the warmth ("Who is Daisy?"), so the ensemble doesn't turn into a love-in | [Narrative craft] |
| 12 | **Three rules for killing a character**: not a single regular can be safe; the dead must be someone the audience knows and loves—"**It's like a whodunit. The killer has to be someone who was there all the time.**"; **deliberately mislead the audience about who dies** | [Narrative craft] |
| 13 | **The season finale must give a reason to come back**; the last scene is pure pay-off plus **"streamers"**—"we won't resolve this tonight, but if you keep watching, at some point we will." | [Narrative craft] |
| 14 | **Timing the lovers' union**: "you keep them apart and keep them apart, but… there comes a time when the audience thinks, oh, for Christ's sake, are they going to get together or not?" | [Narrative craft] |
| 15 | **"drama requires a logic not always supplied by truth." But history and medicine must be verified** (a Decree Nisi set aside for collusion, spinal shock vs transection, brachial plexus, the Spanish flu killing only the young); and he favors references that **"look baffling, but check out as completely accurate"** | [Historical accuracy] |
| 16 | **Don't give period characters modern attitudes**: "It always slightly annoys me when characters in period drama, in order to make them sympathetic, are given modern attitudes and modern choices." | [Historical accuracy] |
| 17 | **Put it in without explaining**: "the style of Downton… refer to people and events… without making a point of explaining them." | [Narrative craft] |
| 18 | **Production reality rewrites the script, and he records it as normal**: rolling up the carpets at Highclere requires **the owner's approval**; **there is a cap on dining-room scenes** (Carson and the footmen can't stand that long); a **convertible studio bedroom** is kept standing (no ceiling, movable walls, so overhead shots are possible); **actor availability → cut a scene or write an exit**; **actor physical features → remove a vaccination scar with effects** | [Production cost] + [Actor-driven rewrite] |
| 18b | **If the actor is good enough, cut the dialogue**: "A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue." (see Section 15) | [Actor-driven rewrite] |
| 19 | **Editing is a three-person consensus; the author often loses and admits he was wrong**: "the consensus went against me… **I think I was wrong and they were right.**"; what gets lost is mostly **explanatory dialogue** and small gestures that "add a new color to a character," and **he notes every time what was lost** (see Section 16) | [Editing choice] |
| 20 | **American broadcast has no concept of a "special"**: the Christmas special must "play either as the end of a series, or as a stand-alone" | [Production cost] (distribution/scheduling constraint) |
| 21 | **Write the character's opposition as an "institution," not "malice"**: written evidence required, an illegitimate child has no legal claim on the father, buying off a scandal, a Decree Nisi set aside—**the resistance comes from the rules; the villain is just someone who knows the rules** | [Historical accuracy] + [Narrative craft] |
| 22 | **Jokes and power in the same line**: the Dowager's lines are often **both a scene button and an exercise of power** ("I hope it is not vulgar in me to suggest that you find some way to overcome your scruples." / "I have a cold.") | [Narrative craft] |

**Category counts and inference**: of the 22 items, [Narrative craft] 14, [Historical accuracy] 4 (2 of them shared with craft), [Production cost] 2, [Actor-driven rewrite] 2, [Editing choice] 1. → **The value distribution of the footnotes is not in "how much production reality constrains people" but in the repeated self-description of "both sides are right"**; but **for every forced change he wrote down what was lost** (item 19), which is a directly copyable working habit: **a change log with two columns—what changed / what was lost.**

## 25. First-Hand Revision Evidence in the Succession Footnotes: The Migration Chain of Cut Scenes

> Source: Armstrong's footnotes in the Faber edition Succession script collection (which contains **the scripts as they stood when shooting began**, "as they stood when we started to film"). In `Note on the Text` he explains three reasons the script differs from the finished show: **editing choices / new lines the writer added on set / actors improvising on the day**, and says **footnotes are added only where "the reason for the change is not self-evident."** The two S1 examples (the Shiv photo-album scene → sewn into S2E8 "Dundee"; Tom's "You're fired. Follow the dream" shot for both S1E3 and S2E2 and never aired) are in Section 16; this section gives the S2→S3 chain.

| # | Original Position | Destination | Footnote Wording / Reason | Working Method Demonstrated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **S2E10** Roman–Gerri restaurant scene ("It will be incredibly brief" / "Maybe I'll throw you under the bus?") | **S3E1** Roman–Gerri scene in the airport hotel (footnote L3993) | cut because the episode's material was overloaded, but "we kept its **essence** for a Roman–Gerri scene at the start of the next season, **when the episode's resting heart rate is lower**"; the S3 footnote says the scene was **"first written for episode ten of season two"** and in S3E1's flow "**find a spot to nestle more comfortably**" | **cross-season reuse of the material library**: a good scene isn't thrown away; wait for a structural gap with a "lower resting heart rate" |
| 2 | **S2E10** the four siblings playing cards ("We knew." / "Well I did not know." / "We kind of knew.") | **S3E2** (footnote L7899) | S2 side: the scene "**didn't fit the very specific rhythm of the end of this episode**," but "we kept that dynamic and argument… **the emotional temperature probably lands it better**"; S3 side: this **"wolfpack-speculation material"** had been tried in S2E10 and **felt superfluous**; here it is "**more germane and sprung-in**" | **the value of a scene depends on whether there's enough pressure in front of it**—the scene isn't bad; the position is wrong |
| 3 | **S3E3** Tom's direct line "Divorce" to the front desk (footnote L11725) | **cut**, leaving only the lawyer's name **Rex Hendon**, paid off only in **S3E9** | the on-the-nose line was cut because it "**cast too heavy a shadow over subsequent events**"; attentive viewers can discover at the season finale that Rex Hendon is a divorce lawyer | **dimmer on the foreshadowing**: too bright and it leaks the finale; a single proper noun can carry the whole plant |
| 4 | **S3E8** the entire **nanny-firing** subplot (Rava's reminder, Kendall asking Iverson, firing Bianca, Bianca leaving; footnote L31375) | **cut entirely**, not migrated | **"We had to cut the whole nanny-firing-game strand from the episode. It was a tough one to see go but the episode was just too long."** | **length is a hard constraint; cut the "most complete but most detachable" subplot first**—complete so it cuts cleanly, detachable so it doesn't drag down other lines |
| 5 | **S3E9** the long Marcia–Shiv talk (footnote L35111) | **cut**, not migrated | **"I would love to have included this Marcia–Shiv exchange, but the velocity required by the gathering storm of the end of the episode meant we had to lose it."** | **the finale's acceleration takes priority over a single scene's quality**—a good scene gets cut in a finale because it "slows things down" |

**A sixth piece of evidence in the same set of footnotes (not editing but an on-set improvement, recorded so the table above isn't misread)**: the footnote beside the last line of S3E9—**"The end of this episode is a good example of how scripted scenes get improved by the opportunities afforded on set. At script, I didn't know what the final image would be."** On set, the director Mark Mylod and the two actors found a "much stronger final image." → **The script's job is to make the emotion and the allegiances clear, not to lock the camera; the final image can be left to the set.**

→ Combined with Fellowes's practice in Section 16, this is the complete working method: **Armstrong decides where cut scenes go; Fellowes records what cut scenes lost. Do both.**
