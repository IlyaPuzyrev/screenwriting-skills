# Writers' Room & Production Reality (编剧室与制作现实)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

Television is not written by one person and handed in. It is **a group of people in a room, producing one episode a week under a fixed schedule and a fixed budget**, and that production process rewrites your script in turn. This skill covers: how the room operates, what documents are delivered at what size and in what order, how a story is "broken" collectively, how to take notes, how the rank ladder is climbed, and how to change the script when money, actors, weather, or locations veto you without destroying the episode.

Schedule tables, physical documents, and verbatim interviews are in the same directory, [reference.md](reference.md).

Three premises (a consensus across the books): ① **"the entire ladder of television is built on writers' titles"** (Douglas, Chinese edition, ch. 5, p. 199), fundamentally different from the film industry, where "creative power lies with the director and financiers can buy titles" (Blum, ch. 19, p. 246) — learning to write series television means learning to be a writer-producer. ② **"An episode is a product made every week in a specified number of days for a certain price and delivered at a guaranteed running time on an inflexible deadline."** (Goldberg & Rabkin, ch. 1, p. 19) ③ **How you perform in the room matters more than your idea, even more than the script you eventually deliver** — "crafting stories is much harder than actually writing scripts" (Goldberg, p. 86).

**Not covered**: act structure and page-count anchors, season shape (→ `sw-series-structure`); series design, engine, bible, and pitch-document fields (→ `sw-series-engine-bible`); the mechanics of sitcom jokes and comedy-room punch-up (→ `sw-sitcom-comedy`). **The general account of American contracts, options, credit arbitration, and the agency system is already in `sw-industry-business`, Sections 5 and 6; this skill covers only the series-specific step deal and staff compensation. For the mainland Chinese series production chain, regulatory filing and review, 一度 / 二度 / 三度创作 (yī dù / èr dù / sān dù chuàngzuò — creation in three stages: script, then direction and performance, then editing and post-production), and the per-episode outline system, see `sw-chinese-series-practice`.** General dialogue, scene, and character methods are in `sw-dialogue`, `sw-scene-craft`, and `sw-character-conflict`.

## Sources and How the Books Relate

- **Goldberg & Rabkin, *Successful Television Writing* (Wiley, 2003), provides the skeleton**: both authors went from writing Spenser specs to showrunning Diagnosis Murder and Martial Law; the book is a process manual for "writing, selling, and rewriting an episode for an existing series," with real leave-behinds and beat sheets reproduced in the appendix. Shelf life: the "always four acts" rule for one-hour drama and the $27,000 figure are 2003 data.
- **Douglas, *Writing the TV Drama Series* (3rd ed., 2011; an earlier Chinese translation also exists), provides the physics of the room and the ladder**: Chapter 5 is the only account that treats the writers' room **as a place with office seating, a culture, and politics**; Chapter 4 gives the outline format and the 14-day method; Chapters 6–7 give the spec rules and a fourteen-year follow-up interview.
- **Landau, *The TV Showrunner's Roadmap*, 1e (2014) / 2e (2022), provides first-hand craft decisions from showrunners of the same generation**, and is also **the source with the most concentrated disagreements** in this skill (Lindelof vs. Mazzara, Hillian vs. Levinson).
- **Smith, *Writing Television Sitcoms*, Chapters 14–19, provides the on-site details of pitch meetings and roundtables**; **Blum, Chapters 18–19, provides the 1999–2001 market structure and the working habits of four showrunners** (the numbers are outdated; the structural judgments still hold).
- **Script collections and commentaries provide "what actually happened"**: Chase's Sopranos foreword (35 beats, scissors and tape), Armstrong and Prebble's Succession foreword/footnotes (mega-chart, maybe culture, alts, freebie take, writing long), Sorkin's West Wing preface (the budget forcing a bottle episode), Fellowes's Downton footnotes (editing by consensus, cutting dialogue when an actor is good), Waller-Bridge's Fleabag commentary (a single author as an alternative writers' room).
- **Miller, *Your Hero Succeeds*, Chapters 9–10, provides draft-number discipline and the two rounds of readers**: draft name = draft number, warm read / cold read, three rules for filtering notes.

## I. What a Writers' Room Is: The Showrunner's Duties and Power

**Definition** (Armstrong, Succession S4 foreword): a showrunner "essentially a writer. But **a writer empowered to protect the scripts through the rooms where they are conceived and written and rewritten, performed, captured and edited**." — those four verbs define the jurisdiction.

- **Make decisions and communicate them** (Goldberg ch. 15, p. 121–123): "there's really only one skill an executive producer needs to succeed: he has to be able to **make a decision and communicate it**." Counterexample Ferd: one day "a serial killer is killing nuns," the next "nuns are killing serial killers," wipe the whiteboard and start over; he does not decide, so scripts, casting, and location scouting all stop, money burns every day, and he does not last a year.
- **Be the show's single voice** (Mazzara, Landau 1e Tip 13): "I have several scripts this year that I've written every word of and **have not put my name on them**" — if the delivered episodes do not sound like that voice, the producers, actors, and crew all feel something is wrong.
- **Get the director a script they can prep from on the first day of prep** (Hart Hanson, Bones, Landau 1e Tip 17): "**I think that's the first responsibility**" — you cannot have a director spend two or three days working from an outline that may still change.
- **Hold final cut**: Robert King (Landau 1e): "TV showrunner has final cut… twenty-two to twenty-three little movies every year." Chase's division of labor (Sopranos foreword p. 12): editor's first cut → director's cut (changes shots but generally does not reorder scenes or delete scenes) → the showrunner produces multiple versions through final, **and may reorder and delete scenes**.
- **Do not hand the essential things to a committee** (Bays, HIMYM, Landau 1e Tip 21): the room will make big changes, "but that mostly just applies to **the comedy**. **The dramatic moments I try to not mess with or punch up in the room. Drama needs to feel personal, and can't be written by committee.**"
- **Once you are up there, the job is mostly management**: Goldberg p. 123, "**80 percent of the job is management**"; when Souders was showrunner on Smallville (Douglas, English edition, ch. 7, 2011), **"writing is now only 15 percent of my job"**; the rest is budget, management, and a great many phone calls — "I miss it. That's what I got into this for."
- **Set the room's atmosphere** (Prebble, Succession S3 foreword): Armstrong compares running a writers' room to **throwing a party** — "you're responsible for the guests and the atmosphere and ensuring people want to come."

**"Of all the EPs, only one is the showrunner."** The executive producers in the end credits are three kinds of people (Douglas, Chinese edition, p. 206–207): the one who oversees physical production / **the head writer (oversees content, including directors, casting, and editing)** / an honorary title given to a star. The test is **who makes the key decisions**: the ER pilot was written by Michael Crichton, who carried an EP credit all season but did not work on the show, and "only one person — John Wells — made the key decisions"; the reverse case is an action series with four EPs who "could write scripts fine but could not run a show at all," while the person actually running it was a semi-retired veteran carrying the title **Creative Consultant**. → **The first thing to do when you join a show is to figure out who is actually running it, and answer to that person.**

**Your identity in the room**: Goldberg's philosophy for staff writers (p. 124): "**It's your show. Tell us what you want, and we will give it to you.**"; Wendy West (Dexter co-EP, 2011): "Defensiveness comes from wanting to protect your voice, **and on a show you are not there to protect your voice**… **When you're in this room it's not about your voice. Make your showrunner look like a star.**"; the companion "chameleon" rule: "you have to adapt to the style of the show you're writing" (but "That doesn't mean losing your voice").

## II. Break Story: How the Room Breaks an Episode

Goldberg calls it a **story meeting / "cracking the story"**: first talk broadly about the paths the story could take, and once there is consensus move to **the big whiteboard**, beat by beat, scene by scene, act by act — "this lively and arduous give-and-take that is **the soul of television writing**" (ch. 11, p. 85). Seven real methods; **choose one first, do not mix them**. For the full process and schedule, see reference.md, Sections 1 and 2.

For the comparison table of the seven methods (source, a one-sentence description of each method, quantitative anchors for people/weeks/beats), see reference.md Section 1; for Prebble's four room rules (the pitch opening move, two ordering rules, maybe culture, "what the room produces is what this show is not"), see Section 2. **The physical setup of the ER writers' room (ten chairs, a 12-episode whiteboard, four headings Big/Serious/Humorous/Other), the six weeks in June spent laying out the season and the eight-week-per-episode timetable, a step-by-step transcript of a real notes meeting, plus Smallville's anonymous script reads and Dexter's February camp → reference.md Section 19.**

**The strict definition of worst case** (Douglas ch. 4, the rule most often gotten wrong): **the worst thing is "the thing that opposes the protagonist's goal / the antagonist's victory," not generic badness** — a doctor fears his wife does not love him, at the same time a patient needs surgery, and it is raining outside; the worst case is his wife rejecting him, **not** the patient dying or a flood. Four companion judging rules (student Sonny's list): **every beat is an action** (a character "realizing" something is not a scene); **the antagonist must be as strong and as motivated as the protagonist**; aim for **a nearly balanced moral equation**; **anchor the worst case at the three-quarter point**. Also: **read the cards as if you were watching the screen — if you are bored, the audience will be bored.**

**The opposing view: reject the outline, reject A/B/C stories** (Mazzara, The Walking Dead, Landau 1e Tip 13). "I don't use outlines… **I can't shoot an outline**… they are used to engage TV and studio executives… None of their notes are applicable to script." Alternative: **give the platform a two- or three-page story document** (in paragraphs, stating what the episode must accomplish and what the main set piece is); he himself uses only a **beat sheet, two or three words per beat** ("Rick kills Shane."). Also **no A/B/C** — "That doesn't always equal a theme. It just pushes the ball further on each one." Instead ask **"What is this episode about?"**; the cost, by his own admission, is that **secondary characters may remain secondary forever**. Same camp: Gilligan (Breaking Bad): six people in the room all day, no A/B/C, episode by episode "brick by brick," the mantra being "Where is Walt's head at? What does he want right now? What is he afraid of?"
→ **How to choose**: if you need platform approval / a line producer will scout and budget from it / you are a freelancer or a newcomer → **you must write an outline**; if you are the showrunner and can personally carry structure → you can use only a story document + a ten-word beat sheet, **but that is a by-product of power, not a methodological superiority**. Multiple storylines and an ensemble whose stories must interweave → A/B/C + colored cards; a pressure drama with a single protagonist → the one question "what is this episode about."

**General room operating rules (a consensus across the books)**
- **Run quickly to each act break and the ending first, then go back and fix it** (J.J. Abrams, quoted in Douglas ch. 5): "get to the act breaks and the end quickly, so you can reverse and make it better."
- **Solve one problem at a time** (Matt Williams, Smith p. 280): ignore the Act Two blow-up; when you get there, change gears. The young writer's mistake is to "chase everything in the script at once."
- **Your job is not to point out the problem but to offer a better solution** (Gurvitz, Smith p. 279): "your job is to **pitch a better joke or story fix**." **The opposing view is retained**: Kalish quotes Norman Lear — "**Don't be afraid to put your finger on something that's wrong, even if you don't know how to fix it.**" → Compromise: say plainly "I don't have a solution, but I don't believe this," and take on the ten minutes of discussion that follows.
- **If the same proposal is rejected three times, stop** (Sandy Frank, Smith p. 281); worse is pitching your way down the ladder (EP → co-EP → supervising producer → story editor) until only a junior writer agrees with you.
- **Put the showrunner in the position of "the person asking for help"** (Craig Thomas, Landau 1e Tip 21): "I'm trying to figure out the story and these guys are helping me do it" — more useful than "What do we as a hive mind think this story should be?" **The room needs different types of people** (Lloyd): "encourage quieter, more thoughtful writers to come forward, and sometimes need to **butterfly net** the more aggressive ones."
- **The best episodes come from real events** (Bays & Thomas): "The best episodes **always** come from real stories. Like, 100 percent of the time, without fail." **An institutional ban can save a room that has gone off the rails** (Johannessen, taking over Dexter S5): he instituted a **no metaphors rule** — "Because everyone came up with metaphors and **nobody came up with an actual story**."
- **Every change has a price** (Miller ch. 9, quoting the room's standard line): "**What does this change buy us?** … If it costs us more than it buys us, we think of another direction."

## V. Taking and Giving Notes

**5.1 The seven rules for taking notes** (Goldberg ch. 13, p. 103–106): ① **The most important thing is to listen. Do not argue, do not explain, do not defend** — "we think it's a cliché and we want you to change it. **Say 'got it' and move on.**" ② **But nodding is not enough**: if a note will create more problems than it solves, **ask what problem the note is trying to fix** ("people giving notes are all trying to help; they just often give a solution rather than a problem"). ③ **Distinguish two kinds of notes**: **overall** (tone, style, characterization, structure — the most serious) and **line/page**; overall notes divide again into "the ones that give you a roadmap" and **"the killer"** (says only that they don't like it, gives no solution); with the latter, **do not just write it down and leave** — you must throw out a suggestion on the spot to start a discussion — "it's in no one's best interest to let you leave the room without an idea for a fix." ④ **Do all the line notes**; do not pick and choose (whatever annoyed them in one draft will only annoy them more when they see it again). ⑤ **Write the notes down** — writers who claim to have good memories almost never include the notes they were given in the rewrite. ⑥ **Got a note you hate? "Do the notes. And do them well."** There is only one reason: **"Whether you do it or not, the note is going to get done."** ⑦ **Take your ego out of the conference room** — the first draft of seaQuest was met by Hasburgh with "Hated it. Hated it. Hated it."; the writer and his partner vented for a night, and the next day opened with "**We know you hate the script. So forget it. Don't bother giving us notes on this draft**," threw the script in the trash, and said "let's go back to the outline and figure out how to make this a script you'll love" — and it became an efficient working meeting.

> **First, be clear about what in this section is actually useful.** "Daring to reject a wrong note" does not need a skill to teach it — when you get an opinion like "cut this character," not following it and offering an alternative is common sense. The value of this section lies entirely in **wording and mechanism**: diagnosing "this storyline is scattered" as "the orchestration is homogeneous" (so you know to change the unit of escalation rather than cut people), translating "we can't shoot it" into "this draft advances by changing locations," and the ranking below. **Reason from the argument, not from bravado.**
>
> **The ranking of ⑥ against ② / 5.3 / Diagnostic 26 (this item is added by this pack; Goldberg does not give it).** Literally, ⑥ ("notes you hate must also be done, and done well") and ② ("if it will create more problems, first ask what it is trying to fix") contradict each other. Define the boundary by their respective examples:
> - **⑥ governs notes that you merely dislike** — a difference of taste, thinking it is vulgar, thinking it is not classy enough. Do all of these, and do them well. The reason still holds: "whether you do it or not, the note is going to get done."
> - **② / 5.3 / Diagnostic 26 govern notes that will break something else** — that violate established conditions (the character list, what has already aired, the episode count, the budget), or that create more problems than they solve. These **are not executed literally**: first translate the "solution" you were given back into a "problem," then offer an alternative that solves the same problem.
> - **The test is one question**: if you do not comply, whose taste goes unsatisfied; if you do comply, what structural thing breaks? **If the latter has an answer, take ②.**
> - Whichever path you take, **the on-site action is the same**: do not argue, do not explain, say "got it," write it down. The disagreement is about what you do after you go back, not at the meeting table.

Telephone code table (the real meanings of "Nice work" / "Really good first draft" / "Thanks for getting it done so fast") → reference.md Section 10.

**5.2 Use a recorder, not a laptop** (Douglas ch. 5, three reasons): ① you are not fast enough, you will not capture it all, and you cannot tell which of several contradictory opinions is worth recording; ② under pressure, what you type on a laptop "has all the detail and nuance of a tweet"; ③ **with your head buried in a screen you are not in the discussion**, and you are always one step behind. (Smith p. 263 also recommends bringing a small recorder — "after you sell an idea, a producer may dictate five pages of story outline.")

- **5.3 metabolize: do not execute it, digest it first** — Le Bureau's six-page note and Éric Rochant's four-step process → reference.md Section 10.
- **5.4 Find your own readers first: warm read → cold read** — how to use the two kinds of readers, the one question you must ask, the three rules for filtering notes, reading aloud and single-strand revision → reference.md Section 10.

## VII. Production Reality as a Creative Trigger

One core principle (Goldberg ch. 16, p. 129): **"Know what your script is about and you'll know what you can lose, and what you have to keep."** For long cases, see reference.md Sections 13–17.

- **7.1 Everything costs money, and locations are the first thing changed**: the itemized account of a cat becoming a dog, the page-count economics of shooting 7–12 pages a day and the cost of company moves, the case where it "worked" in West Wing's "The Drop-In" → reference.md Section 13.

**7.2 Translate the "solution" back into the "problem" (the most transferable rule).** When you hear "move scenes 18 and 34 off the standing sets, cut the car chase," **ask them what problem they are facing** (p. 132–133). The truth may be: the standing sets have 8 extra pages scheduled, and stunts are $5,000 over budget. So you give a better solution: move the four-page walk-and-talk at Fred's Bar into the standing sets (gaining a full day of stage shooting, saving one location fee); keep the car chase, cut two one-line day players, and cut the bar fight in Act One (the bar is gone anyway), offsetting the $5,000. **"they won't object, as long as you come up with a better one."** Companion posture: **do not enter bunker mentality** — "If you try to hold on to everything you wrote, you'll lose it all."

**7.3 The bottle episode forced by budget**: the deficit-financing economic model of West Wing's "17 People," Warner's five prohibitions and Sorkin's verbatim transformation "I got to write a play," how to write a six-night teaser → reference.md Section 14.

→ **When you are told "this episode cannot spend money," do not think "which scenes can we cut"; think "which two characters have a confrontation that should have happened long ago, and which secret in the season arc should move forward a step."** Supporting data: this episode takes place in one night, in one building; the teaser is only 6 pages yet spans **six nights** (using `TITLE: The Same Night / Two Nights Later / …` + the repeated sound of a ball bouncing off a wall as transitions, with only one or two exchanges per segment) — **the cheapest way to write a teaser: one room, one action motif, six repetitions that escalate.** Similar: Sopranos "Pine Barrens." For the bottle episode's place in the season shape, see `sw-series-structure`.

- **7.4 Actors: vetoes, availability, bodies, and writing for an actor**: the three redoings of "Sammo Blammo," the writers' guidelines parameters for Diagnosis Murder, the actor cases in Fleabag and Succession → reference.md Section 15; **Fellowes's 22 rules distilled from 419 footnotes, and their decision types (editing choices / historical accuracy / actor-driven rewrites / production cost), are in Section 24**.

You are the showrunner and can carry structure → you may skip it, but that is **a by-product of power** |
| 2 | A/B/C stories | Chase, Douglas, and Landau all use them | Mazzara and Gilligan refuse, asking only "what is this episode about" | Multi-strand ensemble → A/B/C; single-protagonist pressure drama → one question (cost: secondary characters stay secondary, as Mazzara admits) |
| 3 | Whether to know the season ending before the season starts | Armstrong enters the room with a clear season ending; West: "We know what's in episode twelve" | Gould has no master plan; Feldman did not know Steve would die at the end of S1 (it was pitched by a Co-EP in the room's first month) | High continuity, endgame-driven → fix the ending first; a relationship show whose asset is actor chemistry → allow it to be decided later |
| 4 | Whether newcomers should speak up more | Lapiduss: "almost better to kind of not speak up too much"; Konner: "**You haven't yet earned the right to show disrespect**" | Kalish quotes Norman Lear: "**Don't be afraid to put your finger on something that's wrong, even if you don't know how to fix it**" | **Watch the showrunner's signals**; the compromise is to say "I don't believe this" while taking on the discussion — do not just report problems without offering solutions |
| 5 | Whether to spec the series you plan to pitch | Douglas's Rule 2: don't (outsiders don't know the show's minefields) | Her own "fairy tale": breaking in by repeatedly submitting specs to the same show | Follow A by default; B works only with the stamina to "trade three scripts for a note" |
| 6 | How many to bring to a pitch, and how long each | Goldberg: 3 fully formed + half a dozen half-baked | Smith: 10–15 springboards; Blum: 3–5 sentences each, six at most | Take the number from Smith, the length from Blum, the delivery from Goldberg |
| 7 | Write long or write short | Armstrong: 70+ pages, "I rewrite long" — writing long = not having to choose too early | Kelley: "If I can say something in four words, I will never use five" | Have final cut and believe in the economies of scale of dialogue pages → write long; submission/spec stage → write short ("The fat ones are always the last to be picked") |
| 8 | Write fast or revise long | Kelley: "**I usually write a script in four or five days. If a script takes me a week or longer, it usually isn't very good**" | Armstrong: about 15 drafts for a pilot, then **at least 30, usually 50 drafts** per episode | On staff, weekly broadcast, rushing to table read → Kelley mode (pressure is part of quality); development, with final cut, dense subtext → Armstrong mode |
| 9 | Whether to discuss theme/tone in the room | Hillian: thematic unity is central; once the story is set, go back and fuse A/B/C into the season theme | McNamara: "**I'm all about the characters**"; Levinson refuses an overarching theme; Berg never discussed tone | Theme can be front-loaded or back-loaded, but **after breaking the story, check back at least once**; if tone is the buyer's biggest question, answer it with what you shoot |
| 10 | Whether performance and editing can replace writing | Fellowes: "A good actor's skill will always obviate some of the dialogue"; Dollner: "**Editing is essentially the final draft of the script**" | Armstrong: "the final draft of the show isn't the script. **It's the version we air.**" | No conflict: **treat editing as the final draft, but give the editor enough choices on the page first** — that is the reason for "writing long" and "shooting alts" |

## X. Diagnostic Checklist

**Before entering the room**　1 How many episodes have you seen? Have you broken down the title sequence shot by shot? Can you state the franchise in ≤4 lines? Could my story be moved to another show (if yes, it is wrong)?　2 How many springboards did you bring? Can you tell each in 3–5 sentences? How many came from "what occurs to a character in stasis," and how many are guesses at this year's big arc (delete the latter without exception)?　3 Have you done a pre-pitch? Does the person you are pitching to have hiring power (do not pitch to the story editor)?　4 Are you ready to have the subject or the killer changed on the spot? Can you tell "which change really destroys everything" and give the reason?　5 Do you know what kind of room this is (one that argues / one that is quiet), and who is actually running the show?　6 Have you listed the standing sets/locations and their exact wording, the spelling of character names, and the dialogue heading format?

**When breaking story**　7 How many beats does each story have? A story 12–16? B story 8–12? C story 3–8? Is the total around thirty-five beats?　8 Are the three tent-poles standing? **Is the worst case "the thing that opposes the protagonist's goal / the antagonist's victory," or just generic badness?**　9 Is every beat an action ("realizing" does not count as a scene)? Am I bored when I read the cards?　10 Is the room solving **one** problem now, or chasing four at once?　11 For every change, "What does this change buy us?" — does it buy more than it costs?　12 Is the pitch opening "This is shit but –" (cut) or "This is the trashy version" (keep)? Has the same proposal been rejected more than three times?

**Before delivering a document**　13 Is the leave-behind one page, three paragraphs, with a beginning, middle, and end? Have you written both the "platform version" and the "show version"?　14 Is the beat sheet 6–7 scenes per act and 6–20 pages total? **Does every scene specify the location and filmable behavior?**　15 Does the outline contain anything "invisible on screen" (the protagonist's fear of heights, a character's inner consciousness)? If so, change it into how it is played, or cut it.　16 What day of the story is this? Do day and night match? Are characters at work when they should be at work?　17 Have you written down verbatim the lines the showrunner said in the room and put them in? Have you left any rotten temp dialogue in?　18 Did you finish a day early? **Let it cool for a day, then polish, then deliver on time** (do not deliver early).

**After taking notes**　19 Did you argue, explain, or defend? Did you say "got it"? Did you record it or write down every note?　20 Which are overall notes and which are line notes? Is there a "killer"? Did you throw out a fix on the spot?　21 Did you go through every word and layer of meaning (without rebutting), digest it (without copying), and come back with your own questions?　22 Do the notes from multiple readers repeat? Which one points to a weakness I already knew about? Which one makes me deeply uncomfortable (that one is most likely right)?　23 Can a cold reader say "where this story is going"? Did you do **all** the line notes?

**Rewriting and production**　**23a Have you read back the diagnostic table item by item before delivering?** (Every item must be answered with "where in the revision was this solved." The most common failure is not missing a diagnosis but **diagnosing it and only half-solving it** — writing the problem into the diagnostic table and committing it again in the revision. If you cannot say where, it is not fixed yet.)　**23b Have you checked item by item against episodes already aired/locked?** (Has the hook planted in earlier episodes been paid off in this one? Does this episode pick up from the last image of the previous episode? Does this episode spend something early that a later episode has already locked in to use — a scene, a prop, a medical condition, a price increase?) This item appears in no diagnostic checklist outside this skill, and it is the most frequent failure point when rewriting an aired show.　24 What is this episode really about? On that basis, what can be lost and what must be kept?　25 Are these locations the core of the scene's meaning or decoration? How many scenes can be merged into standing sets?　26 Has someone given you a "solution"? Did you translate it back into the "problem" (how many extra pages, how much over budget) and offer a better solution?　27 Has the budget compressed this episode into a single set / no guest cast? Then **which two characters have a confrontation that should have happened long ago, and which secret in the season arc should move forward a step**?　28 How many alts did you prepare (0–15)? How many colleagues did you ask for alts (3–5)? Did you keep one freebie take?　29 For every cut, did you note "what was lost"? Should it be restored in a later episode?　30 Is this episode already beyond saving? (If so, say so and put your energy into the next one.)

**Career self-check**　31 Is my office the farthest and quietest one on the floor? Did I hide at home to write this week? If so, what happened on the show this week that I don't know about?　32 Is my writing better or worse now than when I joined? If worse, have I talked to the showrunner?　33 Do I have a second writing income / a non-writing job / someone who can share expenses?

## XI. Workflow: An Episode from Assignment to Delivery

| Step | Action | Passing Standard |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | **Confirm the process**: does the room break story together or separately? How full does the outline need to be? Who gives notes, and in what order? Ask the production assistant for past outlines and scripts as format samples | All three questions answered |
| 1 | **Write this show's rules on one page**: the franchise in one sentence; three tone rules reverse-engineered from the title sequence; standing sets/locations and their wording; character names and headings; page count and maximum pages for series regulars; shooting days | One page filled |
| 2 | **Break story**: log line (one sentence per story) → two tests → three tent-poles → work backward to the middle beats → beat count falls within the ratio → physically reorder once on slips of paper or cards | Worst case is "the antagonist's victory"; every beat is an action |
| 3 | **leave-behind** (if approval is needed): one page, three paragraphs; two versions; the conspiracy line uses "What we will discover is that…" / "What X doesn't know is that…" | Both versions written |
| 4 | **outline / beat sheet**: 6–7 scenes per act, about one page per act; every step is a scene with a protagonist, a goal, and opposition; slug lines complete; note the story day; put the showrunner's words in verbatim; no rotten temp dialogue | Delivered within 7 working days |
| 5 | **Take the first round of notes and revise the outline**: listen, record, write it down, say got it; ask what problem lies behind each overall note; throw out a fix on the spot for a killer note | Outline is **locked** |
| 6 | **First draft (14-day method)**: 28 beats ÷ 2 scenes/day; each day read and revise yesterday's → write today's first scene → stop → step away → reread and revise → write the second scene → stop. If you drift too far from the approved beats, **make a phone call** | Finish a day early, cool for a day, **deliver on time** |
| 7 | **Notes meeting and second draft**: classify notes; do all line notes; metabolize overall notes before acting; there may be a polish after the second draft | Every note can be found in the script |
| 8 | **Into prep and shooting**: the script becomes colored pages (pink→blue→green…, changes marked with asterisks). Prepare three things: ① a one-page "what is this episode really about"; ② a list of "scenes that can be merged into standing sets" with page counts; ③ the habit of translating "solution-type notes" back into problems | When a single-set/no-guest-cast requirement comes, return to 7.3 |
| 9 | **alts and the set**: choose 0–15 lines, send them to 3–5 colleagues for alts, filter down to 5–10 and take them to set; after shooting the scripted version, keep one freebie take; treat an actor's paraphrase as an implied note | alts in your pocket |
| 10 | **Editing and review**: treat editing as the final draft; note item by item **what was lost** in each cut and decide whether to restore it later; store cut material in the cut-outs document; keep all drafts | Loss list complete |
| 11 | **The next episode**: watch new shows; stay in touch with producers who have bought your scripts; **record the pilot and one or two episodes of every new show, think of two stories for each, and have your agent approach the showrunner**; tell your agent what you can and cannot write; **write another spec in a different genre** | "If it's easy, you're not stretching." (Douglas) |

## XII. Training

1. **Reverse-engineer the title sequence** (Goldberg ch. 9 exercise): record the main titles of three series, break them down shot by shot, and for each shot write one sentence on "why it is there and what it wants to convey"; then write three things the show allows and three it forbids.
2. **Beat sheet reverse engineering** (ch. 11 exercise): record an episode of your favorite show and break it down scene by scene, act by act into a beat sheet (using the real slug lines). "You're holding a snapshot of the series."
3. **Budget-note exercise** (ch. 12 exercise): take the beat sheet from the previous exercise, pretend you wrote it, and receive notes — **cut three exteriors for budget and find four other scenes to place on standing sets**. Rewrite without changing the plot.
4. **Actor-illness exercise** (ch. 16 exercise): same beat sheet, the second lead has the flu — **write him/her out of the episode and make the story hold**. How much can you keep? Which conflicts must be redone or abandoned?
5. **Notes-meeting exercise** (ch. 13 exercise): show a story idea to a partner/friend/"sworn enemy," listen without arguing, write down all the notes, thank them; look at it again a day later. Then do something rarely possible in television: **take only what is useful**.
6. **Two-minute pitch** (ch. 8 exercise): tell a friend about a movie you saw last weekend and watch when they drift off and when they want to escape; use that to sharpen your delivery. Then pitch three stories you have for a current show the same way.
7. **trashy version exercise** (Prebble): write the pulpiest, most over-the-top version of the episode you are working on, then dial it back three notches. Record what was lost and what was kept at each notch.
8. **stasis list** (Douglas Rule 3): pick a current show and list "five impulses or issues that would arise in the regular characters when they are behaving normally," with one sentence after each on "why they do it." Do not write this year's big arc.
9. **Single-strand revision** (Miller): take one of your own scripts, pull all the B-story scenes into a separate document, revise them until they stand, then put them back. Compare the page count and clarity before and after.
10. **Ten chairs** (Wells): find 3–5 people to form a temporary room and break an episode of a current show in six hours: four-heading whiteboard (Big / Serious / Humorous / Other) → list beats for each story (A/B/C) → cut apart and order → assign one person to write the treatment → the whole group gives notes → he revises → the whole group gives notes again. Record how long each step took.
