# Story Structure (故事结构)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

This skill merges six structural systems: Syd Field's 《电影剧本写作基础》 (Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting) (paradigm/plot points), Blake Snyder's 《救猫咪》 (Save the Cat!) (the fifteen-beat BS2 sheet, the board), Robert McKee's 《故事》 (Story) (levels of structure, story triangle, inciting incident, progressive complications, crisis-climax-resolution), Julian Hoxter's 《编剧的十二条法则》 (The Creative Screenwriter: 12 Rules) (nine beats, A/B story), Neill Hicks's 《编剧的核心技巧》 (The Essential Craft of Screenwriting) (three acts = attraction/anticipation/satisfaction, ten elements), and 陆军 (Lu Jun)'s 《编剧理论与技法》 (Playwriting Theory and Technique) (qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé, eight ways to open, eight ways to end, diseases of the climax, eight taboos of transformation, six kinds of change). They are compatible with one another: **Field gives the skeleton, Snyder gives the page numbers, McKee gives the principles, Hoxter gives the beat names, Hicks gives the audience psychology, and Lu Jun gives the practical techniques of Chinese drama.**

For comparison tables, examples, and film-by-film plot points, see [reference.md](reference.md) in this directory.

## I. The Levels of Structure (McKee)

- **Structure** = the selection of a series of events from the characters' lives, composed into a strategic sequence that provokes specific emotions and expresses a particular view of life.
- **Event** = change, which must be expressed through **value** (a binary that can flip between positive and negative at any moment: life/death, love/hate, freedom/slavery, truth/lie...). Rain in East Africa is a coincidence, not a story event; the rain that a rainmaker strikes out of conflict is.
- Levels: **beat** (the alternation of action/reaction) → **scene** (an action in relatively continuous time and space, expressed through conflict, that turns a value-charged situation on at least one level; a minor change) → **sequence** (2–5 scenes whose impact escalates to a peak; a moderate change; give each sequence a title) → **act** (a series of sequences crowned by a climax scene of major reversal) → **story** (a series of acts, the final act climax = the story climax, a change that is **absolute and irreversible**; changes at the scene/sequence/act level can all be reversed — E.T. dies in Act Two and comes back).
- **No scene without a turn**: note the value's positive or negative charge at the start, note it again at the end; if it is the same, ask "why is this scene here?" — almost always for exposition → cut it and weave the information in elsewhere.
- Plot = "navigating dangerous terrain"; every story has a plot.

### The Story Triangle (deciding which structure you are writing)
- At the apex, **archplot** (classical design): active protagonist, external conflict, continuous time, causal unity, closed ending — "a mirror of the human mind," the largest audience.
- Left corner, **miniplot** (minimalism): open ending, internal conflict, passive protagonist, multiple protagonists (《意外的旅客》 (The Accidental Tourist), 《温馨家族》 (Parenthood), 《征服者佩尔》 (Pelle the Conqueror)).
- Right corner, **antiplot**: non-linear time, coincidence, non-continuous reality (《下班后》 (After Hours), 《周末》 (Weekend); Roger Rabbit turns two-dimensional once and it becomes a rule).
- Below the baseline lies **nonplot** (stasis).
- Commandments: a writer must live by writing, must first master classical form (Bergman, Fellini, Godard, and Altman all mastered archplot first), and must believe in what he writes — "to be different for the sake of being different is as empty as blindly following the laws of commerce."

### The Three Structural Types (顾仲彝 / Gu Zhongyi, pp. 0169–0182)
- **Open form** (开放式, kāifàng shì): the plot is presented on stage from beginning to end, exactly as it is (《梁祝》 (Butterfly Lovers), 《威尼斯商人》 (The Merchant of Venice)). Its strengths: it has a beginning and an end, the scenes are lively and easy to understand, and because its time span is long, character has a process of development. Its weaknesses: when the breadth is wide the depth is shallow — "attend to the plot and you cannot attend to delicate characterization; add transition scenes and the big scenes are inevitably dispersed." **The wider the breadth, the more refined the chosen plot must be.**
- **Closed form** (锁闭式, suǒbì shì; retrospective / endgame form): only the climax through the resolution are dramatized; backstory is gradually revealed through recollection and introspection as the plot unfolds (the endgame form of 《玩偶之家》 (A Doll's House) compresses a loan from eight years earlier into two conversations in Act One; the retrospective form of 《群鬼》 (Ghosts) is "more than nine-tenths a review of the past"). Its strengths: concentration and tightness, inner and outer action interlocking, and the deeper you dig the deeper it goes. Its weaknesses: it is not lively and easily monotonous, and it is unsuited to reflecting a broad social life. **Measure**: recollection must be dominated by the present and subordinate the past; once the action of the present weakens, you have the deviation of Ghosts.
- **Tableau play** (人像展览式, rénxiàng zhǎnlǎn shì; John Howard Lawson's Tableau Play): many characters and little plot, "like a group portrait," extremely slow progression, potential conflict stronger than external conflict, **no prominent protagonist**, and the characters who run through to the end often serve only as connecting threads (《在底层》 (The Lower Depths), 《日出》 (Sunrise), 《上海屋檐下》 (Under the Eaves of Shanghai), 《茶馆》 (Teahouse)); the loose structure is held together by theme rather than causality.
- **Choosing a type is not choosing the best**: Kholodov's **centripetal force** (extreme concentration of time, place, and characters to preserve unity of action) and **centrifugal force** (the demand to express the richness of life completely and naturally) — "every play is like the resultant of two forces"; "you must use it according to the material (the subject); never cut the foot to fit the shoe." **Self-check**: if you cannot say which type you are writing, you have probably crammed the breadth of open form into the running time of closed form.
- **Interface**: Gu classifies full-length plays as open form and excerpted highlight scenes (折子戏, zhézi xì) as closed form, and concludes that classical Chinese opera has no tableau play (see `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang`); the tableau play is the interface with the later spoken-drama layer (《茶馆》 (Teahouse) by 老舍 (Lao She)).

## II. The Three-Act Paradigm and Plot Points (Field)

```
Act I Setup          Act II Confrontation          Act III Resolution
pp. 1–30             pp. 30–90                     pp. 90–120
           Plot Point I (pp. 25–27)             Plot Point II (pp. 85–90)
```
- One page = one minute. **Plot point** = "an incident or event that locks into the story and spins it in another direction"; a finished script may have fifteen plot points, but the two at the act breaks are the "anchors."
- **Before you write, you must know four things: the ending, the opening, Plot Point I, and Plot Point II.** Know the ending first: "You don't have to know the details, but you must know what happens at the end."
- **The pivot-action method** (支点动作法, zhīdiǎn dòngzuò fǎ — a single mid-play action used as a fulcrum), after 魏明伦 (Wei Minglun), p. 0081: another starting route — first fix a mid-play action as the fulcrum, then use it to push the backstory backward and the resolution forward. 《巴山秀才》 (The Bashan Scholar) uses "burning the books to lodge a complaint" as its fulcrum: backward, it pushes out an extremely low starting point — a scholar who has failed the examinations for years; forward, it pushes out the "certain death" that comes from violating ritual law; the fulcrum falls at the exact center of the play (the opening of the fifth of eight scenes). **Failure mode**: if the pivot action does not violate the character's existing values, it can push nothing out in either direction. **Self-check**: write the pivot in one sentence and ask, "Who must he be before he can do this?" and "How must the world deal with him after he does it?" Only when you can answer both is it valid.
- Each of the three acts also has a beginning/middle/end; each act has a "direction."
- **The first ten pages must establish three things**: who the main character is; the dramatic premise (what the story is about); and the dramatic situation (the circumstances surrounding the story). The set-up is complete by page 30. Two kinds of opening: a strong visual action sequence (《星球大战》 (Star Wars)) or a fast expository character introduction (《洗发》 (Shampoo): the moans in bed are interrupted by a phone call).
- The opening is tied to the ending: a road movie begins and ends on the road, a gambling story begins and ends with a gamble, a boxing story begins and ends with a fight, "Will they report it?"
- **Sequence** = "a series of scenes linked by a single idea," the spine of the screenplay (wedding, chase, homecoming, bank robbery). Fix the sequence idea (the cup) before filling in the content (the coffee). Frank Pierson wrote 《三伏天下午》 (Dog Day Afternoon) with only twelve sequences, writing four first (the opening, the two plot points, the ending) and then adding eight.
- Theme = action + character: "A screenplay is like a noun: a certain person in a certain place doing his thing." Action divides into the physical and the emotional.
- Variants: 《第三类接触》 (Close Encounters of the Third Kind) is 40/40/40; in 《纳施维尔》 (Nashville) the protagonist is the city — "just follow the protagonist and you will find the plot points."

## III. The Fifteen BS2 Beats (Snyder, 110 pages)

| # | Beat | Page | Key point |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Opening image | 1 | Tone, genre, scale; a snapshot of the protagonist's "before"; the opposite of the final image |
| 2 | Theme stated | 5 | Usually one line spoken to the protagonist by a non-protagonist; the screenplay = an argument built around it |
| 3 | Set-up | 1–10 | Every main-story character appears or is mentioned; "the six things that need fixing" (a ticking clock, detonated and cured later); a storm is coming, and stasis is death |
| 4 | Catalyst | 12 | A telegram, a dismissal, catching an affair, only three days left; bad news disguised as good news; if it runs to page 20, cut it |
| 5 | Debate | 12–25 | The last chance to say "this is crazy"; pose one question |
| 6 | Break into two | 25 | No later than page 25; the protagonist **decides for himself** to enter; he cannot be lured in or wake up already on the spaceship |
| 7 | B story | 30 | Usually a love line that carries the theme; the new character is the reverse image of an Act One character |
| 8 | Fun and games | 30–55 | **Delivers the promise of the premise**; the core of the poster, the material of the trailer |
| 9 | Midpoint | 55 | A false victory or a false defeat; "the stakes escalate"; the mirror opposite of "all is lost" |
| 10 | Bad guys close in | 55–75 | The hardest to write; the external antagonist regroups + the internal team splits |
| 11 | All is lost | 75 | Worse than the beginning; **the whiff of death** (Obi-Wan dies; if not, put in a goldfish or a flower) |
| 12 | Dark night of the soul | 75–85 | 5 seconds to 5 minutes; only after being defeated can the lesson be learned |
| 13 | Break into three | 85 | The solution comes from a B-story character or the theme discussion; main plot and subplot merge |
| 14 | Finale | 85–110 | The villains are resolved one by one from low to high (henchmen die first); the protagonist must change the world; "synthesis" |
| 15 | Final image | 110 | The opposite of the opening; without it, Act Two is missing something |

The three acts = thesis / antithesis / synthesis. **A set-up longer than 25 pages = the audience wants a refund** (《少数派报告》 (Minority Report) waits 40 minutes to drop the hook).

### The Board (40 Cards)
- The board is divided into four rows: 1–25 / 25–55 (to the midpoint) / 55–85 / 85–110, with a turning point at the end of each row.
- Pin the most exciting scenes first → discover the blanks → pin the three main turning points (the midpoint first, then all is lost as its opposite, then break into three).
- Common diseases: Act One gets 20 cards crammed into it (all backstory, finally compressed into one "protagonist introduced" card); **Act Three always has only two cards** ("the protagonist knows what to do" and "the big showdown") — go back and check the six things that need fixing, the B story, and the villain list.
- On each card write **+/-** (the emotional change in the scene; if you can't think of one, throw the card out) and **><** (who has a goal, who blocks the way, who wins; one conflict per scene).
- Field's card method has the same origin: 3×5 cards with a few words each, different colors for different acts; the process is one day for Act One, two days for Act Two, one day for Act Three, then several weeks of shuffling and moving scenes. "Trust your story." Once you start writing, the board can be thrown away.

## IV. The Nine Beats (Hoxter) and the Linear Development Table

- Act One (<30 minutes): **basic exposition** (the protagonist has a problem/desire; the inciting incident) → **the argument** (he wants it but does not know how to get it; the first commitment is weak and unrealistic — in 《乔乔》 (Jojo Rabbit), throwing a hand grenade and injuring himself = "a long way to go").
- Act Two (≈45 minutes, in two halves): **initial progress** → **raising the stakes** (the wolf chases him into the cave and there is a mother bear; the ex-girlfriend is dating the ex-boyfriend you hate, and tomorrow he becomes your department head) → **midpoint** (the second commitment, no way back, the protagonist becomes a hero) → **commitment** (aid) → **crisis** (the lowest point + revelation; the first opening of the heart).
- Act Three (<30 minutes): **confrontation** (the plan meets counterattack) → **resolution** (the goal achieved, the theme revealed, the world changed; "give us a moment to feel it").
- Different stories weight different beats: 《冬天的骨头》 (Winter's Bone) has the longest argument (rejected at house after house); 《驯龙高手》 (How to Train Your Dragon) spends more than 20 minutes on initial progress.
- **A story** = the main line, what the protagonist wants, the surface motivation; **B story** = the key relationship, "who you would tell your innermost thoughts to," strongest in Act Two; at the end of Act Two the B-story character helps the protagonist make the Act Three plan, and when the plan is executed the A story is completed. Once the theme is set it never changes; what changes is the attitude toward it.
- The six questions of "Are three acts right for me?": Is it a popular genre? Is it strongly goal-driven? Is it plot-driven? Is the ending clearly resolved? Does the protagonist undergo a major transformation? Is it aimed at a mainstream audience?
- Tarantino's three-act information gap: in Act One the audience does not understand and the characters know more; in Act Two it is a tie; in Act Three the audience is far ahead.
- Goldman's Ten Commandments: never take the crisis away from the protagonist; never let the protagonist have it easy; never argue for the sake of arguing; never mystify and startle; respect the audience; know your world as God knows the world; if the elements are already rich, do not make them artificially complex; exploration must do more than bring the character into the deepest conflict; never be on-the-nose — every line of dialogue must carry a subtext; rewrite and rewrite.

## V. Three Acts = Attraction / Anticipation / Satisfaction (Hicks)

- Act One must create **attraction**: make the audience "willing to go along"; Act Two manages **anticipation**: keep the audience guessing the next step and guessing wrong; Act Three gives **satisfaction**: pay off every expectation, but not in the expected way.
- **Drama is conflict**: arrange conflict, compress time, break events down — 《邦妮和克莱德》 (Bonnie and Clyde) breaks "stealing a car" into a chain of small actions: the parking space, the key, starting the engine, and every step can go wrong.
- **The ten-element checklist** (fill in each item before writing an outline): backstory by subtraction (keep only what relates to the goal); no flashbacks; inner need; inciting incident; exterior goal; preparation; the opposing force must be stronger; self-revelation; obsession; the battle must be won by the protagonist personally; resolution.
- Every scene does two things: advance the external goal + expose the inner need. "Less is more."
- The five premise questions: Who? Wants what? Why now? Who stops him? What is the result?

## VI. Structural Techniques of the Chinese Short Play (Lu Jun)

**Boundary**: qǐ chéng zhuǎn hé is the whole-play structural level of the short play / one-act play / Chinese opera; feature films and series borrow only its local techniques (the eight openings, addition/subtraction/multiplication/division, the four diseases of the climax, the eight endings), not its skeleton; for opera projects see Section II of `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang`.

### Qǐ Chéng Zhuǎn Hé (起承转合) = Phoenix Head, Pig Belly, Leopard Tail (凤头猪肚豹尾)
- **Qǐ (opening)**: the first conflict at the beginning of the contradiction, the fuse is lit; clearly reveal the appearance and the direction of strength of both sides; usually very short, with exposition hidden in action — steep and striking.
- **Chéng (development)**: about two-thirds of the play, linked ring by ring, climbing step by step, winding and turning, now fast now slow; 亨脱 (Hunter): "To make the horse run forward you kick it in the belly, but you also hold the reins so that it keeps a natural pace."
- **Zhuǎn (turn)**: the peak of the climax, where character and theme go deepest, "thunder and lightning."
- **Hé (resolution)**: resolution follows the turn immediately, brief and with lingering flavor.
- The opening should make people expect, the development should draw them in, the turn should shake them, the resolution should make them linger — even a thousand-character sketch needs all four parts.

### Get Into the Drama Fast: Three Dos, Three Don'ts, and Eight Kinds of Opening
- Prefer near to far (make the first cut close to the climax and delete the retrospective account of events before the conflict); prefer simple to complex (one small incident, straight in, with a twist at every turn); prefer movement to stillness (open with a dramatic situation; even one person can create "noise"). Avoid a starting point too far away, a focus too cluttered, and too many doubts — **drama is most afraid of explanation**.
- Eight openings: the conflict method (冲突法, chōngtū fǎ): conflict from the opening line — "The current township head won't come." The situation-planting method (布阵法, bùzhèn fǎ): plant the conflict situation from the start. The heightening method (渲染法, xuànrǎn fǎ): render the main event directly, from the side, or in reverse. The surprise method (意外法, yìwài fǎ): a blind girl comes to apply. The opposite-course method (南辕北辙法, nányuánběizhé fǎ): the lazy man — "If you'd told me, I'd have come to get it myself." The blunt-blow method (当头一棒法, dāngtóu yī bàng fǎ): the father rules that three flower baskets must be woven every night. The self-introduction method (自报家门法, zìbào jiāmén fǎ): chorus / host / telephone call to reduce exposition. The driven-to-rebellion method (逼上梁山法, bī shàng Liángshān fǎ): the old man secretly plants breeding rabbits and is sitting on the wall being interrogated.
- William Archer: the opening must "clearly indicate the direction in which the clouds are gathering on the horizon."

### Keep the Through-Line Clear: Find the Core + Addition, Subtraction, Multiplication, Division
- Rodin cut the "perfect hand" off the Balzac statue — the four-character principle of proper detail and summary: **highlight the key point**. 《孔乙己》 (Kong Yiji) by 鲁迅 (Lu Xun) spends 150 characters on the broken leg and 500 on the fennel beans — find the core, "the heart of a child," as the standard for what to keep and what to cut.
- Wherever character is portrayed, write in detail (one song, three sighs; one wave, three turns; one step, three chants); write the opening and ending in brief, write the "surface" in brief, write anything unrelated to the theme in brief.
- Sorting the through-line: **subtraction** (cut people and events to clear out the shining point; dare to give up what you love), **addition** (supply the social factors and emotional grounds for abnormal behavior), **multiplication** (magnify one ridiculous factor exponentially to the point of exaggeration), **division** (if the entry point is wrong, start over from a new stove).

### Build the Climax High
- "Never put pen to paper until you have found the climax" — Dumas fils: "Do not begin unless you have completely thought through the action and dialogue of the last scene"; Ibsen: "I may almost say that I wrote the play for the sake of the last scene"; the 白玉霜 (Bai Yushuang) case: the red wedding dress turns into the black 奠 (diàn, the character for mourning), the climax was set, and all eight scenes were written in one breath.
- Three paradigms: **driven by character** — 张桂蓉 (Zhang Guirong) moves from forbearance to tearing the whip and taking the land deed: "I am a person, I am a person of the nation! Not a beast you feed"; **driven by environment** — the new system provides the larger environment; in 《警察与小偷》 (The Policeman and the Thief), the fake policeman, guided by the patrolman, enters his role and finally "catches one" — the other is himself; **accident** — the "dead" son suddenly returns.
- Four diseases: no climax (three episodes given equal weight, like a peep show); climax too early ("weeping, cursing, and hanging herself" five minutes in); climax not high enough (a fierce quarrel but the plot skids with no rising action); manufactured climax (kneeling, divorce, splitting the family, slaps at every turn — kneeling is usable only when it is a necessity of character and leads to a new relationship, as in 《包公赔情》 (Bao Gong Makes Amends), where the sister-in-law kneels: "I kneel to my third brother's loyalty, to his service to the country and the people").
- **The run-up to the climax is also drama**: set the obligatory scene, learn to control delay and to suppress before releasing (the magic mirror is first turned into a bronze mirror, then turned back for "one last look").

### Make the Change Smooth (解扣, jiěkòu — untying the knot)
- Eight taboos: passing the conflict upward (a superior sends a person, a call, a document); treating a grave illness lightly (one probe and he sees the light); a remedy that does not fit the disease (saving the girlfriend's father is one thing, "messing around with research" is another); the long sermon of the 赋子板 (fùzǐbǎn — a rapid, extended rhythmic monologue in Chinese opera); handling it offstage (Secretary Zhang criticized him); fetching reinforcements; accidentally discovering a blood tie; the old trap of 请君入瓮 (qǐng jūn rù wèng — "please step into the jar," a trap turned on the trapper).
- Three methods: **the power of environment** (hand the untransformable villain over to an irresistible social force); **the power of setup** (the folk song advances layer by layer: love song → song of oppression → contact code); **the power of action — best of all a masterstroke** (in 《阿二接妻》 (A'er Brings His Wife Home), weaving the basket: it proves the transformation, suits the stage, and echoes the opening).

### Make the Ending Decisive (结尾要绝, jiéwěi yào jué — end with a decisive stroke)
- Think of the ending first, as 曹禺 (Cao Yu) advised. Four taboos: too neat (a halo of idealism); too drawn out ("end when it is time to end" — one word more in 《项链》 (The Necklace) and the lingering flavor is diminished); too jumpy (a divine stroke flies in at the end and inflates the theme without limit); too soft (four lines of offstage singing force a close).
- Eight methods: **thunderclap** (响雷式, xiǎngléi shì): climax and resolution fuse into one — "those typewriters"; "I am the inspector; I've lived next door for three nights"; "he would rather confess"; "the engagement wine we drank last month" and the towel falls. **Unresolved** (悬而未决式, xuán ér wèijué shì): "Should I forgive him?" to the audience. **Extension** (延伸式, yánshēn shì): 《压迫》 (Oppression) — "What is your name?" "I— ah— I—." **Rebound** (反弹式, fǎntán shì): the singer abandons her suicidal lover — "I still have one minute and ten seconds" to catch the train; a gunshot offstage. **Finishing touch** (点睛式, diǎnjīng shì): the fiancée's speech illuminates the whole play. **Echo** (呼应式, hūyìng shì): three gongs at the opening and three at the end, with a contrast of weight. **Cyclical** (轮回式, lúnhuí shì): reunion → memory → back to the reunion — "beware one day of the anger of the masses"; Annie begins writing letters again. **Conclusion** (结论式, jiélùn shì): "a draw"; a summarizing chorus.

### Multiply the Changes
- "Where there is change there is drama." Six kinds: change in relationships (the deceiver and the deceived rotate); change in character; change in fortune (three great rises and falls); change in the conflict situation (one gun changes hands four times); change in atmosphere (duel → courtship); change in narrative mode. Priority of sources: **character ＞ the playwright's craft ＞ accident ＞ outside force**. Five or six marked changes make a short play pass; 《打铜锣》 (Beating the Gong) has fifteen and is the model.

## VII. Inciting Incident and Progressive Complications (McKee)

### Inciting Incident
- Five-part design: inciting incident → progressive complications → crisis → climax → resolution.
- It must be **dynamically developed enough** (moving to Los Angeles is not; wallpaper made of parking tickets plus a felony arrest warrant and an escape down the fire escape is), break the balance completely and push it toward the positive or the negative; **the protagonist must react** (refusal is also a reaction); it arouses the desire to restore balance → object of desire → quest; a complex protagonist also arouses an unconscious desire.
- **It must be on screen**, pose the major dramatic question, and project the **obligatory scene** (the shark and the police chief face to face).
- Placement: the first quarter; it can come in the first second (《码头风云》 (On the Waterfront)) or at 27–32 minutes (《洛奇》 (Rocky), 《卡萨布兰卡》 (Casablanca)); if it is late, a subplot must carry the load; **bring it in as soon as possible but wait until the time is ripe** — how much the audience needs to know in order to react fully (Rocky needs half an hour to earn "Him? That bum?!").
- Creation methods: **how the worst thing can become the best thing** (《克莱默》 (Kramer vs. Kramer)), **how the best can become the worst** (《魂断威尼斯》 (Death in Venice)); "if a story is to be art, it does not tell the middle ground."
- **Story spine**: without an unconscious desire, the conscious goal is the spine; with one, the unconscious desire is the spine, and the conscious goal is allowed to change constantly.
- Hoxter: two kinds of inciting incident — character-driven (a friend suggests substituting) or coincidence (mistaken identity; a distress signal); hook the reader within ten pages.

### Progressive Complications
- Facing ever-stronger antagonism, ever more conflict, the story passes through one **point of no return** after another: each gap rules out an action of equal force. The "soft big belly" (软大肚子, ruǎndàdùzi — the saggy middle) = the writer repeating the scale of Act One action. You must invent hundreds of scenes to mine 40–60.
- **Law of conflict**: "Nothing in a story can move forward without conflict"; the amount of conflict in life is constant (squeeze a balloon).
- **Gap**: the character always takes the smallest conservative action from his subjective point of view → the world responds differently or more strongly than expected → a second action of greater risk. Risk test: "If the protagonist does not get it, what will he lose?" If the answer is "back to normal," it is not worth telling.
- Complication (single level: an action film has only extrapersonal conflict, a soap opera only the personal level) vs. complexity (inner/personal/extrapersonal all turn at once — the French toast in 《克莱默》 (Kramer vs. Kramer) turns three values on three levels). Advice: **do not multiply characters, do not multiply locations; be relatively simple and complex.**
- Four techniques for expressing progress: social progression (the reach of the action spreads outward), personal progression (wedge deeper into private relationships and the inner life), symbolic ascension (the image moves from the particular to the universal, and must remain invisible), ironic ascension (six kinds: finally getting it but too late; being pushed away from the goal yet led toward it; abandoning something only to learn afterward that it was indispensable; heading unconsciously away from the goal in order to reach it; wanting to destroy and shooting yourself in the foot; getting what you thought was misfortune, wanting to be rid of it, and finding it is a great gift).
- **Every level of failure must teach the protagonist one more thing** — 魏明伦 (Wei Minglun), p. 0082: the action line of 《巴山秀才》 (The Bashan Scholar) is "the four-stage lawsuit" — begging for relief → a roundabout accusation (mistaking a cruel official for an honest one) → a clever accusation (placing hope in the court) → a furious accusation that tears the mask off in the court hall; **four lawsuits = four deceptions = four ascents of understanding**, and the action line and the character line are two sides of the same line. **Self-check**: write down the sentence the protagonist newly understands after each failure ("officials protect one another" → "Sichuan is dark, but there is still the court" → "the court will kill me too"); delete any level for which you cannot write it. **Failure mode**: if the protagonist gains no new information between two failures, those two scenes can be merged into one.

### Act Design
- Aristotle: the longer the work, the more major reversals. A feature needs at least three acts (good → bad → ?). Proportion: Act One ≈25% (climax at 20–30 minutes); **the last act is the shortest (⚠️ **there is a numerical conflict with BS2's "enter Act Three at 77%"**: the 110-page table scaled to 100 pages at 77% = a 23-minute Act Three, which exceeds this limit. **Both are feature-film rules, not a conflict of media; take the shorter Act Three** — move the break into Act Three to 78–80%, keeping Act Three under 20 minutes. BS2's percentage is a proportion, not a law) ≤20 minutes** (a sense of acceleration); Act Two's 70-minute swamp → two solutions: **subplots** (each with its own act structure) or **adding acts** (mid-act climax → four acts; five acts = four weddings; seven acts = 《夺宝奇兵》 (Raiders of the Lost Ark)). The cost of many acts: multiplying climaxes breeds clichés and weakens impact. Three-act main plot + subplot is standard.
- **False endings** (E.T. dying, the alien ship being blown up) do not suit most films; the penultimate act climax should reinforce "what can still happen at this late hour."
- **Act rhythm**: the last two act climaxes cannot carry the same charge (do not follow an up with an up, or a down with a down); for an ironic ending, first fix the inclination, then let the penultimate act run the other way.
- **Four relationships of the subplot**: **contradiction** with the controlling idea (a sweet love main line + a betrayal subplot); **resonance** (multi-plot stories root here: "parenthood can never win"); **foreshadowing**, filling the vacuum of a late main plot; **complicating the main plot** (the most important: the love line in a crime film). If it neither contradicts, resonates, foreshadows, nor complicates → it splits the story. The center of gravity must be controlled (Rocky's love line is handled carefully to preserve the sports genre).
- **Break the rules for one reason only: to replace them with something more important** (《蜘蛛女之吻》 (Kiss of the Spider Woman) postpones the inciting incident to the mid-act climax, establishing empathy first and then revealing the deal).

## VIII. Crisis, Climax, Resolution (McKee)

- **Crisis** = decision; the protagonist has exhausted every action and only one remains; the obligatory scene; it must be a true dilemma (choosing one of two goods or the lesser of two evils; a choice between good and evil is not a choice); **the crisis decision must be a deliberate moment of stasis**, not offscreen and not glossed over (《末路狂花》 (Thelma & Louise): "Go? What do you mean, go?"). Crisis and climax can occur in different places but must be cut together; never cut to a subplot.
- **Crisis inside the climax**: pry the gap open once more during the climactic action (the spiral of three crisis decisions in 《帝国反击战》 (The Empire Strikes Back)).
- **Climax**: the supreme reversal need not be noisy or violent, but **it must be full of meaning** — "meaning generates emotion"; the action is pure, clean, and self-evident; Aristotle: "inevitable yet unexpected"; Goldman: "give the audience what they want, but not the way they expect it"; what the audience wants is not a happy ending but emotional satisfaction (downbeat endings also make money); **tonal image** (基调形象, jīdiào xíngxiàng — the single image that embodies the story's tone and controlling idea): a climax written for the eye + a single image of the controlling idea (the handcuffs in 《贪婪》 (Greed); the apartment being dismantled in 《对话》 (The Conversation)).
- Once the climax is set, begin **rewriting from the end backward**; every scene must justify its existence against the climax; if cutting it changes nothing, cut it; the best is when a subplot reaches its climax inside the main plot's climax (Rick solves love + politics + Renault + Strasser in one action); otherwise the least important subplot reaches its climax first.
- "Cinema is the last twenty minutes"; 75% of the energy goes into design, and 75% of that into the final act's climax.
- **Resolution** has three uses: give a scene to any subplot not resolved inside the climax (the main plot can be dragged into the resolution to create a false complication); show the reach of the climax's effect; respect the audience — add a line on the last page, a shot that slowly pulls back.
- Hoxter's three climax questions: Does the villain's failure express the theme? Has the protagonist done something he could not do in Act One? Does it complete the plot line and account for the whole story at the same time?
- Coincidence: introduce it early so that time can build meaning; no coincidence after the midpoint; **never use coincidence to turn the ending** ("the god from the machine" is a lie and an insult; antiplot is the exception; comedy can tolerate it, but the protagonist must already have suffered enormously and never despaired).

## IX. Non-Linear, Multi-Strand, and Ensemble

- Non-chronological (《落水狗》 (Reservoir Dogs) reveals through flashback; 《500 天》 ((500) Days of Summer) evokes memory; 《低俗小说》 (Pulp Fiction) loops; 《妖夜慌踪》 (Lost Highway) loops as a puzzle) — Hoxter: still keep the sequence coherent; frame narrative can be circular (《堤》 (La Jetée), 《十二猴子》 (Twelve Monkeys)).
- Multi-strand plot (《无事生非》 (Much Ado About Nothing), 《传染病》 (Contagion)); portmanteau (《罪恶之城》 (Sin City)); ensemble (《十二金刚》 (The Dirty Dozen) with a protagonist / 《真爱至上》 (Love Actually) and 《通天塔》 (Babel) linked by theme, one inciting incident affecting every strand); minimal plot (《天堂陌客》 (Stranger Than Paradise): three places, three acts, "nothing happens").
- McKee: multiple protagonists each with a desire → multi-plot, held together by **resonance** (variations on the same controlling idea) rather than causality (in Altman's and Jarmusch's films, "the degree of interlock depends on the degree of echo among the strands").
- Flashback: Tony Bill — "When I see a flashback, I know the story is in trouble"; McKee: dramatize it (a miniature play with an inciting incident, progress, and a turn) and **first create the need and desire to know, then use it**; Trottier: "If the reader doesn't care about the present, we don't have to tell the reader about the past"; Hicks: no flashbacks (use the present tense and active voice to compress backstory into present action).
- Chekhovian anti-climax (even-numbered acts, no central climax, events offstage) see `chekhov-dramaturgy`; Ozu-style "events offstage, reactions onstage" see `ozu-screenplay-style`.

## X. Structure Diagnostic Checklist

1. **Can you say what it is about in one or two sentences** (noun form: who, where, doing what)? If not, you are not ready to write.
2. **Do you know the ending, the opening, Plot Point I, and Plot Point II?** Is the midpoint a false victory or a false defeat, and is it the mirror opposite of "all is lost"?
3. Do the first ten pages establish the protagonist, the premise, and the situation? Is the set-up longer than 25 pages? Has the catalyst drifted past page 20?
4. Does the protagonist **decide for himself** to enter Act Two? Does the protagonist take action, or only react to the environment? Is the protagonist asking questions (many question marks in the dialogue = a problem)?
5. Does the value change from the beginning to the end of every scene? Are the scenes that do not change there for exposition?
6. Does every gap in Act Two rule out an action of equal force? Is there a "soft big belly" (repeating the scale of Act One)? Does it accelerate after the midpoint?
7. Is the sum of antagonistic forces stronger than the protagonist at the inciting incident (the protagonist should be the underdog — 战败狗, zhànbài gǒu, "the beaten dog")? Is the villain bad enough ("the villain must be worse")?
8. Is there a whiff of death around page 75? Is the crisis a static dilemma? Is the climax "inevitable yet unexpected"? Is there a tonal image?
9. Does the subplot contradict, resonate with, foreshadow, or complicate the controlling idea? If none, it splits the story.
10. Is the last act the shortest? Do the last two act climaxes carry opposite charges? Does the resolution take care of the subplot and give the audience breathing room?
11. Lu Jun's four questions: Is the entry fast (starting point near, focus single, doubts few)? Is the climax high (not early, not skidding, not manufactured)? Is the change smooth (not passed upward, not treated lightly, not offstage, no reinforcements)? Is the ending decisive (not neat, not drawn out, not jumpy, not soft)?
12. Count the changes: a short play needs at least five or six marked changes, and they should arise from character first.
13. Has Act One already stated the right answer and had it rejected (the Chekhov/Lopakhin method), and is what was promised at the opening paid off or deliberately not paid off at the end?

## XI. Workflow: From Premise to Script

1. **Logline** (Snyder's four elements: an ironic hook + a mental picture + audience and cost + title; add an adjective for the protagonist, an adjective for the villain, and the primal goal); test it by pitching a stranger. Hoxter's logline formula: protagonist + inciting incident + conflict/challenge + stakes + theme, foregrounding the hook; it is not a tagline.
2. **Fix the genre and the story-triangle position**; list comparable films and identify the conventions and clichés.
3. **Fill in the ten elements** (Hicks) and the five premise questions; determine the protagonist's need and obstacle.
4. **Fix the ending first**, then the opening and the two plot points; find the climax's "tonal image."
5. **The BS2 one-page sheet**: fill in the fifteen beats by page number; theme on page 5, catalyst on page 12, break into two no later than page 25, midpoint 55, all is lost 75, break into three 85.
6. **The board with 40 cards**, each marked +/- and ><; Act Three cannot have only two cards.
7. **Step outline** (McKee): one or two sentences per scene — "He enters the room expecting to see her and finds a note saying she has left forever" — with the structural position on the back; the purpose is **to destroy your own work** — sketch a scene a dozen ways and throw them away; once the climax is found, rewrite from the end backward.
8. **Talk the story for ten minutes** and watch the listener's eyes; do not show the outline; an enthusiastic reaction = a soft "wow" and then silence.
9. **Treatment** (60–90 pages, present tense, moment by moment, **no dialogue**, write the subtext) → at this stage delete, add, and reorder scenes.
10. **Screenplay**: five to ten pages a day, and the dialogue is at its best now; when you find a defect, go back to the treatment and fix the setup and payoff. Skipping the process and writing the screenplay directly = a counterfeit, unexplored treatment, and also the slowest method.
11. **The first draft is just getting it down** (Field: better too long than too short; 180–200 pages is better than 90) → cool down and compress → polish. Before you stop each day, start the next scene (Hoxter).
12. **Revise item by item against the goals**: look at Act Two first ("generally, yes"); Snyder's nine revision questions: is the protagonist active and not asking questions; do not dictate the plot; make the villain worse; accelerate the turns; the emotional color wheel; cover the names and test the dialogue; give the supporting characters a limp and an eye patch; push the starting point back; is it primal enough.
