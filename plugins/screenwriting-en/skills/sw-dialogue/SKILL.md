# Dialogue (对白)

> **Output language = the language of the question**; always anchor terminology back to the original terms — see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md); do not coin your own translations.

There is only one core proposition: **dialogue is an action** (McKee: dia + legein = action through words). Beneath every line lie desire, intention, and strategy; "the problems of dialogue are the problems of story," so when dialogue will not come right, first go back and examine the events and the character design. This skill merges the complete system of Robert McKee's 《对白》 (Dialogue); the American practical taboos of Richard Walter and Neill Hicks; 陆军 (Lu Jun)'s theory of Chinese dramatic language, with the section on 唱词 (chàngcí — Chinese-opera lyrics) moved to `sw-chinese-opera-banqiang`; Lajos Egri's character–dialogue relationship; and the line patterns of screwball comedy. For examples of seven scenes analyzed beat by beat, see [reference.md](reference.md).

## I. What Dialogue Is

- **The complete definition**: anything any character says to anyone — to another person, to himself ("all interior monologue is dialogue"), or to the audience (soliloquy, voice-over, direct address to camera). "No character speaks to another person, or even to himself, without a reason"; beneath every line there must be a desire, an intention, and an action = a verbal strategy.
- **Dramatic dialogue** (inside the scene, charged with conflict, every line aimed at an action that produces a reaction) vs. **narrative dialogue** (outside the scene, with the fourth wall gone). Three kinds of dialogue require three kinds of writing (《格拉斯医生》 (Doctor Glas): diary form → address to the reader → dramatization).
- **Three functions**: exposition, characterization, action.
- **Three layers of content** (three concentric spheres): **the said** (vocabulary, wording, syntax, tone, and the character's education, wit, and emotional range); **the unsaid** (thoughts and feelings consciously hidden; the reader instinctively skips past the words to guess the subtext, and the writer must make it perceptible); **the unsayable** (unconscious needs, exposed only in choices made under pressure). **As language, dialogue conveys characterization; as action, dialogue reveals true character.**
- **Action vs. behavior**: no one ever eats ice cream because he is hungry — dispelling sorrow, defying the doctor, or rewarding himself is the action; any line of dialogue that does not answer "what is the real action in the subtext?" is unfinished. Silence also has action; a pause is also a reaction.
- Three questions to ask when writing dialogue: **What does the character want at this moment? What action does he take to try to get it? What words will he speak to carry out that action?** Mark the subtextual action with a **gerund phrase** ("groveling at her feet" / "ignoring his plea").
- Differences of medium: the theater is primarily aural and can be poetic and rhymed (the tacit "as if" convention); film is primarily visual, the camera magnifies pretense, so dialogue must be natural and actable, written for the actor, and open to improvisation (exceptions: stylized realism, non-realism, extreme characters); television, face to face, leans toward conversation; literature is free. **The level of conflict determines the amount of dialogue: the more external social conflict, the less dialogue; the more personal and intimate the conflict, the more dialogue.**
- Walter: most producers read only the dialogue — "the best possible reason to make dialogue extraordinarily brilliant, every word a gem"; yet **the best dialogue is often no dialogue at all** (《黑道家族》 (The Sopranos): Carmela takes off her ring, and Tony simply stares at her). Hicks: a good script is like a poem; dialogue comes from emotion deep inside.

## II. Exposition: Dramatize It, Use It as a Weapon, Keep Secrets

- Include only the minimum information absolutely necessary, and never release it early; list all the facts in order of importance, and repeat and emphasize the important ones. "Make them laugh, make them cry, make them wait."
- **Dramatize vs. tell**: Charlie's "We've known each other twenty years, since school — good morning" (purposeless) → "You're no different from the immature jerk you were at school twenty years ago — smell your clothes" — and the audience absorbs "twenty years" and "school" without noticing.
- **Narrative drive** (information leaks out drop by drop so the audience guesses what comes next; 《第二十二条军规》 (Catch-22): "There's a trap in it") and **presentation as a weapon** (a character uses what he knows as a weapon; "I am your father" disarms the son at the climax of the duel).
- **The most important revelation is a secret**; backstory is not the history of a lifetime but a piece of the past held back for the moment of choosing the lesser of two evils (《灵欲春宵》 (Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf?): the telegram "I swallowed it") — "using backstory to make a turn is the most powerful exposition technique."
- Direct narration has two uses: speed (García Márquez: "Many years later, as he faced the firing squad") and counterpoint (a narrator who ironizes the theme); turn "and then, and then" into dramatized narration or create an inner conflict with another self.
- **Forced-exposition taboos**: scrolling opening credits, newspaper headlines, characters telling each other facts they both already know ("We've known and loved each other for over twenty years, since that college fraternity party..."); Walter's Principle 28: **never let one character tell another something the audience already knows**.
- Hicks: put expository dialogue into an unexpected environment to inject energy into it (an argument over seating at a wedding banquet → a jewelry-store robbery → the policeman is the brother-in-law who owes money); "What do you want from me?" and "Are you all right?" are the two legitimate exceptions to on-the-nose dialogue.
- McKee: do not write what common sense can infer; do not supply information unless its absence would confuse; keep the secret for last.
- Exercise: rewrite a patch of hard exposition three times — throw it out like a weapon, place it in a seductive scene, and hide the information so the behavior becomes credible.

## III. The Six Tasks and Four Kinds of Flaws (McKee)

**The six tasks**: every spoken utterance performs an inner action; every action/reaction beat intensifies the scene up to the turning point; statement and implication convey information and opinion; a distinctive spoken style individualizes the character; progressive beats capture attention; and the language strikes the reader while remaining true to the characters in the scene.

### Flaws of Credibility
- **Conversation wastes energy; dialogue concentrates on meaning**; credibility ≠ realism (the dialogue in 《爱丽丝》 (Alice in Wonderland) is impossible in reality but true to the characters).
- Empty words (telling the other person what he already knows); excessive sentimentality (language far exceeding the emotion); excessive clarity (the author's knowledge placed in the character's self-awareness); excessive perception (a character describing himself more profoundly than Freud plus Socrates); **mistaking an excuse for a motive** — motive is unconscious need, dialogue is the excuse (the Crusader "saving souls for God"; Richard III "I killed him because you are so breathtakingly beautiful").
- **The problem with melodrama is not excessive expression but insufficient motive**; once motive and action match, ask whether to be direct or indirect — looking at one's nails and saying softly "crucify him" is stronger than roaring.

### Flaws of Language
- **Cliché**: write the first word that comes, then improvise more, go a little crazy → choose carefully and throw away the rest; "no one needs to see your second choice."
- **Character-neutral language**: "Oh my God" → Alabama: "Sweet, bleeding Jesus"; Detroit: "Lord have mercy"; New York: "Well, damn me to hell."
- **Overwrought language**: it shouts to the author, "How clever my dialogue is," and the character snaps shut; "when in doubt, lower the register."
- **The eight ways to cure dry language**: write the concrete rather than the abstract; the familiar rather than the unfamiliar; short words rather than long ones ("His rendering was a falsification of the facts" → "He lied"; four phenomena: the more emotional, the shorter; the more active, the shorter; the more intelligent, the more complex; the more learned, the longer); the direct rather than the roundabout; the active rather than the passive ("She is playing around" → "She plays around"); short sentences rather than long ones; expressive language rather than imitation ("Don't imitate life; express it"); and cut superfluous words ("hey," "oh," "well," "also").
- **If dialogue does not imply thoughts and feelings beneath the surface, either enrich it or cut it.**
- **The diction layer of the 四十禁 (sìshí jìn — the forty prohibitions)** (王骥德 (Wang Jide), 《曲律》 (Rules of Prosody); 吴梅 (Wu Mei), 《曲学通论》 (General Treatise on Qu Studies), p0097–0098): items 17–34 have nothing to do with prosody and form a ready-made checklist for dialogue — stale, coined, vulgar, halting, coarse, disordered, derivative (idioms are acceptable), too literary (unsuited to the role), too obscure, classical/historical, pedantic (头巾气, tóujīn qì — bookish pedantry), and scholar's language; there is also 语病 (yǔbìng — speech fault): wording that, read aloud, sounds like something else; change the word, not the tune. **Usage**: Wu Mei's general note — "**为法至苛也。如不能尽守，须检点去其甚者**" ("The rules are extremely strict; if you cannot observe them all, inspect and remove the worst offenders") — a physical examination is not an admission threshold; do not use it to veto a line that stands on stage.

### Flaws of Content
- **On-the-nose**: writing the subtext as text, "having characters speak in a way no human being ever would"; a candlelit dinner of "I love you, I love you," sincerely meant — "dead as a dog in the road, absolutely unactable." Always having subtext is the guiding principle of realism; non-realistic genres (myth, fairy tale, science fiction, animation, fable) may reduce subtext.
- **The interior-monologue fallacy**: true interior monologue (long, uninterrupted, addressed to no one) degenerates into the author's mouthpiece; turn it into action/reaction (insert the other person's or the speaker's own nonverbal reaction). A talkative character may make the other characters uncomfortable, but must not bore the audience (《八月：奥色治郡》 (August: Osage County): the relatives' weary expressions; 《强者》 (The Stronger): the silent Miss Y becomes the star role).
- **The two-hander** (duologue) is "stiff as a brick" (《角斗士》 (Gladiator): Lucilla and Maximus in the cell) → **rewrite it as a three-way dialogue** (trialogue): the two use a third thing as the outlet for their struggle — a canary (《腿》 (A Leg)), Heisenberg, the East–West divide, Godot, wine (《杯酒人生》 (Sideways)), 《秃鹰 72 小时》 (Three Days of the Condor, discussed in 《战略高手》 (Out of Sight)).

### Flaws of Design
- **Repetition**: accidental echo (a recording played aloud); **repeated beats** (the same strategy in different words: begging/refusal three times) — "the greatest sin is boredom," and returns diminish (the first ice cream tastes good; the third makes you sick; of three crying scenes, the third turns into comedy).
- **Defective dialogue**: blurred meaning ("use a big nail well" vs. "hammer a long nail"); meaning that arrives at the wrong moment; **cues that arrive at the wrong moment** — the last word of each speech should ideally be the key word that triggers the other person's reaction (in the original version of 《神迹奇案》 (Agnes of God) every key word falls at the end of the sentence); use a highlighter to mark the last sentence of each speech.
- **Poorly designed scenes**: the turning point comes too early, too late, or not at all; **the split scene** (four reasons subtext and text do not match: the inner life is all there and the dialogue is flat / the inner life is too weak and the dialogue overdoes it / the two are unrelated / there is no conflict); vulgarity cannot raise the temperature ("you know things are bad when the criminal stops swearing"); **the rewrite trap** — the more you rewrite, the more literal the dialogue becomes, until the subtext disappears; "the reason a scene fails is often not the words; the solution lies deep in the events and the character design."

## IV. Techniques: Rhetoric, Syntactic Design, Economy, Pauses, Silence

- **Rhetoric**: the more sensory the figure, the more memorable ("walks like a model" vs. "moves like a slow, sexy song"); Blanche: "Sometimes God is there, and He comes so fast."
- **Line design** (placement of the key word): **the suspensive sentence** (periodic sentence, holding the core idea until the last word: "Then why did you give me that... look? Gun? Kiss?"; a joke also lands on its tail); **the cumulative sentence** (key word first, then support, giving a conversational naturalness); **the balanced sentence** (core in the middle); **parallel design**; any design used too often becomes wallpaper → **mix it** (《真探》 (True Detective): Rust Cohle has twenty thoughts, a dozen in suspensive sentences, the rest mixed).
- **Economy**: "make every word count"; Walter's Principle 26: **screenwriters are not paid by the word** (《逃出亚卡拉》 (Escape from Alcatraz): "What was your childhood like?" — "Short"); ask of every line: does it advance the plot? Does it tell us something new? Is there a more concise and efficient way?
- **Pauses**: tighten before the turning point, absorb after it; "a pause should be hard-earned"; Walter: no underlining for emphasis; an ellipsis is acceptable once or twice but the third is too many (use it only when one person speaks the first half of a line and another completes it); no exclamation points; parentheticals almost never ("(angrily, face livid)" → the actor says "like teasing a puppy with a biscuit" radiantly, proudly, innocently, adorably, and the pathology is revealed instead; the exception is a crowded scene where you must indicate whom the line is addressed to).
- **Silence**: when a scene is too talky, write for the eye — paralanguage (a nod, a look, a wave) and physical action; Bergman's 《沉默》 (The Silence): the waitress drops a napkin, bends over and sniffs from head to foot, and she draws a deep breath — "the turning point is in that deep breath; silence is the most economical thing in language." Hicks: "the most vivid character may be the one who does not speak" (Tom Harkin); Hoxter exercise: two pages, two people, only one of them speaks.
- **Argument** (Walter's Principle 27): in life consensus is an important part; in art it is not — let every line of dialogue challenge the next, never let any two people agree with each other, "look at the script as an argument from beginning to end" (《伟人爱迪生》 (Edison, the Man): polite, courteous disagreement remains tense).
- **Rhythm**: a daughter not yet two replays adult conversation in her crib — "da-di-da? da-da! di-da? da!" — with not one real word, yet she captures the rises and falls, the questions, the pauses exactly; "language is music with words." The one who controls the rhythm wins (Tony's clipped phrases vs. Melfi's slow, even cadence).

### Walter's List of Taboos
"I think," "this this," "you know," and "listen" are unforgivable; no dialect spelled phonetically (John Wayne refused to read "drivin'"); do not pursue the "realistic" hem-and-haw ("What's so great about natural? Arsenic, botulism, influenza, boredom — they're all natural"); **small talk is permissible only when it rises above itself and connects to the theme** (《12 怒汉》 (12 Angry Men), on the steps: "I still don't know your name"); long speeches are the exception and must give information in every sentence (Patton's opening, Joad's ending, Bernstein and the girl with the umbrella); "Hi, Debbie" / "Hi, Tom" → "That dress is sexy!" — "You like it?" — "I love it!"

## V. Character-Specific Dialogue

- "The more complex the psychology, the more distinctive the dialogue"; the foundation of dialogue is **vocabulary** — "nothing conveys or betrays a character's personality more than the words he uses and the names he gives to the things in his life." Work from inside the character outward: content (what he sees and feels but does not or cannot say) → form (wording) → effect.
- **Grammar and character**: **nouns and verbs express the range of knowledge** ("big nail" vs. "shank, patch, long nail, plug, plate — the whole taxonomy of nails"; "walk slowly across the room" vs. "stroll, waltz, glide, drag oneself, stride"); **modifiers, voice, and modality express personality** ("really big" vs. "magnificent"; active "that family planned the wedding" vs. passive "the wedding was planned by that family"; modality "could plan" vs. "had to be planned by the family").
- **Culture and character**: give a character a cultural symbol to serve as the third thing (the Colossus of Rhodes, 《秃鹰 72 小时》 (Three Days of the Condor), a yacht acquisition, Pinot grapes).
- **The principle of creative constraint**: writing the first clichéd sentence that comes to mind is far easier than descending into the imagination to express an image; "even if the character is inherently dull, the lines must express a lifeless soul — even dullness must be written vividly."
- Enter the character's subjective view of the world: Cassius says "this narrow world" (to a city-state politician the world is small; Caesar alone can take it); the aristocratic schoolboy "thinks like a sage and speaks like a commoner" — short sentences and strings of monosyllables make him seem casual and sincere.
- Case comparisons: 《真探》 (True Detective): Rust's abstract, polysyllabic Latinate endings vs. Marty's concrete images and strong rhythmic cadence; 《我为喜剧狂》 (30 Rock): Jack's nouns (flawless, Audrey Hepburn, moose tongue) and modal verbs (no longer, you can't, you must) "dress him like a character's clothes, from the inside out"; 《杯酒人生》 (Sideways): Miles's sensitive modal adjectives (thin-skinned, hard to grow, delicate, heartbreaking) vs. Maya's bold nouns and verbs (life, sun, shining to the peak, tastes so good).
- **Snyder's cover-the-names test**: cover the names — can you still tell who is speaking? If everyone sounds like the writer — "Hi, how are you, I'm fine"; 《大丑宝宝》 (Big, Ugly Baby!), each person has a distinct language trait.
- Egri: dialogue is born of three-dimensional characters and conflict, reveals character and background, and foreshadows events; be concise; **sacrifice the brilliant line for the character**; fit the character's profession (a mechanic thinks in mechanical terms); no speechifying, no showing off; a joke must have another function; arrange lists in escalating order, with the most important last ("The mayor is there, the governor is there, the president is there!"); 《骑马下海的人》 (Riders to the Sea): the Aran Islands accent has its own rhythm for each speaker.
- Hoxter: speech patterns expose origin, class, education, occupation, gender, and race; a group ≠ everyone the same (who has the thickest accent? who loves catchphrases most?); Shane Black: "a little larger than life."
- Lu Jun: hear the voice and know the person — "each generation is worse than the last," "if you can't find one, just grab one," "how many? not many," "I suspect every boat on the river"; four people compose linked verses on a snowy day (the merchant: "Snowflakes fall thick and fast to earth"; the scholar: "All are the emperor's auspicious air"; the landlord: "Why not let it fall three more years?"; the woodcutter: "Bullshit!"); 《火车上的威风》 (The Prestige on the Train) sketches Mr. Ma and the tea attendant Xiao Zhao in sixteen lines and three or four hundred words ("Then you can ask the ticket inspector later, I have no objection" — the single word "then" reveals his self-abasement).
- **The "set-piece grand talk" diagnosis** (魏明伦 (Wei Minglun), 《四姑娘》 (The Fourth Girl), "Three Knocks at the Door" rewrite notes, p0112): the Fourth Girl knocks a third time; how does the person inside answer? The author tried several replies through the door and none worked — complaining of his own hardship was no good, speaking against his heart was unbearable, and grand principles were even worse; the root cause was that "**the trouble lies in the set-piece 'revolutionary grand talk,' which violates the character's nature in that specific situation**"; finally he changed it to two lines of interior monologue: "Words cannot tell my hardship; with tears I blow out the lamp in reply" (言语难把苦衷诉，含泪吹灯作答复). **Self-check**: give this speech to another character — does it still work? If it does, it is grand talk, not human speech (the reverse side of the cover-the-names test).

## VI. 陆军 (Lu Jun) on "语言要美" (yǔyán yào měi — language must be beautiful)

- Script language = stage directions + spoken lines; spoken drama dialogue stays close to everyday speech, Chinese opera gives equal weight to song and 宾白 (bīnbái — the spoken passages of opera), and the common law is that language must be beautiful.
- **Elegant beauty (典雅美, diǎnyǎ měi — poeticized beauty) = poeticization** (full feeling, imagery, concision; Lawson: "a playwright who is not a poet is only half a playwright"; Tagore's 《牺牲》 (Sacrifice), Raghupati: "The world is one continuous mutual slaughter"; 《西厢记》 (Romance of the Western Chamber): "blue clouds in the sky, yellow flowers on the ground" (碧云天黄花地); 《牡丹亭》 (The Peony Pavilion): "without warning the mirror steals half her face" (没揣菱花偷人半面) — a person looking at a mirror described as the mirror looking at the person).
- **Plain beauty (通俗美, tōngsú měi; also 本色美, běnsè měi — natural-color beauty) is perhaps more important than elegant beauty**: an audience in the theater cannot be allowed even a moment's lull; 李渔 (Li Yu): "words should be rooted in street talk and alley gossip; events should be taken from direct statement and plain speech"; plainness and popularity are not the same as bald vulgarity — shallow language with deep meaning: in 《木匠退亲》 (The Carpenter Breaks Off the Engagement), 桃叶妈 (Tao Ye's mother) says, "her hair like a chicken coop, her clothes dirty as a mud donkey, her face like the Kitchen God"; in 《杨八姐游春》 (Yang Eighth Sister's Spring Outing), "I want one ounce of moon, two ounces of stars, three ounces of clear wind, four ounces of cloud... and leave out Lü Dongbin" (我要一两月二两星三两清风四两云……其中不要吕洞宾). **The natural-color beauty of opera lyrics should surpass elegant beauty, and so should spoken drama**; the key is whether it fits the character.
- **The three requirements of dialogue**: ①**动作性 (dòngzuòxìng — action)** (《雷雨》 (Thunderstorm), the forced-medicine scene: "I tell you to kneel!" — you cannot wedge a single word in; the two words "Dad! I—" spoken by 周萍 (Zhou Ping) contain more meanings than can be counted); ②**性格化 (xìnggéhuà — characterization)** (Horace: "gods speak one way, heroes another, nurses another, peddlers another — they are all different"; the worst is a line that any character could say; 老舍 (Lao She): "hear the voice and know the person"); ③**潜台词 (qiántáicí — subtext)** (刘知几 (Liu Zhiji): "words are near but the meaning is far; the diction is shallow but the sense is deep... see one thing in the sentence and infer three more beyond the words"; 《求婚》 (The Proposal): "Bring him back!"; 《祖母》 (The Grandmother): "Kurt, why do you ask me whether that story happened that way?" — three repetitions of the question).
- 老舍 (Lao She): "What is said matters, but how it is said matters more... a single sigh is better than a flood of words; a halting phrase or a long brooding silence may be stronger than pouring everything out at once."

**Opera lyrics** (the three rules and examples for rhyme selection, sentence pattern, and layout) are in [`sw-chinese-opera-banqiang`](../sw-chinese-opera-banqiang/SKILL.md). What remains in this section applies equally to 宾白.

## VII. Comedy Dialogue

- Comic characters are driven by **blind obsession**; rigid thinking is what makes them funny (nearsighted pursuit of the immediate desire); "for a comedy writer, empathy equals death," and the audience must remain cool, with a safe distance; the comic protagonist lacks unconscious self-contradiction, and surface vs. reality takes its place; minor characters have only one trait, and "many comedies fail because a supporting character opens his mouth and out comes a cliché."
- **Four techniques**: ①clarity (everything from the subtext up is clear; the fewest, clearest words); ②exaggeration (making a mountain out of a molehill — "you stole my mommy" — or a molehill out of a mountain); ③timing (setup–payoff; the punch lands after the last word and then no more words; "never step on your own joke"); ④incongruity (civilized adults fighting like children). Vulgarize the noble; once the subtext has risen to the text, it may be on-the-nose; the spiral descends from personality → appearance → emotion → violence.
- The three systems of comic energy: defensive emotion, aggressive emotion, and sex; a joke = setup/payoff.
- **The screwball-comedy pattern**: the narrative center of gravity always falls on scenes where the protagonists trade insults and match wits; the female lead must be strong and never lose ground; the dialogue hides a sexually teasing subtext; the pace is fast and editing maximizes it; lines do double duty as setup (《淑女伊芙》 (The Lady Eve): "Washington and Valley Forge," "Napoleon and Josephine," "I need him like the ax needs the turkey"); it invented "sex comedy without sex" and the true sound comedy.
- 尹迪克 (Yin Dike): place the joke before or after the point of highest tension; breaking taboos gets a cheap laugh, and the best humor is driven by the character's psychological background. Field: comedy = creating a situation and having the character respond to it with utter seriousness; Woody Allen: "acting funny is the worst thing you can do."

## VIII. Dialogue in the Scene: Beat Analysis (McKee)

1. Before writing any scene, answer, for each character, their **desire / opposition / scene intention / strategy** (the conscious layer) plus the **super-intention** (the unconscious layer, often the opposite of the characterization).
2. List the 2–3 pairs of values on the table and mark their charge at the start; write each beat as an "action/reaction" gerund + subtext + value change, and confirm that no beat repeats, every beat escalates, and the turning point comes neither too early nor too late — but does come.
3. Contrast the two characters by content, length, and rhythm.
4. Each of the six forms of conflict has its own way of writing: balanced (《黑道家族》 (The Sopranos): Tony vs. Melfi), comic (《欢乐一家亲》 (Frasier)), asymmetrical (《日光下的葡萄干》 (A Raisin in the Sun): one presses, the other avoids), indirect (《盖茨比》 (The Great Gatsby): using language to conceal resentment and manipulate), reflexive (《埃尔泽小姐》 (Fräulein Else): the speaking self vs. the silent self), minimal (《迷失东京》 (Lost in Translation): confession replaces argument, and pauses open the door to thought).
5. Before writing dialogue, build the story: inciting incident → core value → the five dimensions of desire (object / super-intention / motive / scene intention / background desire) → the four layers of opposition → the spine of action → each scene's turning point (action or revelation) → beats → **the five steps of behavior** (desire → awareness of opposition → choice of action → action → expression). "A scene does not exist in the act of speaking but in the action the character takes by speaking."
6. High-context versus low-context culture determines the ratio of text to subtext (the Mafia's "an offer he can't refuse" is understood inside the circle).

## IX. Diagnostic Checklist

1. What is the action beneath this line (a gerund)? What does the character want, and what strategy is he using? If you cannot answer, cut it or rewrite it.
2. Cover the names — can you tell who is speaking? Do the nouns and verbs show the range of his knowledge, and do the modifiers show his personality?
3. Does a character tell the other person something they both already know? Is it ammunition? If not, cut it.
4. Is it on-the-nose (subtext turned into text)? Can the candlelit "I love you" be acted? The only exceptions are "What do you want from me?", "Are you all right?", and non-realistic genres.
5. Is the two-hander stiff? Find a third thing and turn it into a trialogue.
6. How many times does the same beat repeat? Of three crying scenes, the third turns into comedy.
7. Is the last word of each speech the key word? Does the meaning arrive at the right moment?
8. Is there any "I think," "you know," "listen," "hi," "oh," dialect spelled phonetically, underlining, exclamation points, three or more ellipses, or parenthetical acting directions?
9. Does every sentence of a long speech give new information? Can it be broken into action/reaction beats?
10. Do two people agree with each other anywhere? (Agreement is boring.)
11. Can this scene work without dialogue? Could a deep breath or the removal of a ring replace it?
12. Cliché: is this your first choice? Write several wild alternatives, then choose.
13. Is the melodrama feeling caused by insufficient motive? Is the restrained version harsher?

## X. Training

- Eavesdrop on text and subtext (Singer listened through a crack in the floor to the kitchen maid), note new words, and coin new ones; read good scripts and **rewrite bad dialogue** ("the fastest and most effective way to train the skill").
- **Write inside the character**: Stanislavski's "if I were this character in this situation, what would I do?" (not "what would I do" and not "what would he do"); like Dickens, act it out in front of a mirror; take an improvisation class if necessary; write from the inside out, then look from the outside in, then look for the first time as an audience member.
- Hicks's five-step exercise: write two pages of dialogue between a man and a woman ending with "...all right?" → rewrite it **without using any pronouns** except in the first and last sentences → rewrite again **cutting every negative statement** → record ten minutes of real conversation, transcribe it verbatim into ten pages, then condense it to two → copy out two pages of a master's dialogue by hand to feel the rhythm.
- Hoxter exercise: procrastination (three strategies for not saying directly that time is needed, and three strategies for the other person); the power of silence; two people each want something from the other, three beats, at least one polarity reversal, someone wins — and you cannot use action, only dialogue.
- McKee's list of key questions (background desire / object of desire / super-intention / scene intention / motive / scene driver / opposition / value / subtext / beat / progression / strategy / turning point / deep character / scene advancement → text / exposition / characterization): ask them once from each character's point of view, three times — before writing, while writing, and after writing.
