# Korean & French Screenwriting Practice

> **Output language = the language of the question.** Always anchor terminology to the original terms; see [sw-workflow/terms.md](../sw-workflow/terms.md). Do not invent translations.

Source: three of the speakers in the first four lectures of 《故事创作大师班·国际卷》 (Story Creation Masterclass: International Volume): 吴胜郁 (Oh Seung-uk; Korea; 《八月照相馆》 (Christmas in August), 《绿鱼》 (Green Fish), 《无赖汉》 (The Shameless)), 崔石焕 (Choi Seok-hwan; Korea; 《王的男人》 (King and the Clown), 《港囧》 (Lost in Hong Kong)), and 弗雷德里克·奥比坦 (Frédéric Auburtin; France; 《巴黎，我爱你》 (Paris, je t'aime), 《特使》 (Envoyés très spéciaux / Special Correspondents), 《偷情桥》 (Un pont entre deux rives / A Bridge Between Two Shores)). For Japan's 荒井晴彦 (Haruhiko Arai) and ten Japanese directors, see `sw-japanese-screenwriting`; for the four Hollywood lectures, see `sw-american-case-studies` and `sw-industry-business`.

What the three share: **research first, write later; read the script aloud to harsh listeners; and with producers, "if you can persuade them, persuade them; if you can't, accept it and grit your teeth and do it."** Where they differ: Oh Seung-uk emphasizes emotion and space, Choi Seok-hwan emphasizes the genre contract and the double helix of desire/crisis, and Auburtin emphasizes writing dialogue last and participating through the entire post-production process.

## I. Oh Seung-uk: Screenwriting Is a Profession That Requires Endurance

### Depict the Emotion
- Christmas in August was criticized as "a beginner's work with no commercial appeal": he wrote the action in short-story prose (washing hands: a red basin, a crisp sound, raindrops leaving dark spots on a white shirt), growing out of a remark by 李沧东 (Lee Chang-dong) that "what 1990s Korean cinema lacked most was a certain emotion." His self-criticism: too many adjectives and adverbs blur the focus; too much objectivity leaves producers and audiences cold.
- An anti-traditional date scene ("Come and catch me!") → 许秦豪 (Hur Jin-ho) suggested the male lead tell a ghost story, which expressed warmth precisely.
- In a terminal-illness film, he does not make the audience cry; he reveals at the outset that the character has little time left, rather than earning tears after the lovers fall in love: "Since there are already so many tragedies in the world, don't exploit them for commercial profit. That isn't very moral."
- For Christmas in August he visited terminally ill patients: cooking ramen at midnight, riding a bicycle, fooling around with his sister over watermelon, crying under the covers — "being able to manage your own life does not mean you are always depressed" → washing a fountain pen, writing an instruction manual for his father's VCR.

### Research First, Write Later
- Before shooting, 朴光洙 (Park Kwang-su) went in person to a remote island to look at the color of the soil, the shape of the trees, and the conflicts among the residents, and only then wrote; Lee Chang-dong did his field research on foot (a dam release leaves residents displaced → a farmer who lost his land now sells chicken soup; "once you drive, you no longer look like a writer").
- **Undercover field research:** he sent a handsome young man into a homicide squad for a week to observe arrests and keep detailed records; he worked as a secretary for a gang, carrying bags and cleaning (what he saw was men arguing over their cut, "squabbling like teenagers"); he interviewed nightclub hostesses about what makeup they wore, their tone of voice, and the origin of the cigarette burns on their hands; interviewing male customers at a nightclub was worth more than going straight to the hostesses (a fresh angle); "if a subject balks at talking to a screenwriter, send someone else or introduce yourself as a gofer, an apprentice on the crew — interviewees with a sense of superiority will talk and talk"; "Korean screenwriters refuse any interview that reveals who they are; they disguise themselves and get close."
- **Imagination alone cannot build a character** (the female lead of The Shameless): a second round of interviews produced two observations (renting luxury clothes, collecting debts in person) → "a nightclub hostess going to a company to demand the fee for sex from the boss is a brilliant opposition," and then he imagined the male lead following along.
- **The liar who tells the truth:** Lee Chang-dong described his own experiences objectively in flawless third person; for things too shameful to confess, he stood in a third party's position and became a perfect liar. "To convey truth through a lie is the screenwriter's ultimate goal."

### Structure and Space
- **First act and last act first:** he cannot work until the first act and the last act are designed; 《双生警贼》 (Kilimanjaro) began with a snow scene in Gangwon Province — "a dying man lying down, another kneeling before him without a word" — and only then was the earlier part pieced together; the final scene of The Shameless is "from a distance an embrace of love; up close, a woman stabbing a man with a knife"; an investor demanded an opening in the rain with an old van and a body being dumped, and he struggled for three months without making it work.
- **Write 60 of 120 scenes and leave the rest blank:** he drove aimlessly around old Incheon neighborhoods with the production designer, improvising: "A woman comes out of that apartment — she's the one we're tailing."
- **Space as the skeleton of the whole film:** the female lead moves three times (a white-collar apartment where she waits for her lover → forced to move by pursuit → a suburban slum of total degradation; "the floors get higher and higher, the place more and more broken"); a slaughterhouse dormitory with wasp-nest partitions and twenty-odd pairs of shoes at the door.
- The three acts are divided by emotional rises and falls, not evenly (Christmas in August has about 30 scenes each in Acts I and II and 10 in Act III); he checks his watch constantly while watching films; he likes the story to continue after the audience thinks it is over (in The Shameless, the search for the female lead after his arrest is the third act).
- **Transition fatigue:** in a 90-minute film around minute 60, in a 120-minute film around minute 70, in a 120-scene script around scene 70, the audience starts checking its watch; "the most boring stage of a film is the transition point." A masterpiece puts the characters' emotional conflicts to work exactly where the audience first begins to feel bored; many directors instead force in inappropriate scenes or borrow someone else's set piece.
- **The climax point is the hardest to write:** the point where the second act's emotion reaches its explosive peak (the male lead decides to betray the female lead and use her completely to kill the criminal) still needs two or three transitional scenes before the climax.
- Transitions should hit hard: while writing one scene, the next appears in his mind (a strong wind behind, a full moon); The Shameless opens with a minute of a man's back → his face surfaces in a mirror.
- The undercover setup: every word the male lead says is a lie, while the female lead is someone who does not lie — the nightclub's female manager says, "The only counterattack we have is to tell the truth in every sentence."

### Find the Angle You Like
- The first principle is logical coherence; the second is being interesting (interesting is not objective; he insists on persuading the audience to accept his feelings); "only Oh Seung-uk can write an Oh Seung-uk script."
- He learned fresh entry points from 《水浒传》 (Water Margin) — the perspectives of 李逵 (Li Kui) and 武大郎 (Wu Dalang) — and from the 张彻 (Chang Cheh) film 《快活林》 (The Delightful Forest), with 武松 (Wu Song) as a killer steeped in blood; from 《大刺客》 (The Assassin), the character detail that 聂政 (Nie Zheng) "was an ordinary man who, once appreciated, had to repay the kindness"; inspired by a minor character in Water Margin, he built an entire film around just two people — the detective and the criminal's lover.
- Breaking the tough-guy formula: the male lead is androgynous, even feminine, and listless at the start; investors confronted him about the many back-view close-ups — "Why so few faces?" — "With great acting, even the way he walks is the character."
- He pays more attention to the antagonist than the protagonist, adding the cruelest things he himself has done and the bad things others have done to him; when drawing on friends and family, he disguises them, or else he would "have no friends left."
- "The day I admit my films are art films, I should retire" — he devotes himself to giving the audience a perfect product.

### Working with Producers and Directors
- **Read the script to harsh listeners:** gather five or six people who know screenwriting and still resent your past criticism, and have them listen to the reading from morning to night; when a bare back in a bathhouse was meant to express exhaustion and someone snapped, "Have you lost your mind?" — he went back and planted the foreshadowing; for the romance line, he had those present take roles and rehearse it.
- **"Take my name off it"** is the last weapon; multiple investors each made demands and the lead actor wavered repeatedly; he persuaded the director to restore the first draft before going to actors (a rapport sealed by spitting watermelon seeds) → investors came in one after another.
- His favorite scenes were always cut: he placed a beloved scene at a crucial moment (a French priest letting a little girl stand on his feet to waltz, moved to the most urgent point when the city is surrounded) so that it "became indispensable"; the one or two scenes that were cut carried the work's central image, so he found a way to rescue and fold them into the whole.
- His darkest moment: the director was ecstatic at first, then after a few meetings with producers and investors turned 180 degrees, and the new script was "soulless"; when revising, "don't forget the original intention; persuade them to change as little as possible — the probability that a producer's suggestion is a good one is very low."
- After delivery, the gates of hell opened: a small company wanted to "turn all the protagonists into rich people and move them to Gangnam to shoot a glittering life of luxury" — "I've never experienced a rich man's life; I can't write a single word"; a hotel anecdote from the boss suddenly became the opening, with three people staring at one another; he accepted a compromise adding the criminal's scenes; for the ending he was asked to add martial arts — "the director shot it with such insincerity."
- Rewriting on set: he could not write the scene of the man and woman meeting for the first time and left it as on-set homework; instead he persuaded the actor to assure the director, "I'll play it well" — "I thank that day's air and breeze" — the script is only a skeleton.
- His biggest motivation is the deadline (with a three-week limit, he enjoys life for the first two weeks and writes in tears in the last few days); "writing a script is like staying up all night to write a love letter to someone you deeply love, then tearing it up in shame the next morning."
- Lee Chang-dong asked every day, over and over, "Is this scene useful, interesting, does it have a reason to exist?" Even after seven or eight passes he would reluctantly say, "Fine, leave it like that."
- The Shameless was rewritten for ten years, rejected by six production companies, and turned down by seven or eight top actors — "patience is the key word."

## II. Choi Seok-hwan: How Genre Films Tell Stories

### The Industry and the Three-Person Model
- Korean film history: translated Japanese novels → domestic novels → 1980s adult films (every four films made earned one import quota) → 1990s new genres → full liberalization in 2006, yet good films still appear every year; "only China, Korea, and India have domestic industries that can hold their own globally."
- **One big happy family:** the producer stands entirely on the audience's side; the director is a vampire who lives on content; the screenwriter is "like a prostitute" and must cater to the producer; the three eat, live, and brainstorm together — "the distinctive feature of Korean cinema, the secret of beating Hollywood."
- The best liar: "A lie must be told vividly and entertainingly"; a man who comes back empty-handed from a prehistoric cave performs a fight with a bear; a boy who tricks his mother out of money for study guides and gives it to a beggar, making her weep with emotion — "the lie has to be on a higher level." The three-act structure is "the screenwriter's highest truth."
- Building a house means choosing a location: the Wanda Plaza model (starting at $100 million) versus a department store; the auteur chooses the family-friendly model; "if you're lucky, get the script off your hands and sell it as fast as possible, then go write something better."

### Fidelity to Genre Is a Promise to the Audience
- Structure has no effect on the audience but matters to the screenwriter (the safety of a building); genre follows the rules like the floor plan of a department store; **in comedy the laughs cannot stop from beginning to end (even if the male lead must die at the end, there must still be a laugh); in a mystery, no matter how many laughs, don't forget the essence**; rich elements but one through-line; "genre is a contract made from the moment someone buys a ticket based on the poster; you cannot cheat, at most 5–10% minor innovation"; mixing genres requires "100 points in every genre, expressed through a unified tone." Truby's "twist the beats while hitting every beat" is kept alongside this; see Section 2 of `sw-genre-anatomy`.
- Details are like interior decoration — don't build a golden palace for a solid-gold toilet; if the whole is good, don't worry about flawed details; fix them gradually.

### Make the Audience Wait, Enjoy, and Savor
- It is normal for the first stretch to be long and dull, as long as the middle and end are interesting; the reverse is doomed to fail.
- **Grandmother's fairy tale** (the tiger and the rice cakes), broken down: a few strokes of introduction → an inciting incident → **qualitative change, not quantitative** (the rice cake is an offering → the mother becomes the second offering; "an army of ten million followed by an army of one million" is only quantitative change) → tension and speed → the resolution must come through a reversal (a rope from heaven) → **a second rope falls, a second reversal** — "one reversal is not enough; squeeze out every ounce of imagination to give the ending two reversals" → **bittersweet** (add beauty to sorrow, add sorrow to a happy ending); reversals must not be forced or merely functional; "a rope falling from heaven is fine in a fairy tale, but a film can't do that."

### Collision with the World
- The original story idea behind Lost in Hong Kong (a father with three months of cancer left helps his family realize their dreams) was questioned in China, so 徐峥 (Xu Zheng) made it more comic. "The more you push boundaries and touch desire, the stronger the hold on the audience" — what is not allowed in reality is allowed in film.
- Ask yourself: what makes me tense / happy / angry / reflective / ashamed, and what in the past is interrogating the present? There are only two kinds of good scripts: a new subject no one has ever written, and a retelling of an existing story.
- If you have no entanglements or conflicts in your own life, you cannot be a good screenwriter; male screenwriters write fathers and sons, female screenwriters write mothers and daughters; write the field you love rather than pandering; subjects that provoke anger have a high success rate; for historical drama, ask, "Why this story, and what does it say to today?"

### Desire and Crisis
- **The crisis in the story's environment deepens step by step while the protagonist's desire keeps intensifying**; the protagonist has a clear motive and, at the same time, a persistent source of fear; the audience does not want a perfect protagonist, nor does it want to see a copy of itself — it wants the exotic; the pursuit should be intense and fast-paced.
- The richest-man example: external deterioration plus deepening desire make him inhuman; one line from a supporting character — "Is this how your family wants you to make money?" — is enough for the audience to feel the love of family.
- **The protagonist must undergo qualitative change even if only one night passes** (a person who returns from five years of traveling the world).
- A supporting character is not born for the protagonist; in his own life he is the absolute protagonist, and through him you can convey the core value; define a short, petty man by his goal and his anger trigger (he explodes when mocked for being short versus he only turns and walks away when mocked for being short).
- Ask, "What price does the protagonist pay, what does he give up?" — having 10 billion and earning another 100 million is not dramatic; risking the loss of 10 billion to earn 100 million is; a man who keeps a Persian cat collapses when the cat disappears.
- **You absolutely must fix the ending in advance:** "you can go to Beijing or you can go to Tianjin"; resolution at the climax does not mean the end — only when the psychology of the climax differs from the ending do you get a second climax; avoid putting the theme in the protagonist's mouth; use an image (jumping off a train bound for glory and running to the lover).
- "If a story is well written, no one asks about the theme" — being asked "what do you want to say?" means the work is not fully realized; use expository voice-over sparingly; the worst is the protagonist recapping the plot over coffee the next day; if exposition is necessary, let a quarrel carry it; television can have flat conversation, film needs heightened conflict.
- Two elements make a crime film succeed: a strong criminal motive that does not exist in reality, performed to attract; and what could happen in reality, rendered well. "What is written with the hand comes second; what is written with the feet comes first." 徐克 (Tsui Hark): "If there is one good screenwriter in the world, I'll grab him even if he is in hell."

## III. Auburtin: The Writer-Director Working Model

### The French System
- The auteur-film tradition (卡尔内 (Marcel Carné) and 普雷维尔 (Jacques Prévert); after the New Wave the director became the author, with final cut in the director's hands); a levy on box-office receipts, administered by the National Center for Cinema (CNC), flows back to authors (even Hollywood films pay the levy to support domestic production); almost every film signs a distribution contract with a television network, and television's pursuit of mass content is "harmful to free creation"; the only films that escape are literary adaptations.
- 布拉什 (Gérard Brach), the go-to screenwriter for 波兰斯基 (Roman Polanski) and 阿诺 (Jean-Jacques Annaud), was a recluse who never left home, curled up in bed with the television on mute — "a screenwriter can stay indoors; imagination is what matters most"; "thoughts must stay at a rolling boil."
- A stage adaptation comes with a ready-made structure, but don't film a stage recording; cut characters and plot from the source without hesitation, then add new ones; add a sense of three-dimensional space; after each full emotional passage, let the audience rest.

### Write Dialogue Last
- Many French films go into production before the script is fully written, and they inevitably fail; "all the conception is completed in the head; writing is only the final step"; a young screenwriter who begins with "INTERIOR, time, location" and then goes straight into dialogue is making a major mistake.
- 拉佩诺 (Jean-Paul Rappeneau), the director of 《大鼻子情圣》 (Cyrano de Bergerac) and a script doctor whose office walls were covered in sticky notes, worked in this order: first draft → structure → dialogue last; for every scene he wrote, he asked why it was handled this way and what made it necessary.
- In comedy, rhythm is everything: the effect of 《热情似火》 (Some Like It Hot) by 怀尔德 (Billy Wilder) comes from the protagonist escaping difficulties through situation, not through language; a successful comedy makes the whole theater laugh at the first shot; "you still laugh after the three-hundredth viewing" is the standard.
- **Inflict disasters on your characters:** "pain and misfortune are the source of comedy"; "happy people have no story"; 《虎口脱险》 (La Grande Vadrouille) creates comic situations at the tragic moment of the Nazi Occupation. The script for 《难兄难弟》 (Les Fugitifs / The Fugitives) by 韦贝尔 (Francis Veber) ran 13 pages in tiny type, with even the punctuation carefully chosen; **read the script aloud** (to an assistant, a spouse, or a goldfish); each reading is one more filter, like rainwater seeping down to become groundwater.
- **Only 12 lines of dialogue; 13 won't do** (伊斯特伍德 (Clint Eastwood) to a young screenwriter): silence is more powerful than speech, a rest more powerful than a note; good dialogue carries subtext beneath its surface information, "opening another door"; work on character biographies before writing dialogue (in 《拉丁区》 (Quartier latin / The Latin Quarter), "Sorry I'm late" means "I didn't want to come, so I made you wait on purpose"; "You look fine" means "I won't say anything unpleasant to your face; I have good manners" — like a boxing exchange); **show what can be seen**; if an image can carry it, never use dialogue (television never stops talking).

### Point of View and Hidden Truths
- Every setup needs a hidden truth — either the audience knows what the characters do not (the man doing the beating is sleeping with the beaten man's wife), or the characters know more than the audience; be your own first reader, examining the work from the writer/director's god's-eye view, from the audience's view, and from the protagonist's view; "trust the audience, and you can create complex plots"; film assumes the audience is intelligent, television assumes it is stupid.
- Reality is funnier than imagination: 特吕弗 (François Truffaut) clipped the newspapers every day and put tabloid items into his scripts; journalist-screenwriters supply professional truth.
- **Give characters dignity:** do not treat the immoral character's fate as humiliation (卓别林 (Charlie Chaplin) kicks a policeman's backside, then straightens his tattered clothes); in A Bridge Between Two Shores, a famous screenwriter's Hollywood-style ending faithful to the source (the wife jumps off the bridge, the husband rescues her, fireworks fill the sky) was shot and then cut — "the style was completely wrong" — and changed to "You go this way, I'll go that way," a gentle, dignified departure. **The style of the ending must match the whole film.**

### The Writer Participates Throughout
- Four stages (writing / pre-production / shooting / post-production); "I have never seen a script that could be shot the moment it was received"; every script is too long, and "you can cut it in post" is a lie — in practice you can't cut a thing; reading aloud reveals where to trim; on set the screenwriter answers more than 100 questions a day ("Why is he wearing this tie?"); "We'll fix it in the edit" is wrong — the screenwriter must anticipate; the jazz metaphor: at least three musicians playing together.
- **Italian-style table read:** first make a chart listing every scene step; only when the story is second nature can you reorder it at will; the read lasts 2–3 days, with the actors reading everything aloud — titles, notes, times, scene headings — without entering character, while a sound recordist logs each scene; before shooting, listening to the recording lets the whole structure, even the score, form in your mind.
- Let producers and actors read the script as late as possible (first impressions are hard to change); don't let professionals you don't know read it early; friends and peers carry two risks (they don't want to hurt you, so they say it's good / they are honest, and you end up arguing) — find a co-writer or write with the director.
- "The hard criterion for whether a script deserves to be shot is the time spent on it; a short schedule produces no good work"; start as a director's assistant buying coffee, and learn every job on set; the screenwriter is like a composer writing a score, the director like the conductor.

### Commissioned Films and Collective Creation
- **《激情联盟》 (United Passions)**, commissioned for FIFA's centenary over 18 months amid scandal: the first draft was rejected as "too sycophantic"; FIFA held censorship rights over the entire film — **there is always a clever way to protect the idea**: 布拉特 (Sepp Blatter), taking advice from his predecessor, used Corleone-style visual symbols from 《教父》 (The Godfather) and a huge Swiss flag behind a boat, and refused to cut a long take — "the smallest bit of power I had"; an African delegate receives an envelope (the audience naturally assumes cash) → in the next scene he holds it to camera and says, "Thank you for the ticket to the final"; the Adidas president's case → in the next scene it is opened to reveal an empty box holding only a design sketch. "When you hit an obstacle, don't give up easily."
- **Special Correspondents:** two frontline reporters hide in the Paris home of a friend who is an imam, making noise so that listeners believe they are in Iraq; the second-act climax fakes a hostage video; after watching many real throat-cutting videos, he interrogates the ethical line — "comedy rejects mediocrity and needs extreme situations, but it must not recklessly present cruel truths."
- **Paris, je t'aime:** 20 arrondissements, 20 directors, two days each, no segment over five minutes; he was responsible for the transitions. Each director brought a new story that destroyed the structure (the total would have run four hours; story logic barely existed). At the test screening it was too long, the pieces did not connect, Paris was nowhere to be seen, and the theme had gone off course → with the editor he distilled an "emotional through-line" and built three acts (curiosity and humor / death, drifting, loneliness / sublimation and resolution); he rearranged it back and forth through **82 versions, ignoring logic and respecting only emotion** (the clown couple moved from the beginning and end to the middle, followed by the mother who lost her child, then the child with the backpack — the Kuleshov effect); a mosaic of character images: "all Parisians share one story"; "You must be confident and never stop experimenting"; "Everything has rules, but there are always exceptions."
- 德帕迪约 (Gérard Depardieu): "Find someone to share the anxiety"; "anxiety is necessary for an artist." 伍迪·艾伦 (Woody Allen) and 库布里克 (Stanley Kubrick) dared to reshoot and replace actors; normally the screenwriter must ask questions early to avoid reshoots.

## IV. Rules You Can Apply Directly

1. Research first, write later: visit the locations and look at the soil, the trees, the residents; when interviewing, disguise yourself or send someone in; "the script written with the feet comes first."
2. Depict the emotion (down to the color of the basin and the dark spots left by raindrops), but beware too many adjectives and adverbs blurring the focus; build transitions on the collision of images.
3. Design the first act and the last act first (or start from the final image); with 120 scenes, write 60 and leave the rest blank; use space (the apartment of the three moves) as the skeleton.
4. Divide the three acts by emotion, not evenly; keep checking your watch while watching films; get through the "transition point" at minute 60/70 with the characters' emotional conflicts.
5. After the first draft, read it to harsh rivals, or have people take roles and rehearse it; reading aloud is one filter after another; hold Italian-style table reads.
6. Put a beloved scene at a crucial moment so it won't be cut; when revising, don't forget the original intention; "the probability that a producer's idea is good is low"; if you can persuade them, persuade them; if you can't, accept it.
7. The screenwriter is a liar who tells the truth: write your own experience in flawless third person.
8. Genre is a promise to the audience (comedy keeps the laughs coming; mystery never forgets its essence), with one through-line and 5–10% innovation; genre mixing requires 100 points in every genre.
9. Deepen the crisis step by step and intensify the desire; give the protagonist a clear motive and a persistent source of fear; the price paid must exceed the gain; the protagonist must change qualitatively even in a single night; fix the climax and the resolution in advance; give the ending two reversals and make it bittersweet.
10. Let the theme emerge through presentation, not through the screenwriter's statement; carry exposition in quarrels; avoid coffee-table and car conversations.
11. Write dialogue last: first draft, then structure, then lines; for every scene ask "why is it handled this way?"; write character biographies before dialogue so that subtext exists; silence is more powerful than dialogue (12 lines of dialogue); if an image can carry it, never use dialogue.
12. Play with point of view: the audience knows and the characters do not, or the characters know and the audience does not; be your own first reader from three perspectives — god, audience, and protagonist.
13. Pain and misfortune are the source of comedy; inflict disasters on your characters; comedy depends on situation and rhythm, not language.
14. The screenwriter takes part in pre-production, shooting, and post-production, anticipating the 100 questions that will arise on set; let producers and actors read the script later; find a co-writer rather than asking friends to evaluate it.
15. Commissioned films / collective creation: every rule has its exception; under censorship, protect your intention with visual symbols and "reasonable explanations" in the surrounding scenes; the edit can be reordered entirely by emotion.
16. Give characters dignity; do not treat the immoral character's fate as humiliation; the style of the ending must match the whole film.
